Fostering disabled children: what it involves and could you do it?

Many fostering services are looking for carers for disabled children, both full time and for regular short breaks that give families a rest. Here is what the role involves, who it suits, how training, pay and benefits work, and how short breaks fostering differs across England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland.

The short answer

Yes, and you do not always need previous experience to start. Fostering services across the UK recruit carers for disabled children in two main ways: full-time fostering, short term or long term, for disabled children who cannot live at home; and short breaks fostering, where a disabled child who lives with their own family comes to stay with you regularly, often for a night or a weekend, so the family can rest.

Some services ask for relevant experience and some do not. Manchester City Council says you do not need special qualifications or previous experience for its short break scheme. Bristol City Council says experience of caring for a disabled child is an advantage for its full-time contract role but not essential. Gloucestershire County Council says its Family Link Plus scheme is best suited to carers with a background in care, education or health. Leeds City Council notes that its carers for disabled children often have experience from their personal or working lives.

Whichever route you take, you go through the same fostering assessment and approval as any other foster carer, and the service then matches you with children whose needs fit your skills, your home and your time.

Which children need these carers

Services describe a wide range of needs. Leeds City Council lists physical disabilities, moderate to severe learning disabilities, severe sensory impairments, multiple impairments, autism and complex health needs, and says it also accepts sibling groups where one child has a disability. Its short breaks service is for children and young people aged 0 to 17. Conwy's Foster Wales service mentions physical disabilities, learning disabilities, sensory impairments or a combination of these.

Gloucestershire says the children it places with Family Link Plus carers may have learning or physical disabilities or complex health needs and will need extra time and patience. The Highland Council says there is no typical child who goes for short breaks: some start as babies and some in their teens.

The need is real in every nation. In May 2026 the Department of Health in Northern Ireland said 109 children and young people, from birth to 17, were waiting to be matched with a short breaks foster carer, and launched an urgent regional appeal. West Lothian Council says it urgently needs foster carers to provide short breaks for disabled children and young people.

Short breaks fostering: how it works

Short breaks fostering (also called respite, family link or support care, depending on the service) means a disabled child who still lives at home comes to stay with you on a regular, planned basis. Conwy describes it as a regular arrangement that offers a helping hand to a local family, which can be during the day, overnight, at weekends or in the school holidays. West Lothian says breaks can last a few hours, a day or overnight, and Leeds says they can range from a few hours to a weekend or longer.

Because the same child usually comes back to the same carer, you get to know them and their family well. Many carers choose to support just one or two children. Bristol says its short break carers usually look after one child once a month, with most children staying overnight once every few weeks. The Highland Council says many of its short break carers support one or at most two families and commit to a weekend a month.

The commitment asked for varies by service, so ask before you apply:

  • Essex County Council says you can support children with one weekend a month.
  • The Highland Council asks for anything from an overnight stay a month to a weekend a month.
  • Conwy says you can offer up to six weekends a year, or as many as you wish.
  • Northern Ireland's Department of Health says carers are asked for at least one or two overnights a month.
  • Bristol's full-time short breaks contract carers provide at least 200 nights a year, for example four children for 50 nights each.

Full-time fostering of a disabled child

Some disabled children cannot live with their birth families and need a foster home full time, for a short period or for the rest of their childhood. This is the same as other short-term or long-term fostering, but the day-to-day care can include personal care, medication, therapies, specialist equipment, many appointments and close work with schools and health professionals.

Leeds recruits carers for short-term and long-term fostering of disabled children and gives them specialist training, support groups, and supervising social workers who are themselves experienced in working with disabled children. In Northern Ireland, the South Eastern Health and Social Care Trust set up a Children's Disability Fostering service in 2023 that offers short breaks to children with severe disabilities and also looks for homes for the small number of children who cannot live at home.

Full-time care of a child with complex needs is a big commitment. Highland Council notes that on its most intensive short breaks scheme, where each carer family is linked with four families, working full time is not possible. Talk honestly with the service about the hours your own work and family allow; our guide to fostering and working covers this in more detail.

Who can do it, and what helps

The basic eligibility rules are the same as for any foster carer. Conwy says you must be 21 or older and have a spare bedroom. Northern Ireland's Department of Health says anyone over 21 can apply, with no upper age limit as long as you are healthy and have the energy, and that you do not need to own your home but must have a spare bedroom for the child. Leeds asks applicants aged 21 to 25 to show relevant transferable skills or experience.

What tends to matter most is the person. Leeds looks for patience, resourcefulness, flexibility and a sense of humour. Manchester asks for time, energy and a real wish to help. Experience from work in schools, nursing, care or therapy, or from bringing up a disabled child of your own, is valued, but services that do not require it will train you.

Your home matters too. The assessment looks at whether your home suits the children you might care for, and the service will match children to homes that can meet their needs. Do not rule yourself out before asking.

Training, support and equipment

Expect more specialist training than in standard fostering. Leeds and Manchester both mention specialist training, and Manchester adds that carers can earn enhanced payments as they gain new skills. What it covers depends on the needs of the children you look after, so ask each service what its training includes.

You will have a supervising social worker, as every foster carer does, and services that specialise often add support groups with other carers of disabled children. Short breaks carers also work closely with the child's parents, who know the child best and can show you their routines.

Equipment is usually provided or paid for by the service. Manchester mentions beds, bedding and safety equipment, Essex lists medical equipment among the expenses it covers, and South Lanarkshire Council says its short breaks carers can claim for the equipment they need and travel costs. Ask each service what it provides and who pays for any adaptations to your home.

Pay and allowances

Carers for disabled children are often paid more than the standard rate, because of the extra skills and time involved, but the amounts and structure differ from service to service. As one published example, Essex County Council lists professional fees of £325 to £660 a week per child for fostering children with disabilities, depending on the child's needs, and for respite short breaks £14.63 an hour (between 7am and 7pm) and £115 for an overnight, pro rata. Bristol pays its full-time short breaks contract carers a fee on top of the age-based allowance and pays a fee for periods with no placements.

Many councils and agencies do not publish their fees for this work, so ask each service for its current rates in writing, including what is paid for short stays, retainers, equipment and mileage. Our foster carer pay guide and council allowance pages explain how fees and allowances work in general. Foster carers are usually self-employed, and Essex notes that most of its fees are tax-free under qualifying care relief; HMRC or an accountant can confirm how the tax rules apply to you.

Disability benefits for the child

Many disabled children receive Disability Living Allowance (DLA). GOV.UK says a child must be under 16 and need extra looking after or have walking difficulties that have lasted at least three months and are expected to last at least six months; from 16, young people apply for Personal Independence Payment instead. Northern Ireland has its own DLA application route.

When a child is fostered, council policies set out how DLA is handled. The policies we read from Suffolk, Hertfordshire and Derbyshire say the foster carer is usually the appointee who claims and manages the DLA for the child, that it is paid into an account for the child, and that it is the child's money to be spent on their needs. Suffolk asks carers to tell their supervising social worker before making a claim. Hertfordshire's guidance notes that a foster carer looking after a child on the middle or higher care rate may be able to claim Carer's Allowance.

These rules are detailed and depend on your circumstances, so this is signposting, not benefits advice. Ask the fostering service for its DLA policy, and speak to the DWP or an independent benefits adviser before making any claim.

Across the UK

England
Councils and independent agencies recruit carers for disabled children. Short breaks can be arranged under section 17(6) of the Children Act 1989, in which case the child is not looked after, or under section 20(4), in which case a child staying more than 24 hours is looked after and the carer must be an approved local authority foster carer. Statutory guidance from 2010 explains that simplified care-planning rules (regulation 48 of the Care Planning, Placement and Case Review (England) Regulations 2010) applies where no single stay lasts more than 17 days and the total is no more than 75 days a year.
Scotland
Councils run short breaks and respite fostering for disabled children, alongside independent agencies. The Highland Council asks short break carers for between an overnight and a weekend a month, and West Lothian Council says it urgently needs short break carers for disabled children. Carers are assessed and approved in the same way as other foster carers.
Wales
Council fostering services in Wales work together as Foster Wales. Conwy's Foster Wales service describes short breaks fostering, sometimes called support care, for disabled children who live with their families, asks for carers aged 21 or over with a spare bedroom, and says no previous fostering experience is needed.
Northern Ireland
Fostering is run by the Health and Social Care Trusts, and the Department of Health says a £13.1 million investment in children's disability services has let every Trust expand or develop short breaks fostering. In May 2026 the Department of Health launched an urgent regional appeal for short breaks foster carers, saying 109 children aged up to 17 were waiting, that applicants must be over 21 with a spare bedroom, and that no qualifications or experience are needed. HSC NI Foster Care handles enquiries on 0800 0720 137.

Common questions

Do I need experience of disability to foster a disabled child?

Not always. Some councils, such as Manchester, say no special qualifications or experience are needed for short breaks, while others, such as Gloucestershire, say their schemes suit people with a background in care, education or health. Every service trains its carers.

Is short breaks fostering the same as respite care?

They overlap. Short breaks fostering usually means a disabled child who lives with their own family stays with you regularly, while respite can also mean giving full-time foster carers a break. Services use different names, including family link and support care.

Can I do short breaks fostering if I work?

Often, yes. Because short breaks are planned, many carers fit one or two weekends a month around their job, and North Yorkshire Council says its short breaks can suit people with part-time jobs. More intensive schemes and full-time care of a child with complex needs may not fit with full-time work.

Is a child on a short break in care?

It depends on how the break is arranged. In England, statutory guidance says a child on a short break arranged under section 17(6) of the Children Act 1989 is not looked after, but a child accommodated under section 20(4) for more than 24 hours is. The service will explain the arrangement for each child.

Will I be paid more for fostering a disabled child?

Often, but not always. Many services pay enhanced fees for children with complex needs; Essex, for example, publishes fees of £325 to £660 a week depending on the child's needs. Ask each service for its current rates in writing.

How do I find a service that needs carers for disabled children?

Contact your local council fostering team or independent agencies covering your area and ask whether they recruit carers for disabled children or short breaks. You can search for services near you at https://fosteringtoday.com/find-an-agency/.
