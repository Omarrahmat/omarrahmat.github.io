Long-term fostering or adoption: what is the difference?

Both give a child a home for the rest of their childhood, but they are very different in law, in money, in support and in contact with the birth family. Here is how long-term fostering and adoption compare, where special guardianship fits in, and how the rules differ across the UK.

The short answer

Adoption makes you the child's legal parent, permanently. Long-term fostering gives a child a stable home with you, often until they are grown up, but the child stays in the care of the local authority, and you look after them as a foster carer with a social worker, reviews and a fostering allowance.

Foster Wales, the national network of council fostering services in Wales, puts the legal difference simply: adopting makes you the child's legal parent, while in foster care the child remains the legal responsibility of the local authority and the birth family, and the foster carer is responsible for providing day-to-day or longer-term care. Gloucestershire County Council describes long-term fostering as also known as permanent fostering, for children who cannot return to their birth families, who stay until they reach adulthood or are ready to live independently.

Neither option is better in general. Social workers and courts decide which plan is right for each child, and some children need the legal permanence of adoption while others are better served by staying in a long-term foster family, often with continuing links to their birth relatives. For people deciding which route to explore, the differences below are the ones that matter most day to day.

Parental responsibility and who makes decisions

The biggest difference is parental responsibility, the legal rights and duties a parent has for a child. In England and Wales, section 46 of the Adoption and Children Act 2002 gives parental responsibility to the adopters when an adoption order is made and ends the birth parents' parental responsibility. GOV.UK says that once a child is adopted, the birth parents no longer have parental responsibility, and that an adoption court order takes it away from anyone else who held it. The adoption is permanent and the child has the same rights as a birth child, for example to inherit.

Long-term foster carers do not have parental responsibility. Staffordshire County Council explains that while a long-term foster carer plays the role of a parent in the child's life, the local authority and the birth parents have responsibility for the child, and birth parents could still be involved. Foster Wales says parental responsibility in foster care is shared between the birth parent and the local authority, which then delegates some responsibility to the foster carer. Many decisions are made together by the team around the child, and some, such as signing important medical forms, go to the local authority social worker and a senior manager.

In practice this means a long-term foster carer makes everyday decisions, such as bedtimes, clubs and friendships, but bigger decisions are made with or by the local authority. An adoptive parent, as Foster Wales puts it, can approve every decision as the child's parent.

Money: allowances, fees and adoption leave

Long-term foster carers continue to be paid. Gloucestershire County Council says long-term carers receive a fostering allowance to cover the child's needs, plus training and round-the-clock support, and Foster Wales says foster carers are financially supported to care for every child. What you receive depends on the service and on the child's age and needs.

Adopters are not paid in the same way. Gloucestershire says financial support for adopters varies and is typically not ongoing, and Foster Wales says an adoption allowance is available only in some cases. Adopters who are employees can, however, have a legal right to adoption leave. GOV.UK says Statutory Adoption Leave is 52 weeks, made up of 26 weeks of Ordinary Adoption Leave and 26 weeks of Additional Adoption Leave, and that only one person in a couple can take it, while the other may be able to take paternity leave. Statutory Adoption Pay has its own conditions: GOV.UK says you must have been employed continuously for at least 26 weeks by the week you were matched with the child and earn on average at least £129 a week before tax.

GOV.UK also lists situations where adoption pay and leave do not apply, including becoming a special guardian or kinship carer. If money is part of your decision, ask the fostering service or adoption agency exactly what support would apply to you, and check your own entitlements with your employer or an independent adviser. This guide cannot tell you what you personally would receive.

Social workers, reviews and support

A long-term foster child is still a looked-after child, so the professional support around them continues. Foster Wales says foster carers get regular visits from their own supervising social worker and from the child's social worker, along with ongoing training. You remain an approved foster carer, with the responsibilities and the support that go with that.

Once an adoption order is made, that changes. Foster Wales says social workers are no longer involved once an adoption order has been granted, although adoption support remains available through adoption agencies. For some families the end of statutory involvement is a relief and a mark of being an ordinary family; for others the continuing support of fostering is a real advantage, particularly for children with complex needs.

In England, a long-term foster placement has a legal definition. The Care Planning and Fostering (Miscellaneous Amendments) (England) Regulations 2015, in force from 1 April 2015, define a long-term foster placement as one where the child's plan for permanence is foster care, the foster carer has agreed to act as the child's foster parent until the child stops being looked after, and the local authority has confirmed the nature of the arrangement to the carer, the birth parents and the child. That confirmation gives everyone, including the child, a clear statement that this is meant to be their home for the long term.

Contact with the birth family

Contact usually looks different in the two arrangements, but it depends on each child. Foster Wales says contact in foster care can be daily or weekly and is usually face to face, and in long-term fostering it may reduce to monthly or annually. Gloucestershire says birth family contact in long-term fostering may or may not continue, depending on what is in the child's best interests.

Modern adoption is more open than it once was. Foster Wales says most adopted children have indirect contact through a letterbox arrangement run by the adoption agency, which may build to direct contact over time. GOV.UK tells birth parents that, depending on the child's situation, they may stay in contact, usually by letters and photographs and sometimes meetings, arranged through the adoption agency. Before making an adoption order in England and Wales, the court must consider whether there should be arrangements for contact.

If you are drawn to long-term fostering, be ready to support a child's relationships with their parents, brothers and sisters and wider family, sometimes for many years. If you are drawn to adoption, expect to help a child understand their history and keep whatever links have been agreed.

What happens when the child turns 18

An adopted child is your child for life; adoption does not end at 18. Foster Wales describes adoption as making the child a legal and permanent member of your family, forever.

Foster care, legally, ends when a young person leaves care. Many long-term foster families stay close for life, and there are formal arrangements to help young people stay on. In England, the Department for Education's guidance on staying put sets out arrangements that let care leavers remain with their former foster carers after they turn 18, and local authorities must take it into account when writing their own staying put policies. The terms, including payments, are set by each local authority, so ask the service what its policy says.

Assessment, approval and how long each takes

The two assessments look similar from the outside. Foster Wales says both involve an assessment of around four to six months followed by approval at a panel, a fostering panel or an adoption panel. The difference comes after approval: a foster placement can start very quickly, while finding the right adoptive match usually takes at least another three to six months.

Once a child is placed for adoption, there is a court stage. GOV.UK says that for a post-placement adoption, the child must have lived with you for at least 10 weeks before you apply for the adoption order, and the application fee is £212. The court decides, and an adoption order cannot be made unless the court considers it is in the child's best interests.

Adoption is also not open to every child in long-term care. Many children in foster care have plans that do not include adoption, and Foster Wales notes that about 285 children are adopted in Wales each year, compared with 1,857 children needing foster care.

Can foster carers adopt, or become special guardians?

Sometimes. Foster Wales says a small number of foster carers adopt children already in their care, but describes this as not very common. It also describes early permanence schemes, called foster to adopt or fostering for adoption in England and the Welsh Early Permanence framework in Wales, where approved adopters foster a baby or young child while the court decides the plan. Adoption is never guaranteed in these schemes.

A more common route from long-term fostering to legal permanence is a special guardianship order. A special guardian holds parental responsibility that they can use without the agreement of others who hold it, although birth parents keep their parental responsibility. Foster Wales says birth family permission is still needed for some things, such as changing the child's surname or taking them abroad for three months or more, and that the child is no longer looked after once the order is made. Special guardianship orders last until the child is 18 unless a court ends them sooner.

Council pages set out who can apply. These are examples:

  • Shropshire Council: a local authority foster carer with whom the child has lived for one year immediately before the application can apply; special guardians must be at least 18 and birth parents cannot be special guardians.
  • Essex County Council: a foster carer with the council can apply if the child has lived with them for at least the last year, and anyone thinking about applying must contact the council at least 12 weeks before going to court.
  • Surrey Children's Services' procedures: a foster carer applying because the child has lived with them for a year must give the responsible local authority three months' notice, and former foster carers can continue to receive a payment element for up to two years.
  • Foster Wales: foster carers can apply once the child has lived with them for at least a year.

How to decide which is right for you

Think about what you want your role to be. If you want to become a child's parent in law, take on every decision and step away from social services, adoption may suit you. If you want to give a child a permanent home while working alongside a team, receiving an allowance and supporting the child's family links, long-term fostering may suit you better. Some carers start with fostering and later find that special guardianship or adoption is right for a particular child.

Talk to both a fostering service and an adoption agency before you decide. Ask what children in your area are waiting for, what support and payments would apply, and how they handle contact. For anything about your own legal position, take advice from a family law solicitor; for money, check with the service and your employer.

Across the UK

England
Adoption is governed by the Adoption and Children Act 2002: an adoption order gives parental responsibility to the adopters and ends the birth parents' parental responsibility. Since 1 April 2015 the Care Planning, Placement and Case Review (England) Regulations 2010 have defined a long-term foster placement. Local authority foster carers can usually apply for a special guardianship order once the child has lived with them for a year, after giving the council notice. Staying put guidance lets care leavers remain with former foster carers after 18.
Scotland
Scotland uses permanence orders under the Adoption and Children (Scotland) Act 2007. A court can make one on a local authority's application for a child in kinship, foster or residential care; it gives the local authority the right to regulate where the child lives and can give some parental responsibilities and rights to another person. A permanence order can also include authority for the child to be adopted, and a child aged 12 or over must consent unless the court finds them unable to. The Scottish Government lists adoption, permanence orders and kinship care orders among the routes to permanence.
Wales
Foster Wales, the national network of council fostering services, says both fostering and adoption assessments take around four to six months before panel, that about 285 children are adopted in Wales each year compared with 1,857 needing foster care, and that foster carers can apply for special guardianship once a child has lived with them for at least a year. Early permanence runs through the Welsh Early Permanence framework.
Northern Ireland
The Department of Health in Northern Ireland names the Adoption (Northern Ireland) Order 1987 as the principal adoption law, though its page is older, so check nidirect for the current position. Adopters are assessed and approved by a Health and Social Care Trust or a voluntary adoption agency, and must be at least 21 to adopt a child who is not related to them. nidirect says an adoption order severs all legal ties with the birth family and that the child takes the adoptive family's surname, with the same rights as a birth child.

Common questions

Is long-term fostering the same as adoption?

No. Adoption makes you the child's legal parent permanently and ends the birth parents' parental responsibility. In long-term fostering the child stays in the care of the local authority, the birth parents keep parental responsibility, and you care for the child as an approved foster carer with a social worker and an allowance.

Do long-term foster carers still get paid?

Yes. Long-term foster carers receive a fostering allowance for the child's needs. The amounts depend on the service and the child. Adopters do not usually receive ongoing payments, although some receive an adoption allowance in particular cases.

Can I adopt the child I am fostering?

It is possible but not common. It depends on the child's plan, which is decided by social workers and ultimately a court. Many foster carers who want legal permanence for a child they look after apply for a special guardianship order instead, usually once the child has lived with them for at least a year.

Can a long-term foster child take my surname?

Not without the agreement of those with parental responsibility, because the birth parents and local authority keep it. Even with a special guardianship order, Foster Wales says the birth family's permission is needed to change the child's surname. An adopted child takes the adoptive family's name.

What happens to a long-term foster placement when the child turns 18?

The young person leaves care in law, but many stay with their foster family. In England, staying put arrangements let care leavers remain with former foster carers after 18 under each local authority's policy. Ask your service how it works locally.

Which takes longer to get approved, fostering or adoption?

Foster Wales says both assessments take around four to six months before panel. A foster placement can start soon after approval, while adoption matching usually adds at least three to six months, and there is then a court stage.
