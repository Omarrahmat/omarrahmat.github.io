Can you foster if you are not a British citizen?

Yes, in many cases. No UK nation makes British citizenship a condition of fostering. What fostering services look at is whether you live in the UK long term, your right to be and work here, your English, and the checks they must complete, including on any time you have spent abroad.

The short answer

You do not have to be a British citizen to become a foster carer. The Fostering Network, the UK's leading fostering charity, says plainly that British citizenship is not required to foster in the UK, and council fostering teams say the same: the London Borough of Hounslow, for example, lists its basic requirements and adds that British citizenship is not one of them.

What fostering services do look for is stability. A child placed with you may stay for months or years, so services want to know that you are settled in the UK, that your immigration status allows you to stay, and that you can carry out the role, including working with social workers, schools and health services in English. Many people who were born abroad, or who hold another nationality, foster successfully, and services actively want carers from a wide range of backgrounds because the children they look after come from every background too.

This guide explains what the official pages and fostering services say about nationality, residence and immigration status, what extra checks apply if you have lived abroad, and how the picture differs between England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland. It is general information, not immigration advice. Your own position depends on your documents and circumstances, so confirm it with the fostering service you apply to and, if you are unsure about your status, with a regulated immigration adviser or Citizens Advice.

What the official rules actually say

In England, GOV.UK's page on who can foster sets out only a few basic conditions: you must be at least 18 (most fostering services ask for 21 or over), you usually need a spare bedroom, you must be able to take care of a child or young person, and you must have the right to work in the UK. It does not mention nationality or citizenship.

The law behind the assessment points the same way. The Fostering Services (England) Regulations 2011 require a fostering service to gather a set list of information about anyone it assesses, set out in Schedule 3. That list includes your health, your household, your accommodation, your employment, your experience of caring for children, two personal referees and an enhanced criminal record certificate. It also asks about your religious persuasion and your racial origin, cultural and linguistic background, but only in order to understand your capacity to care for children from particular backgrounds. Nationality is not on the list.

In Scotland, mygov.scot says you can foster if you are a full-time resident in the UK, are 18 or over (some agencies ask for 21) and have a spare bedroom, and that you can foster regardless of protected characteristics such as religion. Foster Wales's page on who can foster in Wales, and nidirect's page on becoming a foster or kinship foster carer in Northern Ireland, do not mention nationality at all; nidirect says you can apply whatever your race, religion or sexuality.

Residence and immigration status: what services ask for

Although the official pages are short, individual fostering services set out their own expectations, and this is where most of the detail sits. The Fostering Network says most fostering services expect you to be a full-time resident in the UK. It adds that if you are only in the UK for a limited time, a service will weigh up the time and cost of approving you, because assessment and training take months and children need carers who will be there for them.

Many councils go further and ask for a settled immigration status. Brent and Hounslow councils both list indefinite leave to remain in the UK as a requirement, and Surrey County Council says people who are not from the UK can foster as long as they have indefinite leave to remain or settlement. Lambeth Council asks for applicants to be a full-time resident in the UK or to have indefinite leave to remain.

The practical picture is therefore this:

  • British and Irish citizens, and people with settled status, indefinite leave to remain or another permanent right to live in the UK, meet the residence expectations that services publish.
  • If you are in the UK on a time-limited visa, some services may still consider you, but expect questions about how long you can stay and whether your conditions allow the role. Ask each service directly before you start.
  • Every service will want to see proof of identity and of your right to be in the UK as part of the checks.
  • Published requirements differ from one council or agency to the next, so check each service's own rules.

Language and culture

English does not need to be your first language. Surrey County Council says you do not need to be a native English speaker to foster, but you must be able to communicate well enough with the child and the professionals around them. The Fostering Network says you will need a good level of spoken and written English, while noting that a household where English is not the first language can be of real benefit to some children. Lambeth and Hounslow councils both list good spoken and written English among their basic criteria.

The reason is practical. Foster carers keep daily records, attend meetings and reviews, read care plans, talk to teachers and doctors, and complete training. If your written English is still developing, say so early: some services can offer support, and it may affect which kind of fostering suits you best.

Your own culture, faith and languages are an asset rather than a barrier. The Fostering Network says it does not matter what your religion is, Hounslow welcomes carers from any cultural or religious background, and Manchester City Council says you can be from any ethnic, cultural or religious background. Fostering services try to match children with carers who can meet their cultural, religious and language needs, which is exactly why Schedule 3 of the English regulations asks about your background.

Checks if you have lived abroad

Every applicant has background checks: criminal record checks, a medical, references and visits to your home. If you have spent time living outside the UK, a UK criminal record check will not show what happened while you were away, so services usually ask for extra checks covering that period.

Foster Wales Vale of Glamorgan, for example, lists overseas and military checks among the ten checks it carries out, and says that if you have lived overseas at any time in your life, or served in the armed forces, it will contact the relevant authorities. Other services set their own thresholds, so ask how they apply to you.

The Home Office publishes guidance on criminal records checks for overseas applicants, with country-by-country details. It explains that the process varies from country to country and that you will usually have to apply in that country or through its embassy in the UK. Many countries issue a police certificate or a certificate of good conduct, but it is not always possible to get one. If a check cannot be obtained, services usually look for other evidence, such as references from employers or people who knew you while you were abroad.

Things that help:

  • Tell the service at your first conversation where you have lived and for how long, so it can start overseas checks early. They can take time and can slow the assessment if left late.
  • Gather addresses and dates for each country, and contact details for employers or referees there.
  • Ask the service whether it pays for overseas certificates or whether you should.
  • Partners and other adults in your household may need the same checks if they have lived abroad.

Money, benefits and your immigration conditions

Foster carers receive an allowance for each child's costs and, with many services, a fee for their own time and skill. If you are on a visa with a condition of no recourse to public funds, you may reasonably want to know whether fostering affects that.

The Home Office's list of public funds, published by UK Visas and Immigration, includes benefits such as Universal Credit, Child Benefit, Housing Benefit and Council Tax Reduction. It does not mention fostering allowances or fees. Citizens Advice explains that if your documents say no public funds, you are not usually allowed to claim public funds, and that British and Irish citizens and people with settled status or indefinite leave are usually able to.

That still leaves questions only a specialist can answer for you, such as whether your visa conditions allow the work, how your fostering income is treated, and whether claiming any benefit while fostering could affect a future application. We cannot advise on your individual case. Before you rely on anything, speak to a regulated immigration adviser or Citizens Advice, and ask the fostering service how it has handled similar applications.

How to start if you are not a British citizen

The first step is the same as for anyone else: get in touch with a fostering service near you, either your council's fostering team or an independent fostering agency. Be open from the start about your nationality, your immigration status and any time spent abroad. Services deal with these questions regularly, and an honest early conversation saves you time if one service's rules do not fit your situation.

Useful questions to ask at that first call:

  • What immigration status or length of residence does your service need?
  • Which documents will you want to see?
  • Will you need overseas checks for me or my household, how long do they usually take, and who pays?
  • What level of English do you expect, and is there support with written records and training?
  • Do you have children waiting who share my language, culture or faith?

Across the UK

England
GOV.UK lists the right to work in the UK, being at least 18 (most services ask for 21), usually a spare bedroom and the ability to care for a child; it says nothing about nationality. The Fostering Services (England) Regulations 2011, Schedule 3, require services to record your religion and your racial, cultural and linguistic background, and an enhanced criminal record certificate, but not your nationality. Many councils, such as Brent, Hounslow and Surrey, ask for indefinite leave to remain or settlement.
Scotland
mygov.scot says you can foster if you are a full-time resident in the UK, are 18 or over (some agencies ask for 21) and have a spare bedroom, and that you can foster regardless of protected characteristics such as religion. It does not mention nationality. The assessment, which mygov.scot says can take 6 to 12 months, includes checks on your criminal record, health, finances and home.
Wales
Foster Wales's page on who can foster does not set any nationality or residence condition; it focuses on having a spare room and says relationship status, renting and age are not barriers. Local Foster Wales teams carry out the background checks, and Foster Wales Vale of Glamorgan says it contacts the relevant authorities if you have lived overseas at any time.
Northern Ireland
nidirect says you can apply to foster whatever your race, religion or sexuality, and does not mention nationality. Applicants have a GP medical, references and safeguarding checks including an AccessNI check and a social services check, followed by a social work assessment and a fostering panel.

Common questions

Do I need to be a British citizen to foster?

No. The Fostering Network says British citizenship is not required to foster in the UK, and official pages in England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland do not list nationality as a condition. Services do look at your residence and immigration status.

Do I need indefinite leave to remain to foster?

It depends on the service. Some councils, including Brent, Hounslow and Surrey, ask for indefinite leave to remain or settlement. GOV.UK lists the right to work in the UK, and The Fostering Network says most services expect you to live in the UK full time. Ask each service what it requires.

Can I foster if English is not my first language?

Yes, as long as you can communicate well with the child and the professionals involved and keep written records. Services such as Surrey say you do not need to be a native English speaker, and a second language can help a child who shares it.

I have lived abroad. Will that stop me fostering?

Not on its own. Services will usually ask for checks covering your time abroad, such as a police certificate or certificate of good conduct from that country, or references if no check is available. Tell the service early, as these checks can take time.

Will fostering affect my visa or a no recourse to public funds condition?

We cannot advise on individual immigration cases. The Home Office list of public funds does not mention fostering allowances, but your visa conditions and circumstances matter, so speak to a regulated immigration adviser or Citizens Advice before you apply.
