Can you foster if you don't drive?

Usually, yes. Most UK fostering services say a driving licence is helpful but not essential, as long as you can show how you would get a child to school, family time, appointments and meetings. A few services do ask for a driver with a car. Here is what services say, how non-drivers make it work and what drivers need to know about insurance and mileage.

The short answer

Not being able to drive does not, on its own, stop you becoming a foster carer. None of the national fostering sites for England, Wales, Scotland or Northern Ireland lists driving as an eligibility rule, and many councils say so plainly. Surrey County Council, for example, says you do not need to own a car or hold a driving licence to foster, and Lancashire County Council simply says you don't have to drive.

What every service does need is confidence that a child's travel will be covered. Foster children go to school every day, often have regular time with their birth family, and attend health appointments, reviews, meetings and activities. If you don't drive, your assessing social worker will want to understand how you would manage each of those journeys, reliably and safely.

A small number of services take a firmer line. Central Bedfordshire Council lists being able to drive and having access to a car among its requirements, although it will talk through options with a couple where only one partner drives. Angus Council in Scotland says applicants must be able to drive and have access to a car. So the honest answer is: usually yes, but check the policy of the service you want to apply to before you start.

What fostering services say about driving

Fostering services write their own guidance on this, and the wording varies more than the substance. Most fall into one of three groups.

The first group says driving is not needed. Fostering Stoke says not driving would not prevent you from becoming a foster carer. Knowsley says you don't need to drive and that many of its carers manage on public transport. Lincolnshire County Council says not driving isn't a barrier. Derby City Council, writing for the East Midlands councils in February 2026, lists "you must drive to foster" as a myth and says what matters is reliable transport for school, health appointments and family time. Action for Children, a national charity that runs fostering services, says a driving licence is not a requirement for approval with it.

The second group says driving is useful and may widen the children you are matched with. Bolton Council says it is helpful to drive because it makes meetings easier to reach, that not driving may limit the number of children it would consider placing with you, and that its carers who don't drive have good support networks or public transport links. Norfolk County Council calls a licence handy but not essential, and Foster East, which represents a group of councils in the East of England, says much the same, adding that it is handy in some areas.

The third group asks for a driver, as with Central Bedfordshire and Angus above. Because services differ, it is worth asking the question directly at your first enquiry call: it costs nothing and saves time on both sides.

The journeys a foster carer is expected to make

To judge whether you can manage without a car, it helps to know what the travel actually involves. Hampshire County Council puts it simply: you don't need to drive, but you will be expected to attend meetings and appointments and take children to and from school, so you need to think about how you would meet those travel needs. Surrey lists school runs, health appointments, family contact and activities.

The journeys usually include:

  • The daily school run, sometimes to a school the child already attends some distance away, so they can stay settled. Lancashire notes that children sometimes need to travel to other parts of the county for school, and Medway Council expects carers living outside Medway to take a child to and from a Medway school each day.
  • Family time (contact) with parents, brothers and sisters or other relatives.
  • Health appointments: GP, dentist, optician, and sometimes hospital, therapy or specialist clinics.
  • Meetings about the child, such as looked after child reviews and school meetings.
  • Your own training, support groups and supervision visits, some of which may be held away from your home.
  • Clubs, activities and ordinary family outings, which matter just as much to a child's sense of belonging.

How carers who don't drive make it work

Services that welcome non-drivers tend to look for the same few things, and you can prepare your answers before your assessment starts.

Good public transport is the most common answer. Derby says that in urban areas public transport may be enough, and Medway says it will accept applications from non-drivers who can explain how they would use public transport links to get a child to school. Think about the actual routes: how long the school journey would take with a young child, whether buses run early enough and late enough, and how you would get to the places where family time is usually held.

A support network is the second. Medway also accepts applicants backed by a partner, friend or family member who can drive, and Knowsley mentions carers who have help from family members. If someone close to you would help with lifts, say who they are and how often they could realistically do it; ask the service whether anyone who regularly helps with lifts would need checks.

Where you live matters too. Trafford Council points out that most of its carers' travel, including school runs and family contact, is likely to be within the borough. Living close to schools, a GP surgery and good transport links makes a strong case. In rural areas the answer can be different: Action for Children notes that not having a licence may prevent fostering in some circumstances, such as rural areas.

Your assessing social worker will talk with you about how you plan to manage travel, as Surrey describes, and the plan will help shape which children you are best matched with. For example, a non-driving carer near a town centre might be a good match for a child who goes to a local school, and less so for a child who needs a long daily journey to stay at their current school.

If you do drive: insurance, documents and mileage

If you will drive foster children, your service will usually ask about your car. Norfolk County Council says you must have fully comprehensive car insurance if you use your car to transport foster children. Argyll and Bute Council asks carers to tell their insurer that they foster, to hold fully comprehensive cover including business use, and to show their insurance documents, MOT and driving licence to their supervising social worker every year. Policies differ, so check what your service and your insurer need before a child moves in.

Who pays for travel also varies by service. Many treat everyday journeys as covered by the weekly fostering allowance, with extra mileage for longer or special trips. The Newham foster care handbook says an element of the allowance covers travel to school, family contact and meetings, and that mileage or fares can be claimed in exceptional circumstances such as trips outside London. Argyll and Bute includes a weekly travel element in its 2026/27 allowances (from £13.66 a week) and says travel above 50 miles may be claimed at 45p a mile.

Essex County Council's expenses guide for carers, applicable from 1 April 2025, pays 45p a mile and can reimburse the full cost of public transport a child uses for school, but does not pay the first three miles each way of a school journey. It says travel can be claimed for all contact arrangements, but not for routine health appointments such as the GP, dentist or optician. These are examples only: ask any service you are considering for its current expenses policy in writing.

What to ask a fostering service before you apply

If you don't drive, a few direct questions at the start will tell you whether a service is a good fit:

  • Do you approve carers who don't drive, and how many of your current carers don't?
  • Would not driving limit the ages or needs of the children you would match with me?
  • Where is family time usually held, and how do carers who don't drive get there?
  • Do you pay for public transport, taxis or other travel when a journey cannot reasonably be made by bus or train?
  • Would a friend or relative who helps with lifts need to be checked, and how?
  • Are training sessions and support groups reachable by public transport, or held online?

Learning to drive later

Some people start the process as non-drivers and learn during or after assessment. If you plan to do this, say so to your assessing social worker, because it may change the kind of fostering you are approved for. Until you have passed, your application will be judged on the travel arrangements you can show now.

Whatever your situation, remember that a service's decision on travel is about the needs of the particular children it is looking to place. Being turned down by one service on transport grounds does not mean another service, with different children, different local schools or a more compact area, would reach the same view. Your local council fostering team and independent agencies in your area can both tell you how they would see your application.

Across the UK

England
There is no national rule that foster carers must drive. Each council and independent agency sets its own approach: most, such as Surrey, Lancashire, Lincolnshire, Hampshire and Stoke, say it is not required as long as travel needs are met, while Central Bedfordshire lists driving and access to a car as a requirement.
Scotland
Policies are set locally. Angus Council asks applicants to be able to drive and have access to a car. Argyll and Bute Council asks carers who drive to show their licence, MOT and insurance every year, with fully comprehensive cover including business use.
Wales
Foster Wales, the national network of council fostering services in Wales, does not list driving among the things that affect who can foster. Ask your local Foster Wales team how it would see your travel arrangements.
Northern Ireland
The nidirect guide to becoming a foster or kinship foster carer does not list driving among its eligibility points. The health and social care trust or agency you apply to will discuss how you would manage a child's travel during the assessment.

Common questions

Do I need a driving licence to be a foster carer?

Usually not. Most UK fostering services say a licence is helpful but not essential, as long as you can show how you would get a child to school, family time, appointments and meetings. A few services, such as Central Bedfordshire and Angus councils, do ask for a driver with a car, so check with the service you want to join.

Can I foster if I don't have a car?

Often yes. Services such as Surrey, Knowsley and Trafford say not having a car does not stop you fostering. You will need reliable public transport, a support network who can help with lifts, or both, and the plan will be discussed during your assessment.

Will not driving limit which children I can foster?

It can. Bolton Council, for example, says not driving may limit the number of children it would consider placing with you. A child who needs a long daily journey to stay at their current school may be better matched with a carer who drives.

Do foster carers get paid mileage?

It depends on the service. Many treat everyday travel as part of the weekly allowance and pay mileage for longer or special journeys. Essex County Council, for example, pays 45p a mile from April 2025 but not for the first three miles each way of a school run. Ask your service for its current expenses policy.

Do I need special car insurance to foster?

If you will drive foster children, your service is likely to ask for fully comprehensive cover, and some, such as Argyll and Bute Council, ask for business use and for you to tell your insurer that you foster. Your service and your insurer can confirm exactly what you need.
