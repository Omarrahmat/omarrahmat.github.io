What training do foster carers get? Skills to Foster and beyond

You do not need qualifications to foster, but you will train before and after approval. Here is what the Skills to Foster preparation course covers, what England's TSD Standards ask of new carers, the training most services expect each year, and how the rules differ in Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland.

The short answer

You do not need a degree, a childcare qualification or any previous training to become a foster carer. What you do need is a willingness to learn, because training runs through every stage of fostering: a preparation course while you are being assessed, induction training once you are approved, and ongoing learning for as long as you foster.

Most fostering services across the UK use the same preparation course, The Skills to Foster, produced by the charity The Fostering Network, or their own version of it. After approval, the rules differ by nation. In England, new carers must complete the Training, Support and Development (TSD) Standards within 12 months of approval. Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland have no equivalent mandatory induction standard, although each has its own expectations and frameworks.

The training is arranged by your fostering service, whether that is a council or an independent agency, and many services now offer evening, online or blended options. If timing, work or childcare would make training difficult for you, raise it early with the service so you can plan around it.

Before approval: the Skills to Foster preparation course

Preparation training happens during the assessment, before you go to the fostering panel. The Fostering Network describes The Skills to Foster as the UK's leading preparation course for prospective foster carers. A new edition launched in June 2025, built around being child-centred, relationship-based and trauma-informed. It is certified for 18 hours of continuing professional development, and when a service commissions it, it is usually delivered over three days to no more than 12 fostering households at a time, in person or online.

The current edition is organised into six sessions:

  • What children need
  • Fostering relationships
  • A protective and loving home
  • Therapeutic care
  • Supporting identity
  • Transitions and endings

What the preparation course is really for

Preparation training is not an exam, and nobody is expecting you to arrive knowing the answers. It does three jobs. First, it gives you a realistic picture of what fostering involves: why children come into care, how trauma and loss can affect behaviour, and why keeping links with birth family matters. Second, it introduces you to the language and processes you will meet, such as care plans, contact, safer caring and record keeping. Third, it helps you and the service decide together whether fostering is right for your household.

Foster Wales says the course is typically run over three full days or six evenings, and that most councils in Wales use The Skills to Foster while some use other versions of preparation training. In Northern Ireland, the regional HSC fostering service says every enquirer is invited to a preparation course, named as Skills to Foster, before the social work assessment interviews begin.

In Wales, Foster Wales says the course is delivered by local authority fostering teams. Whoever runs it, the course is the right place to ask questions and to be open about anything you are unsure of: it sits alongside your assessment, which in Northern Ireland, for example, continues with strength-based social work interviews before your application goes to the fostering panel. If you live with a partner, ask the service whether they should attend with you.

After approval in England: the TSD Standards

In England, every newly approved foster carer must complete the Training, Support and Development Standards for foster care. GOV.UK says you need to complete the TSD workbook within 12 months of being approved. The Fostering Network says the deadline is 18 months for kinship (family and friends) carers, and Camden Council's guidance for its carers sets out the same 12 and 18 month split. The Department for Education publishes the workbook, a guide for foster carers and a guide for supervising social workers and managers.

The standards are not a course you sit, but a set of things you show you understand, with evidence gathered in the workbook from your training, your day-to-day fostering and conversations with your supervising social worker. Camden lists the seven standards for foster carers as:

  • Understand the principles and values essential for fostering children and young people
  • Understand your role as a foster carer
  • Understand health and safety, and health care
  • Know how to communicate effectively
  • Understand the development of children and young people
  • Keep children and young people safe from harm
  • Develop yourself

How carers are supported through the TSD Standards

You do not work through the standards alone. Camden, for example, says carers are supported by their supervising social worker or an allocated mentor, and that progress is part of the annual foster carer review. GOV.UK says your fostering service should help you with a personal development plan and with taking part in learning and development sessions.

Family and friends carers have their own version with six standards, reflecting the fact that they are caring for a child they already know. There is also separate Department for Education guidance on how the standards apply to support carers, who provide short breaks for children and their families.

A practical tip: start the workbook early and add to it as you go. Much of the evidence comes from things you will be doing anyway, such as keeping daily records, attending a meeting about a child, or completing a safeguarding course. Ask your supervising social worker what form of evidence your service prefers and how often you will review progress together.

Ongoing training every year

Approval is the start, not the end, of your learning. The Fostering Network says each service sets its own expectations about how much training carers do each year, and that learning and development is discussed at your annual review. As one example, Coventry City Council's guidance for its carers sets a guideline of 20 hours of development activity every 12 months, alongside a personal development plan.

The Fostering Network describes the usual offer as pre-approval training, induction training for newly approved carers, the TSD Standards in England, and a set of mandatory courses that are often refreshed every two or three years. Foster Wales describes something similar: induction usually before your first child is placed, a programme for your first year, and core courses every carer must do. Typical core and refresher topics include:

  • safeguarding and child protection
  • safer caring in the home
  • first aid
  • attachment and the effects of trauma
  • online and social media safety
  • record keeping and working with birth families

Specialist and optional training

Beyond the core courses, most services offer a wider programme you can choose from depending on the children you care for. The Fostering Network lists examples such as life story work, trauma, fetal alcohol spectrum disorder (FASD), digital skills, tax and finances for foster carers, and caring for unaccompanied asylum-seeking children.

Training comes in many forms: classroom days, evening sessions, online modules you can complete at home, support groups, and learning from your supervising social worker. In Northern Ireland, for example, each HSC trust publishes a yearly training programme for its kinship and non-kinship foster carers, alongside online training. The HSC fostering service there also lists membership of The Fostering Network among the support it gives carers.

When you compare fostering services, it is reasonable to ask to see their training programme, how flexible the timings are, whether they offer childcare or online options, and whether more training leads to a higher fee level. Training is one of the clearest signs of how well a service supports its carers.

Training, pay and time off work

Training expectations are not set at one national level, so they vary from service to service. Any link between the training you complete and the fee you are paid is a matter for each service's own payment scheme, so if this matters to you, ask the service you are considering to explain how its fees work and whether training plays any part.

Training is also a matter of fairness and time. GOV.UK points out that foster carers do not have a statutory right to time off work for fostering, so if you work, check how a service schedules its preparation and core training. Many run evening, weekend or online sessions for this reason.

Across the UK

England
Preparation training (usually The Skills to Foster or a similar course) happens during assessment. After approval, carers must complete the Training, Support and Development Standards workbook within 12 months; The Fostering Network says kinship carers have 18 months. The Fostering Network notes there is no national expectation beyond the induction standards, so ongoing training levels are set by each service.
Scotland
There are no mandatory induction standards like England's TSD Standards. The Scottish Social Services Council's Standard for Foster Care, published in 2017, sets learning expectations before approval, after approval and for continuing development, but it is not mandatory. In its 2024 to 2025 consultation on the future of foster care, the Scottish Government asked whether there should be a new national learning framework for foster carers; most of those who answered that question agreed, and the analysis was published in October 2025. No decision on mandatory training had been made in that report.
Wales
Foster Wales says most councils in Wales use The Skills to Foster for preparation training, typically over three full days or six evenings, followed by induction, a first-year programme and core courses every carer must complete. The All Wales Induction Framework for Health and Social Care is not mandatory for foster carers, but The Fostering Network says carers are expected to complete it.
Northern Ireland
The regional HSC fostering service invites every enquirer to a Skills to Foster preparation course before the assessment interviews. The Fostering Network says there are no induction standards or learning and development frameworks for foster carers in Northern Ireland, but each HSC trust publishes an annual training programme for its carers, with online training alongside.

Common questions

Do I need qualifications to become a foster carer?

No. You do not need any formal qualifications or previous training. Your fostering service will provide preparation training during your assessment and further training after approval. What services look for is the ability to care for children safely and a willingness to learn.

How long is the Skills to Foster course?

The Fostering Network certifies the current edition for 18 hours of continuing professional development, usually delivered over three days. Foster Wales says courses in Wales typically run over three full days or six evenings. The exact format depends on the service running it.

What are the TSD Standards?

The Training, Support and Development Standards are England's induction standards for foster carers. You show you meet them by completing a workbook of evidence within 12 months of approval (18 months for kinship carers, according to The Fostering Network), with support from your supervising social worker. They do not apply in Scotland, Wales or Northern Ireland.

When does Skills to Foster happen?

During your assessment, before you go to the fostering panel. In Northern Ireland, for example, the HSC fostering service invites every enquirer to the preparation course before the social work assessment interviews. Your service will tell you the dates of its next course.

How much training do foster carers do each year?

There is no single national figure. Each service sets its own expectations; for example, Coventry City Council sets a guideline of 20 hours a year for its carers. Mandatory courses such as safeguarding and first aid are often refreshed every two or three years, and your training is reviewed at your annual review.

Can I do foster carer training online or in the evenings?

Often, yes. Many services run preparation courses over evenings or online, and offer online modules for ongoing training. Ask the service you are considering how it fits training around work and childcare.
