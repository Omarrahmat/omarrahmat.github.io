How does school work for a foster child? PEPs, virtual schools and extra support explained

If you foster a school-age child, you become one of the most important people in their education. This guide explains the personal education plan, the virtual school head and the designated teacher, the extra funding schools and councils receive, what happens about school moves and admissions, and how the arrangements differ in England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland.

The short answer

A child in foster care goes to school like any other child, usually a local mainstream school, and you do the everyday things a parent would: the school run, homework, parents' evenings, reading the newsletters and keeping in touch with teachers. What is different is that the law in every part of the UK recognises that children in care often have had their education disrupted, so there is a layer of extra planning, people and money around them.

Three things sit at the heart of that. Every looked-after child should have a personal education plan (a PEP), which is part of their wider care plan and is reviewed regularly. There is a named member of staff in the school whose job is to look out for children in care. And the council that looks after the child has a duty to promote the child's education, with extra funding to help.

The details, including the names of the plans, the timescales and how the money works, differ between the four nations. As a foster carer you are not expected to know the law; you are expected to be involved, to speak up for the child, and to tell your supervising social worker or the child's social worker when something is not working. The rest of this guide explains what to expect.

The personal education plan (PEP)

The PEP is a written plan for the child's education: where they are now, what they need, the targets they are working towards, and who will do what to help. In England, the Department for Education's statutory guidance for local authorities says that all looked-after children must have a care plan, of which the PEP is an integral part.

In England the child's social worker, supported by the virtual school head, takes the lead in starting the PEP, which includes meeting the school and the carer. Where a child comes into care in an emergency, the guidance says the PEP should be started within 10 working days of the child becoming looked after, and it should be available for the first statutory review of the care plan. The guidance asks virtual school heads to arrange for PEPs to be reviewed each school term, including how the extra funding has been used. Where a child also has an education, health and care (EHC) plan, the PEP review should be linked with the EHC plan review at least once a year.

In Wales, the Welsh Government's PEP guidance for practitioners, published in July 2026, says that in an emergency placement the PEP must be started within 10 working days, and that a version must be available for the first statutory review of the child's care and support plan, 20 days after the child enters care. It recommends as good practice that the PEP is reviewed at least every six months and whenever something significant changes, such as a placement move, a change of school or turning 16. The carer or foster parent is listed among the people who should attend PEP meetings.

What this means for you in practice:

  • You will usually be invited to PEP meetings, normally held at the school. Go if you can: you see the child every day and your view of how they are doing matters.
  • In England the guidance says the carer should have a copy of the latest version of the PEP. If you have not been given one, ask the child's social worker.
  • Before a meeting, jot down what you have noticed: friendships, sleep, worries about particular lessons, homework battles, things the child is proud of.
  • The child's own views should be part of the plan. You can help them think about what they want to say, or say it for them if they would rather.

The virtual school head and the designated teacher

In England every council has a virtual school head. Despite the name, the virtual school is not a building; it is a small team that oversees the education of all the children the council looks after, wherever they live and go to school, as if they all attended a single school. The statutory guidance describes the virtual school head as the lead officer for making sure arrangements are in place to improve the education and outcomes of the council's looked-after children, including those placed outside the council's own area. Since 2023 the Department for Education has also extended the virtual school head's strategic role, through non-statutory guidance, to children with a social worker and children in kinship care, without changing the existing duties for looked-after children.

Inside the school, there is a designated teacher for looked-after children in England. The statutory guidance says the designated teacher leads on how the PEP is developed and used in school, so that the child's progress towards their targets is monitored. Wales has an equivalent role, the designated person, which every maintained school's governing body must appoint under the Children and Young Persons Act 2008. Welsh Government guidance for that role says it should have the same prominence in school as other key roles such as the special educational needs coordinator.

For a foster carer, the designated teacher or designated person is usually the first person to contact at school about anything to do with the child's care status, the PEP, or extra help. Your class teacher or form tutor is still the person for day-to-day matters.

Extra money for a foster child's education

Each nation gives extra education funding for children in care. It is not paid to foster carers and it is not a personal budget for the child; it is managed by the council or the school to pay for support that will help the child learn. It is still worth knowing about, because the PEP meeting is where you can ask how it is being used for the child you look after.

The figures below are the ones published by each government for the current year:

  • England: pupil premium plus. The Department for Education's technical note for 2026 to 2027 sets the rate for looked-after children at £2,690 per pupil, and the same for previously looked-after children. The money for looked-after children goes to the council, and the virtual school head, working with the child's school, is responsible for making sure it is spent on evidence-informed support for the child's educational needs. The conditions of grant say it must not be used for things the council already has to fund, such as support for foster carers, school uniforms or transport to school.
  • Wales: the Pupil Development Grant for children looked after. The Welsh Government's guide, published in March 2026, says funding of £1,150 per child is given to local authorities through the Local Authority Education Grant, based on the number of children aged 3 to 15 in care. It is intended to benefit children looked after, children who have been adopted and children who left care under a special guardianship order, and it should not be used for services the council already has to fund, such as support for foster carers or school transport.
  • Scotland: the Care Experienced Children and Young People Fund. The Scottish Government's operational guidance for 2026/2027 says the fund is provided to local authorities at £1,225 per looked-after child aged 5 to 15, to improve the educational outcomes and experiences of care-experienced children and young people from birth to age 26.
  • Northern Ireland: the Department of Education's pages on children looked after describe the personal education plan and list contacts for Looked After Children teams rather than a per-pupil grant, so ask the child's social worker or the school what extra help is available locally.

School places, school moves and admissions

One of the biggest worries for children coming into care is having to change school as well as home. The English statutory guidance says councils should do everything possible to minimise disruption to a child's education, and that a new school place should be arranged at the same time as the care placement where a move cannot be avoided. Where a child moves in an emergency, the council that looks after the child should secure a suitable new education placement within 20 school days.

On admissions, the same guidance says looked-after and previously looked-after children have the highest priority in all mainstream schools' admission arrangements in England, and that schools judged good or outstanding by Ofsted should be prioritised when choosing a school. A decision about which school a child goes to is usually not the foster carer's alone: it is normally made by the council with the child's parents, after discussion with the child, the social worker and you. Your fostering service's delegated authority arrangements, set out in the child's placement plan, say what you can sign and decide.

If a child is staying with you for a short time, the plan may be to keep them at their existing school even if it is some distance away. Ask early who will arrange and pay for transport. England's pupil premium conditions of grant say the extra funding must not be used for things the council is responsible for funding, including transport to get the child to school.

Additional needs and special educational needs

Many children in care need extra help at school, sometimes because of learning difficulties and sometimes because of what they have been through. The systems for this differ across the UK.

In Scotland the law goes furthest: the Scottish Government's statutory guidance on additional support for learning says the amended Act presumes that all looked-after children have additional support needs unless the education authority decides they do not need additional support to benefit from school education. Its 2026/2027 funding guidance makes the same point for all care-experienced children. In England, where a child has an education, health and care plan, the guidance links the PEP review with the EHC plan review. In Wales, the Pupil Development Grant guide says the grant cannot be used to replace or sustain support a child is already entitled to under the additional learning needs system.

If you think a child you foster needs more help than they are getting, say so at the PEP meeting and to the child's social worker. They, the designated teacher and the virtual school (or its equivalent in your nation) are the people who can take it forward.

What foster carers do day to day

The formal plans matter, but most of what makes a difference happens at home. Carers who value education, encourage children and work with the school can make a real difference. In practice that tends to mean:

  • A calm routine for mornings, homework and bedtime, and a place to do homework.
  • Reading with younger children and showing an interest in what older children are studying.
  • Going to parents' evenings, concerts and sports days, so the child sees someone turn up for them.
  • Keeping the school informed, within what has been agreed about sharing information, when something at home or around family time may affect the child in class.
  • Asking your fostering service about extra costs: gov.uk notes that foster carers in England may be able to apply to their fostering service for extra money for things like school trips. Ask your service what its own policy is for uniform and equipment.
  • Keeping school reports, certificates and photos safely, because they become part of the child's own record of their childhood.

Questions to ask a fostering service before you apply

If school and learning matter to you, it is reasonable to ask a council or agency how it supports carers with education. Useful questions include:

  • Will I be invited to PEP meetings and given a copy of the plan?
  • How do your carers work with the virtual school or the local education support team?
  • What training do you offer on supporting children's learning, trauma and school difficulties?
  • Who pays for school uniform, trips and transport, and how do I claim?
  • What can I sign for school (trips, photos, clubs) under delegated authority, and what needs the social worker?
  • What happens about school if a child is placed with me in an emergency from another area?

Across the UK

England
Department for Education statutory guidance requires every looked-after child to have a personal education plan as part of their care plan, overseen by the council's virtual school head and led in school by a designated teacher. In an emergency the PEP should be started within 10 working days and be ready for the first care plan review, and PEPs should be reviewed each term. Looked-after and previously looked-after children have the highest priority in mainstream school admissions. Pupil premium plus for 2026 to 2027 is £2,690 per looked-after child, managed by the virtual school head with the school.
Scotland
The education authority must presume that every looked-after child has additional support needs unless it decides they do not need additional support, and a co-ordinated support plan is the statutory plan prepared where a child needs significant support from education and at least one other agency. The Care Experienced Children and Young People Fund gives councils £1,225 per looked-after child aged 5 to 15 for 2026/2027, to support care-experienced children and young people up to age 26.
Wales
Every maintained school must have a designated person for children looked after. Welsh Government PEP guidance published in July 2026 says a PEP must be started within 10 working days of an emergency placement and be available for the first statutory review of the care and support plan, with reviews recommended at least every six months. The Pupil Development Grant for children looked after is £1,150 per child, paid to local authorities. In October 2026 the Welsh Government accepted Estyn's recommendations on care-experienced learners and said it would review the grant.
Northern Ireland
Children looked after have a personal education plan. The Department of Education issued revised PEP guidance for primary-age pupils in February 2023 (Circular 2023/03), built around five themes and completed jointly by the school, social worker, carer and education support services, with the child's voice throughout; the 2011 guidance still applies to post-primary pupils. The Department of Health has said the Adoption and Children Act (Northern Ireland) 2022 puts personal education plans on a statutory basis. The Education Authority delivers the guidance and training.

Common questions

Do foster children have to change school when they come into care?

Not necessarily. Guidance in England says councils should do everything possible to avoid disrupting a child's education, and often the plan is to keep the child at their school. Where a move is unavoidable, the new school place should be arranged alongside the care placement, and in an emergency the council should find a suitable school place within 20 school days in England.

What is a PEP in foster care?

A personal education plan is a written plan for a looked-after child's education, setting out their needs, targets and the support they will get. It is part of the child's care plan and is reviewed regularly, termly in England and at least every six months as good practice in Wales. Foster carers are normally invited to PEP meetings.

Do foster carers get the pupil premium?

No. In England pupil premium plus for a looked-after child (£2,690 for 2026 to 2027) is paid to the council and managed by the virtual school head with the school, to pay for support for the child's learning. It is not paid to carers and is not a personal budget. Your allowance and any extra payments for school costs come from your fostering service.

Can a foster carer choose the child's school?

Usually the decision is made by the council with the child's parents, after talking to the child, the social worker and the foster carer. Your views carry weight because you know the child day to day. The child's placement plan sets out which school matters you can decide or sign for yourself.

Who do I talk to at school about a foster child?

The designated teacher for looked-after children in England, or the designated person in Wales, is the usual contact for anything to do with the child being in care, the PEP and extra support. Day-to-day matters go to the class teacher or form tutor as for any child. In Northern Ireland and Scotland, ask the school who leads on care-experienced pupils.

Can I foster if I have no experience of the school system?

Yes. You do not need to be a teacher or know education law. Fostering services train carers, and the social worker, virtual school and designated teacher are there to handle the formal side. What services look for is someone who will encourage the child, keep a steady routine and work with the school.
