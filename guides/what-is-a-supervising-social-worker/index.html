What is a supervising social worker in fostering?

Every approved foster carer has their own social worker from the fostering service, usually called a supervising social worker. Here is what they do, how often you can expect to see them, how they differ from the child's social worker, and what the rules say in England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland.

The short answer

A supervising social worker is a qualified social worker employed by your fostering service, whether that is a council or an independent agency, whose job is to support you and oversee your work as a foster carer. Some services call them a fostering social worker, a link worker or a supervising practitioner, but the role is the same. They are allocated to you, the carer, not to the child.

Official guidance in England, reported by Community Care, says every foster carer should be allocated an appropriately qualified social worker from the fostering service, and describes their role as supervising the carer's work, making sure the child's needs are being met, assessing the carer's performance, helping them develop their skills and offering support. Scotland's care standards for foster care promise that carers will be supervised and supported by an appropriately qualified social worker. In Northern Ireland, the regional HSC fostering service tells carers they will have a supervising social worker allocated from their local fostering team.

If you are thinking about fostering, this person matters a great deal. Research by The Fostering Network, reported by Community Care, found that carers' experience of their supervising social worker is closely linked to whether they stay in fostering. Asking a service about its supervising social workers before you apply is one of the most useful questions you can ask.

What a supervising social worker actually does

The British Association of Social Workers describes the supervising social worker as the link between the fostering household and the fostering service, and as a role distinct from that of the foster child's social worker. The job mixes two things: support, and supervision. At times it leans more towards one than the other, for example becoming more supportive after a bereavement in the family, or more supervisory if a safeguarding concern is raised.

In practice, councils' published fostering procedures describe a fairly consistent set of tasks. Wakefield's procedures put it simply: the role is to supervise the foster carer in carrying out the fostering tasks, and to support them through advice and advocacy. Typical parts of the job include:

  • regular supervision meetings at your home, with an agenda and a written record you both sign
  • talking through each child in your care: their health, education, family time (contact), behaviour and progress against their care plan
  • checking your own records and diary notes about the child, and any accidents, medication or incidents
  • helping you through difficulties, and speaking up for you with the child's social worker, school or other professionals where needed
  • agreeing your training and development plan and tracking your progress against it
  • making at least one unannounced visit to your home each year
  • preparing for and contributing to your annual foster carer review
  • making sure you know how to get help out of hours, and about support groups and helplines

How often will you see them?

There is no single national number for routine visits; the frequency is set by each fostering service within the rules. What the official standards do fix is the unannounced visit. England's National Minimum Standards for fostering services say the foster home is inspected annually, without appointment, by the fostering service, and Scotland's National Care Standards for foster care say supervision includes at least one unannounced visit each year.

Councils' own procedures give a feel for what happens in practice. Brighton and Hove's fostering procedures say the supervising social worker will carry out a supervision visit monthly while a child is placed, with formal supervision meetings at least every six months, and telephone contact at least every four weeks alongside extra support visits. Wakefield's procedures set a local standard of visits every six weeks, plus extra visits when needed, and say a manager may agree less frequent visits for a long-term placement that is very settled, as long as the reason is recorded and raised at the annual review.

Between visits, contact is usually by phone and email. Northern Ireland's HSC fostering service tells its carers they will meet their supervising social worker regularly and that they are always just a phone call away. Bear in mind that supervising social workers usually work office hours: Lancashire County Council's description of the role gives core hours of 08:45 to 17:00, with evening work only for agreed visits or support groups. Out-of-hours help comes from a separate service, covered below.

Supervising social worker or child's social worker: what is the difference?

This is the question new carers most often get muddled. When a child is placed with you, there are at least two social workers involved, and they have different jobs.

The child's social worker works for the council that is responsible for the child. They hold the child's care plan, make the big decisions about the child's future with the courts and the child's family, arrange family time, and carry out the statutory visits to see the child. Wakefield's procedures make the point directly: it is the child's social worker who is responsible for statutory visits to the child. Brighton and Hove's procedures say the child's social worker is the person to contact for specific advice about the child's care plan and placement plan.

Your supervising social worker works for your fostering service and is there for you and your household. If you foster through an independent agency, the child's social worker will be from the placing council while your supervising social worker will be from the agency. If you foster for a council, both may work for the same council, but in different teams.

The Fostering Network describes both as part of the team around the child, alongside you, the birth family and other professionals, and says foster carers always need to work in partnership with both. Its 2024 survey of foster carers, reported by Community Care, suggests carers often feel better supported by their supervising social worker than by children's social workers: 76% of current carers said supervising social workers always or usually treated them as equal and valued, compared with 53% for children's social workers.

Supervision meetings, records and your annual review

Supervision is not an inspection, but it is formal. Councils' procedures describe a set agenda covering anything outstanding from last time, how you and your family are, each child's health, education, family time and care plan, any accidents, complaints, restraint or medication, safer caring, and training. The supervising social worker will usually read the notes you keep about the child.

Afterwards a written supervision record is made. Brighton and Hove's procedures say it is signed by the foster carer and the supervising social worker, kept on the carer's file, and copied to the carer; in Wakefield it is also signed by a team manager. You can, and should, ask for anything you disagree with to be noted.

These records feed into your annual review. Scotland's care standards say that each year the agency reviews your performance and the quality of care you give, and that a review meeting is held with your supervising social worker; they add that a review should be held as soon as possible after any significant incident, complaint or allegation. In England, councils' procedures describe the same pattern: Wakefield's, for example, say training must be evaluated in each foster carer's annual review, and that any agreed change to visit frequency must be highlighted there. Ask your service how its reviews work and what your supervising social worker will bring to them.

Training, out-of-hours help and other support

Your supervising social worker is the main route to the rest of the support your service offers. The law in England says fostering services must give foster parents the training, advice, information and support they need in the interests of the children placed with them, including support outside office hours. Both sets of Welsh regulations, for agencies and for councils, use almost the same words, and add that services must ensure foster parents are appropriately supervised.

Training starts with the preparation course you take during assessment, often Skills to Foster, and continues after approval. In England, newly approved carers work through the Training, Support and Development Standards for foster care, with evidence collected in a workbook or portfolio; Wakefield's procedures expect carers to complete them by their first annual review. Your supervising social worker identifies your training needs, books or releases courses and keeps your training record.

Out-of-hours support is usually provided by a separate on-call or emergency duty team rather than by your own worker. In Northern Ireland, the HSC fostering service tells carers that outside office hours there is the Regional Emergency Social Work Service. Scotland's standards say carers should know where to get support 24 hours a day. Services also run support groups; HSC NI Fostering, for example, lists monthly groups by area and pays for its carers to be members of The Fostering Network, which gives access to a helpline and legal insurance.

When the relationship is not working

Most carers value their supervising social worker, but changes of worker are common. The Fostering Network's 2024 research, as reported by Community Care, found that 96% of carers had an allocated supervising practitioner, but only 44% had had the same one throughout the previous two years, with carers averaging 2.2 supervising social workers over that time. Current carers were far more positive than people who had stopped fostering: 74% of current carers rated their supervising social worker's support as good or excellent, compared with 47% of former carers.

If you are unhappy, start by raising it with the worker directly, then with their team manager. Your service's foster carer handbook should explain how to raise a concern or make a complaint. The Fostering Network's advice lines can talk you through your options if you are a member. Nothing in this guide is legal advice about a specific dispute; your fostering service or an independent adviser can tell you how its procedures apply to you.

Questions to ask a fostering service before you apply

Because the supervising social worker shapes so much of your day-to-day experience, it is worth asking about them early, whether you are talking to a council or an independent agency.

  • How often will my supervising social worker visit when a child is placed, and when no child is placed?
  • How many carers does each supervising social worker look after?
  • How often have your carers had a change of worker in the last two years?
  • Who do I call in the evening, at weekends and on bank holidays, and is that person a social worker who knows fostering?
  • What training is there in the first year, and is it paid or on a day I can manage around work?
  • Are there local support groups, buddies or mentors, and do you pay for membership of The Fostering Network?

Across the UK

England
The Fostering Services (England) Regulations 2011 require fostering services to give foster parents the training, advice, information and support they need, including support outside office hours. The National Minimum Standards for fostering services (2011) set out what supervision and support should look like, including an annual inspection of the foster home without appointment. Councils and independent agencies set their own visit frequency within these rules.
Scotland
Scotland's National Care Standards for foster care and family placement services (Scottish Government, 2011) say carers will be supervised and supported by an appropriately qualified social worker, including at least one unannounced visit a year, that the agency social worker keeps in regular contact, that carers know where to get support 24 hours a day, and that an annual review meeting is held with the supervising social worker. mygov.scot tells carers they will have a supervising social worker to speak to whenever they need help.
Wales
In Wales, the Regulated Fostering Services (Service Providers and Responsible Individuals) (Wales) Regulations 2019 cover independent agencies and the Local Authority Fostering Services (Wales) Regulations 2018 cover councils (Foster Wales). Both require training, advice and support for foster parents, including support outside office hours, require the service to monitor and review that support, and say foster parents must be appropriately supervised.
Northern Ireland
Fostering in Northern Ireland is provided through the five regional HSC fostering teams (Belfast, Northern, South Eastern, Southern and Western) and independent agencies. HSC NI Fostering tells carers they will have a supervising social worker from their local fostering team and that the Regional Emergency Social Work Service covers out of hours. The Department of Health consulted from June to September 2025 on new Minimum Standards for Fostering Services, which it intends to introduce for both statutory and independent providers.

Common questions

Is a supervising social worker the same as the child's social worker?

No. The supervising social worker works for your fostering service and supports and supervises you as a carer. The child's social worker works for the council responsible for the child, holds the child's care plan and visits the child. You will work with both.

How often does a supervising social worker visit?

It depends on the service. Councils' published procedures range from monthly visits while a child is placed (Brighton and Hove) to every six weeks (Wakefield), with phone contact in between. Official standards require at least one unannounced visit to your home each year.

What is an unannounced visit and should I worry about it?

It is a visit without an appointment, at least once a year, to see the home as it normally is: who is there, who is looking after the child and what arrangements are in place if you are out. It is a routine safeguard for every carer, not a sign that anyone has a concern.

Do I get a supervising social worker before a child is placed?

Yes. Lancashire County Council, for example, describes the supervising social worker as the allocated social worker for approved foster carers, so you are allocated one once you are approved. Brighton and Hove's procedures still require formal supervision meetings at least every six months, and training starts in your first year.

Can I ask for a different supervising social worker?

You can ask. Raise any concerns with the worker first, then their team manager; your carer handbook should explain how to raise a complaint. Whether a change is possible depends on the service.

Is my supervising social worker available at night and weekends?

Usually not personally; most work office hours. Fostering services must provide support outside office hours, normally through an on-call or emergency duty team. Ask your service exactly who answers and how.
