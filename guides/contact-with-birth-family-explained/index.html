Contact with birth family in foster care: what family time involves

Most children in foster care keep in touch with their parents, brothers and sisters or other people who matter to them. Here is what contact (often now called family time) is, who decides it, what foster carers are asked to do, how children may react, and how the law differs in England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland.

The short answer

Contact, which many services now call family time, means the ways a child in foster care stays in touch with their birth family and other people who are important to them. It can be a visit in person, a phone or video call, a letter or a card. For most fostered children some form of contact is part of everyday life, and supporting it is one of the most important parts of a foster carer's job.

Foster carers do not decide whether contact happens or how often. That is set out in the child's care plan by the council or trust responsible for the child, and in some cases by a court or, in Scotland, a children's hearing. Your part is to help the child before, during and after contact, to make arrangements work in practice, and to tell the child's social worker what you notice.

Every child is different, and the right amount and type of contact changes over time. Your fostering service will explain how it organises contact and what it expects of you, and your supervising social worker is the person to ask when something is unclear.

Why contact matters

Children do not stop belonging to their families when they come into foster care. Keeping in touch helps them hold on to their identity and history, understand why they are being looked after, and keep relationships that may last long after the placement ends. For children who are expected to go home, regular contact is also part of how that return is planned and tested.

The law reflects this. In every part of the UK, the authority looking after a child has a legal duty either to allow reasonable contact with parents and certain others, or to promote contact, unless that would not be right for the child. The Fostering Network describes foster carers as playing a crucial role in helping children keep these relationships going.

Contact is not automatic in every case, though. The Fostering Network is clear that children must be protected from anyone who poses a risk to them, and that can include members of their own family. Where contact would harm a child, it can be limited, supervised or stopped, through the routes described below.

The different kinds of contact

Direct contact means meeting face to face. Indirect contact means keeping in touch without meeting, for example by phone, video call, letter, card, email or text. A child might have several kinds at once: weekly visits with a parent, a video call with a grandparent and cards from an older brother who lives elsewhere.

Face-to-face contact is usually described by how much support or oversight it has. The Fostering Network sets out three broad levels:

  • Unsupervised: nobody facilitates or watches the contact, for example a child spending an afternoon with a grandparent.
  • Facilitated: someone helps the contact to happen and go well, for example at a children's centre, without formally supervising it.
  • Supervised: a formal arrangement in which a named supervisor is present throughout and keeps a written record of what happens.
  • Other arrangements can include overnight stays with family, which are only agreed where the care plan allows them.

Who decides contact arrangements

The type of contact, how often it happens, where, and who is involved are agreed as part of the child's care plan, and reviewed regularly. The child's social worker leads this, taking account of the child's wishes and feelings, the views of the parents, and the views of the people caring for the child. The Fostering Network says foster carers should be part of those discussions, and your experience of how a child copes is valuable evidence.

Where a child is subject to a court order, the court can also make orders about contact. In England and Wales, for example, section 34 of the Children Act 1989 lets the court make an order about contact with a named person, attach conditions to it, or allow the council to refuse contact with a particular person. Before making a care order, the court must look at the council's proposed contact arrangements and invite the parties to comment on them.

Councils can also act quickly if a child is at risk. Under section 34, a council in England or Wales may refuse contact that it would otherwise have to allow if this is needed to safeguard or promote the child's welfare, but only as a matter of urgency and for no more than seven days. Northern Ireland has an equivalent rule. In England, regulation 8 of the Care Planning, Placement and Case Review (England) Regulations 2010 then requires the council to give written notice of the decision, its reasons and how long it will last, to the people affected, including the child where appropriate and the independent reviewing officer, and to record the decision in the child's care plan.

What foster carers are usually asked to do

What you do depends on the child's plan and your service's procedures, but the role is mostly practical and emotional support. The Fostering Network describes it as offering the child a consistent, reliable base. In practice that can include:

  • Getting the child ready: talking about who they will see and when, in a way that suits their age, and keeping to the plan.
  • Transport: taking the child to and from a family centre or contact centre, or meeting a driver your service has arranged.
  • Phone and video calls: helping a younger child make a call at the agreed time, and sometimes staying nearby or supervising, if the plan says so.
  • Letters and cards: helping the child write or read them, and passing them to the social worker if the plan says post must go through the service.
  • Supervising, only when asked: some carers are asked to supervise contact themselves; this is agreed in advance, and you should be told exactly what to watch for and record.
  • Recording: noting how the child was before and after contact, anything they said, and anything that worried you, and sharing it with the child's social worker.

How children may react, and how to help

Contact can bring up strong feelings. A child may be excited beforehand and then sad, angry or withdrawn afterwards. Some children sleep badly, go back to younger behaviour for a while, or show feelings they have been holding in. The Fostering Network notes that children may find contact stressful or upsetting, that this can show in their behaviour, and asks carers to be mindful of the effect contact has before and after, and to record and share what they see.

None of this means contact is wrong for the child, but patterns matter. If a child is regularly very distressed, a parent often fails to arrive, or something said at contact worries you, tell the child's social worker. They can look at whether the arrangements need to change.

A few habits help most children. Keep the day after contact calm and predictable. Let the child talk about their family without judgement, and never criticise their parents in front of them. If a visit is cancelled, explain it simply and honestly without blaming anyone. Children notice when the adults around them work together, and a respectful relationship between foster carers and birth parents, where that is safe, can make contact much easier for the child.

Brothers, sisters and other important people

Contact is not only about parents. Many children in care are separated from brothers and sisters, and keeping those relationships matters a great deal to them. Grandparents, aunts and uncles, former foster carers and family friends can be important too.

The law is moving towards stronger rights for siblings. In Scotland, the Children (Scotland) Act 2020 added a duty on councils, in force since 26 July 2021, to promote regular personal relations and direct contact between a looked after child and their brothers and sisters, and others they have lived with in a sibling-like relationship, where that fits with the council's duty to the child. In Wales, the duty to promote contact already covers relatives, friends and anyone else connected with the child.

In England, an Act of Parliament passed in 2026 (2026 chapter 21) includes a section adding brothers and sisters, including half and step-siblings, to the list of people a council must allow reasonable contact with under section 34 of the Children Act 1989. When we checked in November 2026 that change had not yet come into force. If you foster siblings who live apart, or a child who has siblings elsewhere, ask the social worker how their contact is planned.

Things to ask a fostering service

If you are thinking about fostering, contact is worth discussing early, because it shapes your week. Useful questions include:

  • How much contact do the children you place usually have, and where does it take place?
  • Will I be expected to drive children to contact, and are mileage or travel costs covered?
  • Who supervises contact, and would I ever be asked to?
  • What training do you offer on supporting family time and working with birth parents?
  • Who do I call if something goes wrong at contact, including out of hours?

Across the UK

England
Under section 34 of the Children Act 1989, a council must allow a child in care reasonable contact with their parents, any guardian or special guardian, and certain others who had care of the child before the care order. Courts can make, vary or refuse contact orders, and the council can refuse contact in an emergency for up to seven days. Regulation 8 of the Care Planning, Placement and Case Review (England) Regulations 2010 sets out the written notice the council must give when it refuses, departs from or changes contact. A 2026 Act adds siblings to section 34, but that change was not yet in force when this guide was checked.
Scotland
Section 17 of the Children (Scotland) Act 1995 requires councils to promote personal relations and direct contact between a looked after child and people with parental responsibility, and, since 26 July 2021, with brothers, sisters and others in a sibling-like relationship, where appropriate. For many looked after children, contact is also shaped by a children's hearing: a compulsory supervision order can include a contact direction regulating contact between the child and a named person or group of people.
Wales
Section 95 of the Social Services and Well-being (Wales) Act 2014 requires councils to promote contact between a looked after child and their parents, others with parental responsibility, and any relative, friend or other person connected with the child, unless that is not reasonably practicable or consistent with the child's well-being. Section 96 lets the council help with travel and other costs of family visits where they would otherwise cause undue financial hardship. Section 34 of the Children Act 1989 also applies in Wales.
Northern Ireland
Article 53 of the Children (Northern Ireland) Order 1995 requires the authority looking after a child in care to allow reasonable contact with parents, any guardian and certain others, with court powers to make or refuse contact orders and an emergency power to refuse contact for up to seven days. The Contact with Children Regulations (Northern Ireland) 1996 set out who must be told when contact is refused, departed from by agreement, or changed.

Common questions

Do I have to meet the child's birth parents?

Often you will, at least at handovers or meetings, but it depends on the child's plan and on safety. Your service will not ask you to meet anyone where that would put you or the child at risk, and some placements are arranged so the birth family does not know where the child lives. Talk to your supervising social worker if you have concerns.

Can a foster carer stop contact if the child is upset?

No. Foster carers cannot change or cancel contact themselves. If you are worried about how contact is affecting a child, record what you see and tell the child's social worker straight away; they and, where relevant, a court or children's hearing decide whether the arrangements should change.

How often do fostered children see their families?

There is no single answer. It is set in each child's care plan and can range from several times a week, for a young child who may be going home, to a few letters a year. It is reviewed as the plan changes.

Will I have to supervise contact?

Sometimes. Many services use family centres and trained contact supervisors, but some ask foster carers to supervise phone calls, video calls or visits. This should be agreed in advance, with clear guidance on what to record. Ask any service you are considering how it usually works.

Is contact the same as family time?

Yes. The Fostering Network notes that family time is sometimes called contact, and many services now use the newer term. Either way it means the ways a child keeps in touch with their family and other important people.
