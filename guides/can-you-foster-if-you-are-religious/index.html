Can you foster if you are religious? Faith and fostering explained

Having a religious faith, or none, does not stop you fostering anywhere in the UK. This guide explains how your beliefs come up in the assessment, what the law says about a foster child's own religion, how matching works, and where the rules differ in England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland.

The short answer

Yes. Being religious does not stop you fostering, and neither does having no religion. The fostering regulations in every UK nation ask about your religion, but none of them makes having a faith, or not having one, a bar to approval. Religion or belief is one of the protected characteristics under the Equality Act 2010, and fostering services actively look for carers from many faiths. Brighton and Hove City Council, for example, says plainly that it welcomes foster carers who follow different faiths, and Herefordshire Council tells applicants that their views will not affect their application.

What the law does care about is the child's religion. Every UK nation requires the council or trust looking after a child to take account of that child's religious persuasion, racial origin and cultural and linguistic background when it makes decisions about them, including where they live. So the question a fostering service will really ask is not whether you have a faith, but whether you can care well for a child whose beliefs may be the same as yours, different from yours, or not yet formed at all.

That is why your beliefs are discussed openly during your assessment. It is not a test you pass by having the right religion. It is how the service gets to know you well enough to match children with you sensibly and to support you once a child arrives.

What the law says about a foster child's religion

In England, section 22 of the Children Act 1989 says that when a local authority makes any decision about a child it looks after, it must give due consideration to the child's religious persuasion, racial origin and cultural and linguistic background. In Wales the equivalent duty now sits in section 78 of the Social Services and Well-being (Wales) Act 2014, which requires the authority to have regard to the same four things before making any decision about a child it looks after or proposes to look after.

In Scotland, section 17 of the Children (Scotland) Act 1995 requires the local authority to have regard, so far as practicable, to the child's religious persuasion, racial origin and cultural and linguistic background. The Looked After Children (Scotland) Regulations 2009 go further: the assessment of a child includes their religious persuasion and the need for the child to continue to be brought up in accordance with it, and the written information a foster carer must be given when a child is placed includes the child's religious persuasion alongside their personal history, culture, language and racial origin.

In Northern Ireland, article 26 of the Children (Northern Ireland) Order 1995 contains the same duty to give due consideration to the child's religious persuasion, racial origin and cultural and linguistic background. Northern Ireland's placement rules, set out below, are the most specific in the UK about religion.

In practice, this means the child's own faith, or their family's, is part of the plan for their care. As a foster carer you are not expected to give up your beliefs, but you are expected to respect the child's and to help them keep the links to their faith and culture that matter to them.

How your faith comes up in the fostering assessment

Every fostering assessment gathers detailed information about you and your household, and religion is one of the topics the regulations list. GOV.UK's guide to the assessment in England says the social worker will ask whether you have religious beliefs and whether you are willing and able to care for a child of a different religion, ethnicity or cultural background.

The regulations behind that are similar in each nation. In England, Schedule 3 to the Fostering Services (England) Regulations 2011 lists a prospective foster carer's religious persuasion and their capacity to care for a child from any particular religious persuasion, with a matching item on racial origin, culture and language. Wales uses almost identical wording in Schedule 1 to the Fostering Panels (Establishment and Functions) (Wales) Regulations 2018. Scotland's 2009 regulations and Northern Ireland's 1996 regulations also ask about the degree of your religious observance, not just your faith.

Notice that the focus is on capacity: what you could offer a child, and what support you might need. A social worker may ask how your faith shapes your daily routine, your family's food, your weekends and festivals, and how you would feel if a child did not want to join in. Honest answers help the service match children with you well. There is no right answer that gets you approved and no wrong one that rules you out.

  • Whether you have religious beliefs, and how much they shape day-to-day life at home.
  • Whether you could care for a child of a different religion, or of no religion.
  • How you would support a child to practise their own faith, such as getting to a place of worship or marking festivals.
  • How you would handle conversations about beliefs that differ from yours.

Will you be matched with a child of your own faith?

Sometimes, but you cannot count on it, and in most of the UK it is not a legal requirement. Herefordshire Council says it will try to match carers and children based on their faiths if it is appropriate, because shared beliefs can help build stable homes. Kirklees Council says children from all backgrounds come into care and that, where possible, they are placed with families that will let them carry on practising and developing their faith, which it describes as particularly important for children who will be fostered for longer.

Faith is weighed alongside everything else a matching decision has to consider: the child's safety, keeping brothers and sisters together, staying near school and family, and the carer's skills and availability. Many children need a home quickly, so a perfect match on every point is not always possible. Kirklees describes Muslim foster carers who had thought about fostering only Muslim children, but went on to foster both Muslim and non-Muslim children because children of all backgrounds need homes, often at short notice. Brighton and Hove describes Jewish carers who have fostered both Jewish and non-Jewish children.

If you are only willing to care for children of your own faith, say so during the assessment. The service will take it into account, but it may mean you wait longer for a child, particularly where few children of that faith need foster care locally.

Caring for a child of a different faith, or none

Most foster carers will care for at least one child whose beliefs are not the same as theirs. Herefordshire Council asks applicants to think about how they would feel discussing differing beliefs with a child, or caring for children of different faiths, because the carer's role is to create a supportive, nurturing home where the child can explore their own values and beliefs.

In practice that usually means helping a child to keep up their own practice, if they have one, rather than bringing them into yours. Decisions about a child's religious upbringing, such as a religious ceremony, are significant, and who can make them depends on the child's care plan and on what has been delegated to you. Our guide to delegated authority explains how that works. If in doubt, ask the child's social worker before acting.

Children who have no religion, or whose families have none, should also feel at home. Many religious foster carers find that sharing their own traditions, as something a child is welcome to join or watch rather than required to take part in, works well. Talk this through with your supervising social worker so you and the child's team agree on what is right for that child.

  • Find out what the child and their family want: practice, food, dress, festivals and worship.
  • Ask what the care plan says about the child's religion and who can make decisions about it.
  • Make space for the child's own practice, such as prayer times or dietary rules, even if they differ from yours.
  • Let the child choose whether to join in with your family's faith activities, and make sure they are not left out if they say no.

Fostering with no religious belief

Having no faith is no barrier either. Religion or belief is a protected characteristic in its own right, and assessments ask about beliefs in order to understand you, not to favour religious applicants. Foster Wales, for example, says that it is the skills and experience you bring that matter most.

If you have no religion and a child with a strong faith is placed with you, the same expectations apply as for any carer: respect the child's beliefs, help them keep up their practice and their links with their community, and ask the child's social worker or family for help with anything you are unsure about, such as food rules or festival dates.

Your faith community as a source of support

Many foster carers say their place of worship is a big part of their support network. Kirklees Council notes that places of worship can offer emotional and physical as well as spiritual support, and Brighton and Hove quotes a Christian foster carer who describes her church as her family and support network.

Some councils have pages written for people of faith who are thinking about fostering, so it is worth checking whether your local council or trust has one. Before you apply, it can also help to talk to foster carers who share your faith about how they balance their own practice with caring for children of other backgrounds. Your fostering service will explain what training and support it offers on faith, culture and identity once you are approved.

Across the UK

England
The Children Act 1989 (section 22) requires the local authority to give due consideration to a looked-after child's religious persuasion, racial origin and cultural and linguistic background. The Fostering Services (England) Regulations 2011 (Schedule 3) require the assessment to cover your religious persuasion and your capacity to care for a child of any religious persuasion. There is no legal rule that a child must be placed with carers of the same faith.
Scotland
The Children (Scotland) Act 1995 (section 17) requires the council to have regard, so far as practicable, to the child's religious persuasion and background. The Looked After Children (Scotland) Regulations 2009 make the child's religious persuasion, and the need for them to continue to be brought up in it, part of the child's assessment, and ask about your religious persuasion, your degree of religious observance and your capacity to care for a child of any religious persuasion.
Wales
The Social Services and Well-being (Wales) Act 2014 (section 78) requires the council to have regard to the child's religious persuasion, racial origin and cultural and linguistic background. The Fostering Panels (Establishment and Functions) (Wales) Regulations 2018 (Schedule 1) ask about your religious persuasion and your capacity to care for a child of any religious persuasion, in the same words as England.
Northern Ireland
The Children (Northern Ireland) Order 1995 (article 26) contains the same duty to consider the child's religious persuasion. The Foster Placement (Children) Regulations (Northern Ireland) 1996 go further than the rest of the UK: as made, regulation 5 says that where possible the foster carer should be of the same religious persuasion as the child, or give an undertaking that the child will be brought up in that religious persuasion. Your Health and Social Care Trust can explain how it applies this today.

Common questions

Can Christians, Muslims, Jews, Hindus or Sikhs foster?

Yes. People of every faith foster across the UK, and faith is not a reason to turn down an application. Fostering services ask about your beliefs so they can match children with you well and support you, not to decide whether your religion is acceptable.

Will I have to stop practising my religion if I foster?

No. You can keep practising your faith. What you will be asked to do is respect a foster child's own beliefs and help them keep up their practice, and not to require a child of a different faith, or none, to take part in yours.

Can I ask to foster only children of my own faith?

You can tell the service this during your assessment and it will be taken into account. Outside Northern Ireland, there is no legal rule that children must be placed with carers of the same faith, and being open to children of any background usually means a child can be placed with you sooner.

Can I take a foster child to my place of worship?

It depends on the child, their wishes, their family's wishes and their care plan. Many children are happy to come along, but some will have their own faith or none. Ask the child's social worker what has been agreed, and never make attendance a condition of anything.

Can I foster if I am an atheist or have no religion?

Yes. Having no religion is no barrier to fostering. If a child with a strong faith is placed with you, you would be expected to support them to keep up their practice, and the child's social worker and family can help you with the details.
