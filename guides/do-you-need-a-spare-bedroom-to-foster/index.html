Do you need a spare bedroom to foster?

For most types of fostering, yes: a child placed with you will usually need a bedroom of their own. But the rules have more room in them than many people think, for babies, brothers and sisters, short breaks and small rooms. Here is what official guidance across the UK actually says.

The short answer

In almost every case, yes. Official guidance in England, Scotland and Wales says you will usually need a spare bedroom to foster. GOV.UK says that in England you do not need to own your home, but you will usually need a spare bedroom. mygov.scot lists a spare bedroom among the requirements for becoming a foster carer in Scotland, adding that some councils may make exceptions depending on the age of the child. Foster Wales says a carer must have a spare, suitable bedroom to be approved.

The word that matters is usually. The national rules set a clear expectation for children over three, and fostering services then add their own policies on babies, siblings and short-break care. Those policies differ from one service to the next, so the honest answer for your household depends on who you foster with and which children you hope to care for.

This guide explains why the rule exists, what the national standards say, the common exceptions, what counts as a suitable room, and what you can do if you do not have a spare room today.

Why a room of their own matters

Children come into foster care after a great deal of upheaval. Many have lost their home, their belongings and their routine in a single day, and some have lived in crowded or unsafe conditions. A bedroom that is theirs, with a door, their own bed and somewhere to keep their things, gives them privacy and a place to feel safe while they settle.

It also protects everyone in the household. Fostering services have to think about how children who do not know each other will get on, and about any history a child may have that makes sharing risky. The Fostering Network made this point in 2019, when a Scottish Parliament debate raised the idea of relaxing the rule. Its director in Scotland, Sara Lurie, told Scottish Housing News that the charity would have significant concerns about removing the requirement, because a child's own bedroom, privacy and personal space are very important, and because the effect on foster carers' own children of sharing a room also has to be considered. She suggested services instead find creative ways to support good applicants, such as helping council tenants move to larger homes.

So the spare room is not a test of how big or well-off your home is. It is about giving a child the space and security they need, and about keeping placements stable.

What the national standards say

In England, the Fostering Services National Minimum Standards, published by the Department for Education in 2011, set the baseline. Standard 10 says children should live in foster homes that provide adequate space, to a suitable standard. Standard 10.6 says each child over the age of three should have their own bedroom in the foster home.

The same standard allows sharing in some cases, but with safeguards. If a child cannot have their own room, sharing must be agreed by each child's responsible authority (normally the council that looks after the child), and each child must have their own area within the room. Before seeking that agreement, the fostering service must consider any potential for bullying, any history of abuse or abusive behaviour, the children's own wishes and anything else relevant, and the decision must be recorded in writing.

The standards also expect the foster home to comfortably accommodate everyone who lives there, to be warm and adequately furnished, and to be checked for health and safety, including an unannounced visit at least once a year. In practice this means the assessor will look at the whole home, not just the bedroom.

Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland have their own regulations and standards, and their official guidance for prospective carers is summarised in the nations section below. The direction is the same everywhere: a spare room is expected, with exceptions decided case by case.

The common exceptions

Because the national rule in England applies to children over three, and because services write their own detailed policies, there are some well-established situations where you may not need a completely separate spare room. Examples from published council policies and guidance:

  • Babies sleeping in your room. Several services allow a baby to sleep in a cot in the foster carer's bedroom for a time. Islington and Fostering North Somerset say a spare room is not needed if you care for a baby under two who could sleep in your room. Worcestershire's bedroom-sharing policy allows children up to two in the carers' bedroom. Surrey says babies can usually share your bedroom until around 36 months. Hertfordshire's 2013 policy sets a limit of 18 months. Foster Wales says babies under six months could sleep in a cot in your room, but adds that a spare bedroom is still usually required.
  • Brothers and sisters. Siblings placed together can sometimes share. Foster Wales says same-sex siblings, or brothers and sisters under eight, may be able to share at the local authority's discretion. Hertfordshire's policy sets out age bands for related children sharing and says children of secondary school age would normally have their own room unless they are siblings of the same sex. Surrey says related children under a certain age may be able to share.
  • Short breaks. Some services treat short-break (respite) carers differently. Surrey says short-break carers only need to provide a room when the child is staying overnight, which can make this type of fostering possible if your spare room is also used for other things.
  • Children who are not related are rarely asked to share. Worcestershire, for example, says it will not normally place a child in a mainstream foster home where they would have to share with an unrelated child.

Can a foster child share with my own child?

Generally, no. Foster Wales answers this question directly: in short, they cannot. Hertfordshire's accommodation policy says it is not good practice for foster carers' own children and children in care to share a bedroom, at any age. Most fostering services take the same approach, so if your plan relies on your own child moving in with a sibling or with a foster child, raise it early.

What often does work is reorganising the home so that your own children share with each other and a room becomes free. Whether that is acceptable depends on your children's ages, their own wishes and the service's policy, and your assessor will want to hear how your children feel about it. Fostering affects the whole household, and services pay close attention to the needs of carers' own children.

What counts as a suitable bedroom

There is no national minimum size. Foster Wales says there is no legal requirement for a foster care bedroom to be a certain size; the room must be big enough for a bed and somewhere to store clothes and belongings, and it must have a window. It says a box room might be fine for a child under 12, which matters for many families with a small third bedroom.

Some spaces are not suitable. Foster Wales says a conservatory, or a sofa bed in a shared living area, is not a suitable bedroom for a child, and recommends that young children sleep on the same floor as the foster carer. Hertfordshire's policy also says children under six should not sleep on a top bunk.

A room that is currently a study, a junk room or a guest room is fine as long as it can be made into a proper bedroom by the time a child arrives. The assessor will want to see it during the home visits, so ask your service what it expects the room to have, and by when.

The bedroom is also part of the health and safety check that every service carries out before approval and again at least once a year. Expect questions about windows, heating, smoke alarms and anything in the home that could be a hazard for the age of child you hope to foster.

If you do not have a spare room yet

You can still make an enquiry. It is worth talking to a service if you are planning a move, a loft conversion or a change in the household, such as an adult child leaving home. Without a suitable room most services will not start a full assessment, but an early conversation can tell you what they would accept and how long the process takes, so you can plan.

Some practical routes people take:

  • Ask about fostering babies or very young children, if the service has a need for this and its policy allows a cot in your room. Be aware that a spare room is often still expected for approval.
  • Ask about short breaks, where a room is needed only on the nights a child stays.
  • Plan a move first. If you rent from a council or housing association, ask whether it can give priority to people who need a larger home to foster. Our guide to fostering when you rent covers this, and landlord permission, in detail.
  • Ask the service whether it has any scheme to help with adaptations or extensions; some do, and the Fostering Network's 2019 comments suggested this as one way forward.

Benefits, the bedroom tax and the spare room

If you rent and receive help with housing costs, you may worry that a spare bedroom kept for fostering will be treated as an under-occupied room. The rules recognise foster carers. The government's Housing Benefit factsheet on the social sector size criteria, issued in 2013 when the rules began, said an additional bedroom is allowed for a foster carer, including someone approved who has no child living with them, as long as the period without a child does not exceed 52 weeks. Carers UK's current guidance says approved foster carers are allowed an additional room even between placements, provided they have fostered a child, or been approved, within the last 12 months.

These rules differ between Housing Benefit and Universal Credit and depend on your household, so do not rely on this summary for your own claim. Your council's benefits team, Citizens Advice, Carers UK or a benefits adviser your fostering service can introduce you to can check how they apply to you.

Across the UK

England
GOV.UK says you will usually need a spare bedroom. National Minimum Standard 10.6 (2011) says each child over three should have their own bedroom; sharing needs the agreement of each child's responsible authority, a written risk assessment and each child's own area in the room. Councils and agencies set their own policies on babies and siblings.
Scotland
mygov.scot (updated 22 October 2026) lists a spare bedroom as a requirement, noting that some councils may make exceptions depending on the child's age. Ask the council or agency you apply to how it applies this.
Wales
Foster Wales says you must have a spare, suitable bedroom to be approved. There is no legal minimum size, a box room may suit a child under 12, siblings may sometimes share at the local authority's discretion, and a foster child cannot share with your own child.
Northern Ireland
nidirect's guidance for prospective carers does not set out a bedroom rule. The Department of Health consulted on new draft minimum standards for fostering services in June 2025. Ask your Health and Social Care Trust or an independent agency what it requires.

Common questions

Do I need a spare bedroom to foster in the UK?

Usually, yes. Official guidance in England, Scotland and Wales expects a spare bedroom for most fostering, with some exceptions for babies, siblings and short-break care that depend on the fostering service.

Can a foster baby sleep in my room?

Often, for a time. Many services allow a baby to sleep in a cot in the carer's room, with limits ranging from under six months to around three years in published policies. A spare room may still be required for approval, so ask your service.

Can a foster child share a bedroom with my child?

Generally not. Foster Wales says they cannot, and other services' policies say it is not good practice. Your own children sharing with each other to free up a room is a different question your service can advise on.

How big does the bedroom need to be?

There is no national minimum size. Foster Wales says the room must fit a bed and storage and have a window, and that a box room might be fine for a child under 12. A conservatory or a sofa bed in a living room is not suitable.

Can siblings share a room in foster care?

Sometimes. In England sharing must be agreed by each child's responsible authority after a risk assessment. Services often allow young siblings, or same-sex siblings, to share, and expect older children to have their own room.

Will a spare room for fostering affect my Housing Benefit or Universal Credit?

Approved foster carers are normally allowed an extra bedroom, including for a limited time between placements. The details depend on your claim, so check with your council's benefits team or an adviser such as Citizens Advice or Carers UK.
