What is foster to adopt? Fostering for adoption and early permanence explained

How foster to adopt works in each UK nation, who the carers are, what happens if the child goes home, contact with birth parents, and the money and leave involved.

The short answer

Foster to adopt is a way of placing a baby or young child in care with the people who may go on to adopt them, before the court has decided whether the child can return to their birth family. The carers foster the child while the court case runs. If the court decides the child cannot go home and adoption is the plan, the child stays and the carers can then adopt them. If the court decides the child should go home or live with relatives, the carers help the child move.

It is a different route from ordinary fostering. The carers are prospective adopters who are also approved, or temporarily approved, as foster carers, and the placement is usually arranged by an adoption agency or a council's adoption service rather than a fostering agency. You will also see it called fostering for adoption, concurrent planning, concurrency or early permanence. Coram, the charity that began a concurrency programme in 1999, says it now tends to use the umbrella term early permanence because the point of all of these approaches is to avoid delay for the child.

This guide explains how it works, what it asks of carers, and how it differs between England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland. It is general information: your local adoption service, or the council looking after the child, is the place to confirm how its own scheme works.

Fostering for adoption and concurrent planning: the two main models

Early permanence covers two main models, and the difference matters to anyone thinking about it.

Fostering for adoption is used when the council is already considering adoption, or is satisfied the child ought to be placed for adoption but does not yet have the legal authority to place them. The carers are approved prospective adopters, and they may be approved as foster carers on a temporary basis for that one child.

Concurrent planning, or concurrency, starts earlier, often when a baby first comes into care. There are two plans at the same time: plan A is for the child to return to their birth family, and plan B is adoption by the same carers. The carers are approved both as adopters and as foster carers, and during the court case they support the work to return the child home, including regular contact with the birth parents.

In both models the carers do not hold parental responsibility for the child while they are fostering, and they are not a party to the care proceedings. The court makes the final decision about the child's future, and the council looking after the child keeps the legal responsibilities it has for any child in its care.

Who can do it and how carers are approved

Early permanence carers start out as people who want to adopt. You apply to an adoption agency or a council's adoption service and go through the usual adoption assessment and preparation. If you are interested in early permanence, the agency will assess whether you can manage the uncertainty and the contact involved, and you are then also approved as a foster carer, either through dual approval or, for fostering for adoption in England, on a temporary basis for a particular child.

Luton Borough Council, for example, describes dual approval as one assessment that covers both adoption and fostering, with extra skills to foster training on top. North Wales Adoption Service says its early permanence carers do preparation training for the scheme and then a further foster carer training course.

Most children placed this way are babies or very young children. Luton says they are usually under two, and the 2026 evaluation says early permanence is usually used for children under two, although there is no rule stopping it being used for older children.

  • You usually apply to an adoption agency or council adoption service, not a fostering agency.
  • You are assessed and approved as a prospective adopter first.
  • You are then also approved as a foster carer, either through dual approval or temporarily for one child.
  • You take extra training on fostering, contact and working with birth families.
  • The children are usually babies or under two.

Contact with the birth family during the placement

While the court case runs, the child is a looked-after child and the birth parents usually still see them. Early permanence carers bring the child to contact and often meet the birth parents there. North Wales Adoption Service says its carers do what any foster carer does, including supporting the child's contact with their birth family and taking part in looked-after child reviews. In concurrency schemes this contact can be very frequent; one CELCIS article on concurrency describes carers taking the child to contact every week.

Coram says contact during proceedings is a chance for parents to keep their relationship with the child, and that carers and parents getting to know each other can make any later contact after adoption easier. The 2026 evaluation is honest that it is also hard: it found contact could be stressful for carers, who are caring for a child as a parent would while the parent is still in the child's life and the outcome is unknown.

If you are thinking about early permanence, ask the agency how often contact usually happens, where, and what support you will get with it.

What if the child goes home?

This is the question everyone asks, and the honest answer is that it can happen. The whole point of early permanence is that the decision has not been made yet. If the court decides the child should return to a parent or go to live with relatives, the carers help the child make that move, and the agency supports the carers through the loss. Luton's adoption service says its social workers will support you if the child leaves your care.

In practice most children placed this way are adopted by their carers. The 2026 evaluation for Adoption England says most early permanence placements do end in adoption, and cites an estimate that around 4% of children are reunified with their birth families. Coram described the children returned from its own programme as a significant if small number. These are estimates from research, not a promise about any one child, and the agency should talk you through what is known about the child before a placement is agreed.

The risk is carried by the adults so that the child does not have to move between carers. If the child is adopted, they have been with the same family since they were very young; if they go home, they have had one stable, caring placement in the meantime.

Money, allowances and adoption leave

Because early permanence carers are fostering until an adoption placement is made, they are usually paid the fostering allowance for the child during that time. Luton, for example, says you receive a weekly fostering allowance while you are fostering the child, which stops if you adopt them. Each council sets its own arrangements, so check with the service you apply to.

Employed early permanence carers can usually take adoption leave and pay. GOV.UK says that if you are eligible, adoption pay and leave in a fostering for adoption placement start from when the child comes to live with you. HMRC's guidance says that since 5 April 2015 an employee approved as both a foster parent and a prospective adopter who has been notified of a match can get Statutory Adoption Pay, subject to the usual qualifying conditions, and that a partner may be able to get paternity pay. Acas says statutory adoption leave is up to 52 weeks and statutory adoption pay up to 39 weeks.

Ordinary foster carers are not entitled to adoption leave just because they foster. The qualifying conditions, such as length of employment and earnings, depend on your circumstances, so your employer, GOV.UK or Acas can confirm what applies to you.

How common is it?

Early permanence has grown in England over the last few years but is still a minority of adoptions. The April 2026 evaluation for Adoption England, citing Coram's figures, says early permanence placements rose from 14% of adoptions in 2020/21 to 23% in 2023/24, then dropped to 17% in 2024/25. It also found significant variation between areas, with 16% in London in 2024/25 against 25% in the South East.

So whether early permanence is offered, and how often, depends a great deal on where you live and which adoption agency you approach. Regional adoption agencies in England, the regional adoption services in Wales and the councils and voluntary agencies in Scotland and Northern Ireland each run their own schemes.

Is it right for you?

Early permanence suits people whose main aim is to adopt and who can live with not knowing the outcome for months. It asks a lot emotionally: you care for a baby as your own, support their relationship with their parents, and may have to say goodbye. In return, the child is spared extra moves at the start of their life, and you are there from the beginning.

If what you want is to look after children for as long as they need while their plans are worked out, ordinary fostering, including short-term or long-term fostering, may suit you better, and you would apply to a fostering agency or a council fostering team. Our guide on long-term fostering and adoption sets out how the two differ. If you are drawn to early permanence, contact your local adoption agency and ask whether it runs a scheme, how many placements it makes, and what support it offers its carers.

  • Ask the adoption agency whether it offers fostering for adoption, concurrency or both.
  • Ask how contact usually works and what support you will have.
  • Ask what allowance is paid while you are fostering, and check your adoption leave with your employer.
  • Ask how the agency would support you if the child returned home.

Across the UK

England
Since the Children and Families Act 2014, a council in England that is considering adoption for a child in its care, or is satisfied the child ought to be placed for adoption, must consider placing them with foster parents who are also approved prospective adopters, after first considering relatives, friends and other connected people (Children Act 1989, section 22C(9A), and regulation 22A of the Care Planning, Placement and Case Review (England) Regulations 2010). Schemes are run by the regional adoption agencies and councils.
Scotland
mygov.scot describes concurrent planning, where carers look after a child while it is decided whether the child can return to their family, and can apply to adopt the child if they cannot. To do this you must be approved both to adopt and to foster in Scotland, and the child may see their birth parents during this time. Ask your council or an adoption agency whether it runs a scheme.
Wales
In Wales the approach is called Welsh Early Permanence. North Wales Adoption Service, for example, places children with approved prospective adopters at the start of court proceedings; the carers foster the child and support contact, help the child move if the plan becomes a return to their family, and adopt the child if the plan is adoption.
Northern Ireland
The Adoption and Children Act (Northern Ireland) 2022 introduces a duty on an authority to consider placing a child with dually approved carers, meaning approved foster parents who are also approved prospective adopters, when adoption is being considered or has been decided. Earlier, Northern Ireland's health and social care trusts ran a concurrent planning scheme called Home on Time. Ask your local trust's adoption service how it works today.

Common questions

Is foster to adopt the same as adopting a child you already foster?

No. Foster to adopt is planned from the start: the carers are prospective adopters who foster the child while the court decides. Ordinary foster carers can sometimes apply to adopt a child they foster, but that is a separate process, and HMRC's guidance says foster carers who apply directly to court to adopt a child they already foster are not eligible for Statutory Adoption Pay.

Do foster to adopt carers get paid?

Usually you receive the fostering allowance for the child while you are fostering, and it stops once you adopt. Councils set their own arrangements, so ask the service you apply to.

Can I take adoption leave for a foster to adopt placement?

Usually yes, if you meet the conditions. GOV.UK says adoption pay and leave in a fostering for adoption placement start when the child comes to live with you, and Acas says adoption leave is up to 52 weeks. Your employer, GOV.UK or Acas can confirm what applies to you.

How likely is it that the child will go home?

Most early permanence placements end in adoption. Research cited in the April 2026 evaluation for Adoption England estimates that around 4% of children are reunified with their birth families, but every case is different and the court makes the decision.

Do I apply to a fostering agency or an adoption agency?

Usually an adoption agency or a council's adoption service, because you are assessed as a prospective adopter first and then approved to foster as well. Fostering agencies recruit carers for ordinary fostering.
