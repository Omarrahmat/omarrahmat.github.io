What is therapeutic or specialist fostering, and could you do it?

Therapeutic and specialist foster carers look after children whose early experiences have left them with complex emotional, behavioural or mental health needs. Here is what the role involves, who it is for, the extra support and higher fees services offer, and how it works in England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland.

The short answer

Therapeutic or specialist fostering is foster care for children and young people whose needs are more complex than most, usually because of trauma, neglect or abuse earlier in their lives, or because previous homes have broken down. The carer's job is still to provide a family home, but the way they parent is shaped around the child's emotional needs, and they work closely with a team of professionals such as therapists, psychologists and specialist social workers.

The UK government's guide to becoming a foster carer in England lists specialist therapeutic fostering as one of the main types of foster care. It describes it as providing specialist therapeutic care to children and young people with complex needs or challenging behaviour, and says it is for experienced foster carers or those with certain skills. nidirect, the Northern Ireland government website, describes it in almost the same terms: care for children and young people with very complex needs and/or challenging behaviour.

Different services use different names for the same broad idea. You will see therapeutic fostering, specialist fostering, intensive fostering, enhanced fostering and professional fostering. Each scheme sets its own rules, so the details below are examples from real services rather than a single national standard.

Which children need therapeutic or specialist foster care

Foster Wales, the national network of the 22 council fostering services in Wales, describes therapeutic foster care as a way of parenting that supports the complex emotional needs of children and young people who have been through traumatic experiences such as neglect or abuse. Councils' own scheme pages describe the children in more detail. Common threads include:

  • Children who have experienced significant early adversity and disrupted attachments, such as those Barnsley Council's specialist scheme is designed for.
  • Children who might otherwise live in a residential children's home, or who are moving out of one. Herefordshire Council says its specialist carers look after children aged 5 to 18 who might otherwise be in residential care, and Barnsley's scheme is often for children stepping down from residential care.
  • Children with social, emotional and mental health needs, which Bristol City Council says can show up as repeated moves between carers, difficulties at school, aggression, substance misuse, risk of exploitation or self-harm.
  • Children whose previous foster homes have broken down, where a stable, specialist placement is seen as the best chance of staying put.
  • Children and young people of all ages with complex needs or very challenging behaviour, which is how The Highland Council describes the young people in its Intensive Fostering Scheme.

What therapeutic parenting means in practice

Therapeutic fostering is not therapy delivered by the carer. The therapy, where it is needed, comes from qualified professionals. What changes is the everyday parenting: carers learn to understand behaviour as a response to past harm, to stay calm and consistent when a child is distressed, and to build trust slowly. Foster Wales Carmarthenshire, for example, says it asks carers to use the PACE approach, which stands for Playfulness, Acceptance, Curiosity and Empathy, and that carers attend sessions to understand each child's history of trauma.

Services also use named approaches in the support they give. Foster Wales Carmarthenshire mentions PACE and Theraplay, and offers time-limited therapeutic support through an emotional health team of therapists and psychologists, with individual sessions for carers and joint sessions for carer and child. Foster Wales Merthyr Tydfil created a therapeutic practitioner post, which it says was the first in a Welsh council fostering service, offering approaches such as cognitive behavioural therapy, dialectical behaviour therapy and trauma-informed practice to children and carers, with two support workers alongside.

Day to day, this can mean more meetings, more recording, more contact with school and health professionals, and more reflection on how things are going, as well as the ordinary work of cooking, school runs, routines and fun.

What services ask of specialist carers

Every specialist carer must first be assessed and approved as a foster carer in the usual way. On top of that, each scheme sets its own extra requirements. Experience matters most, but it does not always have to come from fostering. Barnsley Council welcomes applications from experienced foster carers, kinship carers, residential childcare workers, and people with backgrounds in education, special educational needs, mental health, health, the emergency services or youth work. These are examples of what councils ask for on their own pages:

  • Availability: Barnsley asks carers to be available full time and to offer a solo placement (one child only). Highland says the main carer must not have another job.
  • Other children at home: Barnsley asks for no other children under 16 in the home; Bristol asks for no children under 15 living at home; Highland says other children in the home are usually at least 12.
  • Age and experience: Bristol asks for carers over 25 with experience of looking after children with social, emotional and mental health needs. Herefordshire asks for significant experience of managing complex and challenging behaviour, an understanding of attachment and child development, and the time to commit intensively.
  • Qualifications: Highland asks for a childcare qualification at about HNC or SVQ Level 3, recent childcare training, significant relevant experience, or a willingness to study for a similar qualification within a year of approval.
  • A spare room: Highland lists a spare room as a requirement, and Gloucestershire says the majority of fostering requires one.
  • Ongoing learning: Herefordshire asks for a commitment to ongoing specialist training, and Barnsley provides trauma-informed and therapeutic parenting training.

The support you can expect

Because the children's needs are greater, specialist schemes usually wrap more support around the carer than standard fostering. The details vary, so ask each service exactly what it provides. Examples from councils' own pages:

  • Bristol City Council's Intensive Therapeutic Support Carer role comes with a multidisciplinary team that phones daily, carer-to-carer out-of-hours support, weekly foster carer meetings, regular one-to-one sessions for the carer, joint child and carer sessions, skills coaching for the child, and support from an education worker.
  • Barnsley Council offers reflective supervision with a dedicated supervising social worker, more contact early in a placement, specialist therapeutic input, 24/7 out-of-hours advice and emergency support, and access to a Mockingbird constellation (a support network of fostering households), including planned overnight breaks where needed.
  • Herefordshire Council provides support through a psychology service, including regular therapeutic support, specialist training, out-of-hours support, extra social worker visits, and supervision and guidance from therapists and educational psychologists.
  • The Highland Council offers up to five weeks of paid respite a year, training before approval and ongoing training afterwards, and possible extra training in its local residential units.

How specialist foster carers are paid

Specialist carers are usually paid more than standard foster carers, because the role asks for more skill and more time. Pay normally has two parts: an allowance to cover the cost of looking after the child, and a fee that recognises the carer's skills and time. nidirect explains that in Northern Ireland fee paid foster carers, who are mainly specialist therapeutic carers, receive a fee in recognition of the skills and knowledge they provide, in addition to fostering allowances.

Some councils publish their specialist fees. As shown on their own pages in November 2026 (check the current figure with the council, as rates change):

  • Barnsley Council (a pilot scheme): an enhanced professional fostering fee of £1,300 per week, plus an age-related allowance for the child, retainer payments of £650 during agreed vacant periods for up to two months, and extra payments for mileage, equipment and holidays.
  • Herefordshire Council: £1,000 per week, per child, plus a child's allowance of up to £271 per week depending on the child's age.
  • Bristol City Council: an enhanced fee of £650 per week for its Intensive Therapeutic Support Carers.
  • The Highland Council: a higher fee for intensive foster carers; the amount is not given on its scheme page.

Is it right for you?

Therapeutic and specialist fostering suits people who already have experience of children with high needs, whether through fostering, residential care, teaching, youth work, health or social work, and who can give the role a great deal of time. Most schemes expect at least one carer to be at home and available, and many prefer, or require, that the specialist child is the only foster child in the house. If you have young children of your own, many schemes will not be a fit yet.

If you are new to fostering, it is common to start in mainstream fostering and move into a specialist role once you have experience and training. Some councils, such as Barnsley, say they welcome people from professional backgrounds who have not fostered before, so it is worth asking.

Independent fostering agencies may also offer therapeutic or specialist roles. If you are comparing services, ask each one: which children the scheme is for, what support is available out of hours, whether therapy is provided by the service or arranged through the NHS, what training you will get and when, what happens if a placement is struggling, how fees and retainers work between placements, and whether you can speak to a current specialist carer.

Across the UK

England
The government's guide to becoming a foster carer in England lists specialist therapeutic fostering as a type of foster care for experienced carers or those with certain skills. There is no national specialist fee: each council and independent agency sets its own scheme, requirements and pay, as the Barnsley, Bristol and Herefordshire examples show.
Scotland
Scottish councils run their own specialist and intensive schemes; The Highland Council's Intensive Fostering Scheme is one example, with a higher fee, paid respite and qualification expectations. The Scottish Government says all councils must pay at least the Scottish Recommended Allowance for the child's care, introduced in August 2023; fees for specialist carers are set locally on top of that.
Wales
Foster Wales lists therapeutic foster care among the specialist kinds of fostering that have a specific type of approval, supported by a trauma-aware team around the child and carer. Councils across Wales are building therapeutic support, such as Merthyr Tydfil's therapeutic practitioner and Carmarthenshire's emotional health team.
Northern Ireland
nidirect lists specialist therapeutic fostering for children with very complex needs and/or challenging behaviour, and explains that fee paid foster carers, who are mainly specialist therapeutic carers, receive a fee for their skills and knowledge on top of fostering allowances.

Common questions

Do you need qualifications to be a therapeutic foster carer?

Not always. Some schemes ask for a childcare qualification or a willingness to gain one: The Highland Council, for example, asks for a qualification at about HNC or SVQ Level 3, recent training, significant experience, or a commitment to study within a year. Others focus on experience and provide the training themselves. Ask each service what it requires.

Can I do specialist fostering as a first-time foster carer?

Many schemes want previous experience of children with complex needs, but this can come from work as well as fostering. Barnsley Council, for instance, welcomes residential childcare workers and people from education, mental health, health and youth work backgrounds. Others will expect you to foster in a mainstream role first.

How much do specialist foster carers earn?

It depends entirely on the service. Councils' own pages in November 2026 showed £650 a week in Bristol, £1,000 a week per child plus an allowance in Herefordshire, and £1,300 a week plus an allowance in Barnsley's pilot scheme. Always check the current rate with the service, and ask what is paid between placements.

Can I work while being a therapeutic foster carer?

Usually not full time. Specialist schemes often ask for at least one carer to be available full time; Barnsley asks carers to be available full time and The Highland Council says the main carer must not have another job.

Is therapeutic fostering the same as being a therapist?

No. Therapeutic foster carers provide a stable, trauma-aware family home. Formal therapy for the child comes from qualified professionals, such as psychologists or therapists working with the fostering service or the NHS. Carers are trained and supported to parent in a way that helps that work.

Do I need a spare bedroom for specialist fostering?

Usually. Gloucestershire County Council, for example, says the majority of fostering requires a spare room for the child, and specialist schemes such as The Highland Council's list a spare room as a requirement.
