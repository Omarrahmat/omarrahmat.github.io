The initial home visit for fostering: what to expect

After you first get in touch with a fostering service, someone from the team usually comes to your home for an informal visit. Here is what that visit is for, what you are likely to talk about, what they look at, what happens next, and how it works in England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland.

The short answer

The initial home visit is usually the first proper meeting between you and a fostering service. It comes after your first enquiry or phone call and before any formal application, training or assessment. A member of the fostering team comes to your home, talks with you about your life and why you are interested in fostering, answers your questions and, in many services, has a look around your home.

It is not the assessment, and it is not a test you can pass or fail on the day. Surrey County Council describes its initial home visit as informal and informative, with no obligation to go any further. Most services use the visit to help both sides decide whether it makes sense for you to apply now, apply later, or not apply at all.

Every fostering service runs its own process, so the exact order of steps, who visits and what forms follow will differ. The examples in this guide come from councils' and governments' own published pages; your service will tell you how it does things.

Where the visit fits in the process

Published council timelines put the visit near the very start. Oxfordshire County Council lists a phone chat first, then an expression of interest, then a first home visit from one of its recruitment team, and only after that the application, checks and assessment. Staffordshire's applicant timeline has the same shape: an enquiry, then an initial home visit once you decide you would like to proceed, then a detailed information form sent by email afterwards.

Foster Wales in Newport says it will arrange to visit you at home after your initial talk, or set up a virtual meeting if a home visit is not possible. In Scotland, the Scottish Government's foster care campaign says that after you call Fosterline Scotland, someone from your council's fostering team will get in touch for an initial chat and to organise a home visit, with preparation training following if you still want to apply.

How quickly the visit happens depends on the service. Lincolnshire County Council's published procedures, for example, say its home visit to applicants will be made within 10 days. Many services do not publish a timescale, so it is reasonable to ask when you make your enquiry.

Who comes to the visit

The person who visits varies. Surrey says a social worker will visit your home. Oxfordshire says it is one of its recruitment team. Lincolnshire's procedures say the visit is made by a supervising social worker. East Sussex simply says a member of the team will come and visit you at home to discuss your application in more depth.

The visitor is not necessarily the social worker who will later carry out your full assessment. Oxfordshire, for example, assigns a social worker for the assessment at the next stage, after the first visit. If you are in a couple, or other adults live with you, it is sensible to ask whether the service would like everyone to be there, because fostering affects the whole household and later stages involve every adult in the home.

You can expect the visitor to confirm who you are. Lincolnshire's procedures say proof of identity must be verified at the visit. Bring the identification the service asks for when it books the appointment.

What you are likely to talk about

The conversation is two-way: the service wants to get to know you, and you get the chance to find out what fostering with that service really involves. Lincolnshire describes the purpose of the visit as giving further information, answering questions and exploring your potential to foster. Oxfordshire says it is a chance to tell the service about your life experiences and your expectations.

Published council pages mention a fairly consistent set of topics. East Sussex lists your skills and experience, your finances, your knowledge and understanding of fostering, and your reasons for wanting to foster. Surrey mentions your environment, experiences and motivations, family dynamics, daily routines and support systems, and reflecting on how ready you are. Between them, you can expect questions along these lines:

  • why you want to foster, and why now
  • who lives in your home, and the family and friends you can rely on for support
  • your experience of caring for children, whether as a parent, relative, volunteer or at work
  • your work pattern and daily routine, and how fostering would fit around them
  • your finances in general terms, and how you would manage alongside the fostering allowance
  • what you already know about fostering, and which types of fostering interest you
  • anything that might affect your application, such as health, past convictions or your housing, so the service can tell you early how it would be looked at

Will they look around your home?

Often, yes, but at this stage it is usually a first look rather than a formal inspection. East Sussex says it will look around your home and discuss with you any issues around your suitability to foster. Surrey's page describes the full check later on: in the assessment stage, your home will be assessed for safety and suitability.

GOV.UK says that during the first stage of the formal assessment in England the social worker asks about the property you live in and any pets you have. Lincolnshire's procedures say that during the second stage of the assessment the home will be thoroughly checked. So if something about your home needs to change, such as making a room available for a child, you will normally hear about it early and have time to sort it out before the detailed checks.

There is no need to redecorate or deep-clean before the visit. The service wants to see the home you live in, understand the space, and talk honestly about whether it would work for a child.

Questions worth asking at the visit

The initial visit is your best chance to find out whether a particular service is right for you before you commit time to an application. The visitor will expect questions, and asking good ones shows you are taking the decision seriously. Useful questions include:

  • what types of fostering the service most needs carers for, and for which age groups
  • how long its assessment usually takes, and what the next steps are after today
  • what preparation training it runs, and when the next course is
  • what support you would get once approved, including your supervising social worker, out-of-hours help and support groups
  • what it pays in fees and allowances, and how payments work
  • whether there is anything in your circumstances that it would want to look at more closely

What happens after the visit

If you and the service agree to go ahead, the next step is usually paperwork. East Sussex says it will send you documents to complete for its Stage 1 checks and references, along with the report template used for assessments so you can see what you will talk through. Staffordshire sends an email after the visit with a link to complete a detailed information form online. Lincolnshire's procedures say the social worker assesses whether the application should be pursued, a decision that a team manager or practice supervisor must approve, and then sends a notification to proceed.

After that comes the formal application and assessment. In England, GOV.UK describes two stages, which may be done separately or at the same time: practical information such as your property, health, household and at least two references, then more detailed information about you, your background and your skills. Oxfordshire says its assessment can take between 4 and 6 months, with regular visits from your social worker, checks including the Disclosure and Barring Service, a GP medical and six written references. Surrey mentions a mandatory three-day Skills to Foster course and six to eight visits from a social worker.

If the service decides not to take your enquiry further, or you decide it is not right for you at the moment, that is the end of it for now. You can usually withdraw at any point; nidirect says this directly for Northern Ireland. Some people come back to fostering a few years later when their circumstances change, and some apply to a different service.

How to prepare

You do not need to prepare much, but a little thought beforehand helps the conversation. Talk to the people you live with about why you are interested and what worries you. Think about the support network you could call on. Jot down your questions so you do not forget them. If there is anything you are unsure about, such as a past conviction, a health condition or a rented home, be open about it at the visit: the service can tell you how it would be looked at, and honesty early on avoids problems later in the assessment.

Every fostering service decides for itself how it runs the visit and what happens next, and only the service can tell you whether your circumstances fit its requirements. If you are thinking of fostering, the most useful next step is to contact a service near you and ask to book an initial visit.

Across the UK

England
In England each council and independent fostering agency sets its own first steps. GOV.UK says services may visit you at home, call you or invite you to meetings, and that the formal assessment by a social worker has two stages. Councils such as Oxfordshire, Staffordshire, Surrey and East Sussex all publish an initial home visit as an early step.
Scotland
The Scottish Government's foster care campaign says you can start by calling Fosterline Scotland; your council's fostering team then gets in touch for an initial chat and to organise a home visit. Preparation training follows, then a home study, where the team visits your home to learn about you, a PVG check, medical history and references, and a fostering panel. It says approval can take 6 months to a year.
Wales
In Wales, council fostering teams recruit under the Foster Wales name. Foster Wales Newport, for example, says it will arrange a home visit after your initial talk, or a virtual meeting if a home visit is not possible, followed by preparation training, an assessment with a qualified social worker, a panel and a foster care agreement.
Northern Ireland
nidirect says to contact your local fostering service, either an HSC Trust or an independent agency, which will explain what is involved and help you decide whether fostering is right for you and your family. After some training you complete an application, a social worker assesses you and your family, and checks include an Access NI check, a social services check, a medical and references.

Common questions

Is the initial home visit part of the fostering assessment?

Not usually. It comes before the formal application and assessment. Surrey County Council describes its initial visit as informal and informative with no obligation to proceed. The assessment, with checks, references and several visits from a social worker, follows if you and the service decide to go ahead.

Can I fail the initial home visit?

It is not a pass or fail test. It is a conversation to help you and the service decide whether to move on to an application. A service may suggest waiting, or decide not to take your application forward; you can ask what would need to change.

Do I need a spare bedroom before the initial visit?

The visit is a good time to talk about your space. Some services look around your home at this stage; East Sussex says it will. The full check of your home comes later in the assessment, so if a room needs to be made available you will usually know early. Requirements are set by each service, so ask them.

Can the first visit be done online?

Some services offer it. Foster Wales Newport says it will set up a virtual meeting if a home visit is not possible, and GOV.UK says services may call you or invite you to meetings as well as visit you at home. Assessments still involve visits to your home: the Scottish Government describes a home study, and Oxfordshire says your social worker will visit regularly.

What happens after the initial visit?

If you decide to go ahead, the service usually sends forms to complete, then starts checks, references and preparation training, and assigns a social worker for your assessment. The assessment ends with a fostering panel. Oxfordshire says its assessment can take 4 to 6 months; the Scottish Government says approval can take 6 months to a year.
