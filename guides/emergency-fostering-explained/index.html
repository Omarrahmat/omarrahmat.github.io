What is emergency fostering, and could you do it?

Emergency foster carers take in a child at very short notice, often in the evening, overnight or at a weekend, and look after them for a few nights or a couple of weeks while longer-term plans are made. Here is how it works, what services ask of emergency carers, how they are paid and supported, and how the rules differ across the UK.

The short answer

Emergency fostering is care for a child who needs somewhere safe to stay straight away, because something unexpected has happened at home. The Fostering Network, the UK's largest fostering charity, describes it as unplanned and usually at short notice, and says emergency carers need to be ready to take a child into their home at any time of the day or night, usually for a few days.

Official definitions across the UK say much the same. mygov.scot says emergency foster care is when a child is placed with you at short notice. nidirect, the Northern Ireland government site, describes emergency carers as people who provide time-limited placements at short notice to children who need somewhere safe to stay, and who can be contacted at any time, including weekends.

It is one of the most demanding types of fostering, but also one of the most immediately useful. An emergency carer is often the first safe, calm adult a child meets after a crisis. Some services, such as Hertfordshire County Council, look for emergency carers with experience of supporting children in crisis, and several councils pay emergency carers more than their standard rates in recognition of the commitment.

How much notice you get

Very little. Foster Wales, the national network of council fostering services in Wales, says the call could come within the hour, the same evening or early the next day, and one carer it spoke to had 45 minutes' notice before a child arrived with a social worker. Surrey County Council says children may be placed in emergency care with little or no notice.

Many emergency calls come outside office hours, when councils' out-of-hours or emergency duty teams are dealing with a crisis. Gloucestershire County Council says its emergency arrangements are often needed outside normal working hours, and sets out its out-of-hours cover as 5pm to 9am on weekdays and from 5pm on Friday to 9am on Monday at weekends. Hillingdon Council says its emergency carers must be easily contactable around the clock. Warwickshire County Council says referrals can come in the evening, overnight, at weekends and on bank holidays.

A child arriving in an emergency may have very few belongings, may not have eaten, and may be frightened or in shock. You will usually be told only what the social worker knows at the time, and more information follows over the next few days.

How long an emergency placement lasts

Emergency placements are meant to be short, but each service sets its own expected length, so the figures vary. These are examples taken from councils' own pages:

  • Gloucestershire: typically 72 hours, though carers may be asked to extend.
  • Warwickshire (its Emergency Duty Plus scheme): usually up to three nights and four days.
  • Liverpool: typically up to 7 days, sometimes extended for a limited period.
  • Surrey: a few nights up to about 10 days.
  • Plymouth: up to two weeks.
  • Lambeth: no longer than 14 calendar days at most.
  • Hertfordshire: about 10 to 15 working days, or up to 4 weeks for younger children, according to its emergency foster carers policy.

What happens after the emergency

While the child is with you, social workers assess what has happened and plan the next step. The child might go home, move to relatives or friends, or move on to a short-term or longer-term foster carer. Sometimes the child stays with the emergency carer for longer, if that is right for the child and the carer is approved for it, and Foster Wales notes that longer stays can happen when social workers arrange them.

In England there is a specific legal rule for emergencies. Regulation 23 of the Care Planning, Placement and Case Review (England) Regulations 2010 lets a council place a child in an emergency with any of its approved foster carers even if the placement falls outside the carer's terms of approval (for example, the child is older than the carer is approved for), but only for up to six working days. After that the placement must end unless the carer's terms of approval have been changed. Wales has the same six-working-day rule in regulation 24 of the Care Planning, Placement and Case Review (Wales) Regulations 2015.

This matters if you are a short-term carer who is occasionally asked to help in a crisis: you can say yes to a one-off emergency, and you can also say no if it would not work for your household.

What services look for in an emergency carer

Requirements are set by each fostering service on top of the usual checks that every foster carer goes through. Common requirements on councils' emergency fostering pages include:

  • A spare bedroom kept free for an emergency. Plymouth City Council says you need a spare bedroom available for a child at all times, and Warwickshire and Liverpool also ask for one.
  • Availability. Gloucestershire asks carers to commit in advance, telling it each Friday which days they are available the following week. Hertfordshire's policy says its emergency carers must be fully available throughout the week, without other employment.
  • Transport. Warwickshire asks that emergency carers can take children to school, meetings, health appointments and family time.
  • A calm, flexible temperament. Gloucestershire says the role suits carers who are calm, adaptable and ready to respond quickly; Warwickshire asks for people who are calm under pressure.
  • Age. Plymouth says its emergency carers must be over 21.
  • Experience. Hertfordshire looks for experience of supporting children in crisis, and resilience and trauma-informed practice.

Rotas, rest and time off

Because nobody can be on call every night of the year, many councils run emergency fostering as a rota. Surrey says its emergency carers provide care on a rota basis. Lambeth runs an Emergency Duty Team rota and asks for at least 28 days' notice if a carer wants to leave the scheme.

Warwickshire sets out its pattern in detail: duty windows of three nights and four days, rest periods after each duty window, three weeks on duty followed by five days off, and five days' paid leave a year. Hertfordshire's policy gives carers three days' paid time off after a child moves on, and an extra day a week when they extend a placement.

If you are thinking about emergency fostering, ask any service you talk to how its rota works, how often you would be on call, and what happens if you are unwell or have a family commitment on a duty night.

How emergency foster carers are paid

Pay is set by each fostering service, and emergency carers are often paid more than standard rates because they keep a bed free and are on call. Arrangements usually combine three things: a retainer for being available, the normal weekly fostering allowance for each child placed, and sometimes an extra payment per placement or per night. Examples from councils' own pages (check the current figures with the council, as these can change each April):

  • Lambeth (published August 2024): a £100 a week retainer while on the emergency rota, and an extra £200 a week for each placement made out of office hours, both on top of the full weekly fostering allowance for the child's age.
  • Liverpool: the age-related child allowance and a fostering fee, plus an extra £100 emergency payment per child per night and a set-up grant of up to £500.
  • Gloucestershire: a retainer of £45 a night if you will take children of any age up to 17, or £20 a night if you prefer children up to 9, replaced by the full allowance and fee payments when a child is placed.
  • Hertfordshire: a daily retainer paid whether or not a child is placed, and the standard child's allowance and skills fee when a child arrives.
  • Plymouth: enhanced financial support, plus a disturbance allowance when a child is placed out of hours.

Training and support

Every foster carer gets training during and after assessment, and emergency carers usually get more. Foster Wales says carers' foster care training includes how to handle emergency requests. Surrey offers specialist training in trauma-informed care and crisis response and 24-hour support. Warwickshire lists enhanced training, a dedicated supervising social worker, out-of-hours support from its Emergency Duty Team and family support worker help.

Hertfordshire's policy describes fostering outreach workers who can give one-to-one support to children, available seven days a week. Ask any service you are considering what support you would have at 2am on a Saturday, because that is when you are most likely to need it.

Becoming an emergency carer does not usually need a separate application from becoming a foster carer. You go through the same assessment and fostering panel as any other carer, and the service then agrees with you that emergency care will be part of your approval. Some services, such as Hertfordshire, also have their own selection step for their emergency scheme, which can include a trial period.

Across the UK

England
Councils and independent fostering agencies both recruit emergency carers, and each sets its own rota, retainer and fees. Regulation 23 of the Care Planning, Placement and Case Review (England) Regulations 2010 allows an emergency placement with an approved council foster carer outside their terms of approval for up to six working days.
Scotland
mygov.scot lists emergency foster care as one of the types of foster care, alongside short-term, interim, longer-term and permanent care, and describes it as a child being placed with you at short notice. Your council or agency agrees with you during assessment whether emergency care will be part of what you offer.
Wales
Foster Wales, the network of the 22 council fostering services, recruits emergency carers and says a call can come within the hour. Regulation 24 of the Care Planning, Placement and Case Review (Wales) Regulations 2015 allows an emergency placement with an approved council foster carer outside their terms of approval for up to 6 working days.
Northern Ireland
Fostering is provided by the five Health and Social Care Trusts, supported by HSC NI Adoption and Foster Care, and by independent agencies. HSC NI describes emergency carers as out-of-hours foster carers who give time-limited care at short notice and may be contacted late at night or at a weekend.

Common questions

Can I do emergency fostering as a new foster carer?

It depends on the service. Some, such as Hertfordshire County Council, look for experience of supporting children in crisis, while others offer enhanced training to their emergency carers. Ask the service you apply to what it expects.

Can I work and be an emergency foster carer?

It depends on the service and the scheme. Some rota-based schemes let you choose the days you are available, while Hertfordshire's policy, for example, asks its emergency carers to be fully available without other employment. Ask how the rota works before you apply.

Do I need a spare bedroom for emergency fostering?

Usually yes, and it needs to be kept free so a child can arrive at short notice. Plymouth, Warwickshire and Liverpool all ask emergency carers for a spare bedroom.

Am I paid when no child is staying with me?

Often, yes. Many councils pay emergency carers a retainer for being available, such as Lambeth's weekly retainer or Gloucestershire's and Hertfordshire's nightly or daily retainers. Check the current rates with the service, as they change.

Can a child stay longer than the emergency period?

Sometimes. If it is right for the child and you are approved for it, a placement can be extended or become a short-term placement. In England and Wales, an emergency placement outside your terms of approval can only last six working days unless your approval is changed.
