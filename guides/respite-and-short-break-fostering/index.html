Respite and short break fostering: how part-time fostering works

Respite and short break foster carers look after children for planned stays, often a weekend a month or a week in the holidays, while still working or caring for their own family. Here is what the role involves, who it suits, how approval and payment work, and how the rules differ across the UK.

The short answer

Respite and short break fostering means caring for a child for short, planned periods rather than having them live with you full time. A child might stay for an evening, a night, a weekend, or a week during the school holidays, and then return home. Many carers are linked with the same child for months or years, so the child comes to know them and their home well.

It is often described as part-time fostering, but you are still a fully approved foster carer. You go through the same kind of assessment and panel as any other carer, you get training and a social worker, and you are paid for the care you give. Councils such as East Sussex and East Ayrshire present it as a way to foster if you work full time or do not feel ready to foster full time.

This guide is general information. How often you would care for a child, what you are paid and which children you are matched with are set by each fostering service, so the service you are thinking of joining can confirm what applies to you.

Two kinds of short break care

The words respite and short breaks are used in slightly different ways by different services, but in practice they cover two roles.

The first is supporting other foster carers. A child who lives full time with another foster family comes to you for a planned break, for example one weekend a month, or while their carers attend a family event or go into hospital. East Sussex County Council describes respite as giving parents or other foster carers a break or extra support, and says it can mean regular weekends, a week in the school holidays or an occasional one-off stay. East Ayrshire Council adds that short break carers can also give children who live in children's houses a chance to relax in a family home.

The second is short breaks for disabled children and children with additional needs who live with their own families. Here the child stays with you regularly so that their parents can rest, spend time with their other children or simply catch up on sleep, and the child gets new experiences with people they come to trust. Perth and Kinross Council says it is especially looking for short break carers for families where a child has disabilities, and that carers usually support the same young person over a number of years.

Foster Wales has argued that the word respite gives the wrong impression, because it suggests a child is a burden to be taken away. It prefers short breaks, and also uses terms such as stayovers, sleepovers and weekend fostering. You will see all these names used for similar arrangements.

Who short breaks are for

Short breaks for disabled children are usually arranged through the council's children with disabilities team after an assessment of the child and family. Leeds City Council's guide to its service says it covers children and young people aged 0 to 17 with, for example, physical disabilities or severe sensory impairments, moderate to severe learning disabilities, autism or other neurodiversity that has a significant effect on daily life, or complex health needs. It says breaks can last from a few hours to a weekend or longer and are planned around each child.

Surrey County Council describes its short breaks fostering as being for children aged 0 to 18 with additional needs who are referred by its children with disabilities team, with stays ranging from a few hours to weekends or school holidays. Norfolk County Council says its short break carers often look after the same child regularly and describes the role as a long-term commitment.

The need is real across the UK. In May 2026 the Department of Health in Northern Ireland launched what it called the first regional appeal for short break foster carers for disabled children, saying that 109 children and young people from birth to 17 were waiting to be matched with a short break carer.

How much time it takes

There is no single pattern. Some services ask for a minimum commitment, others build the arrangement around what you can offer. These examples come from the services' own pages:

  • Leicestershire County Council asks short break carers for at least one weekend a month, and describes its minimum as one weekend a month of one or two nights, or up to three weekends a month of one to three nights, with extra time in the summer holidays or daytime care.
  • Northern Ireland's regional appeal asks carers to commit to at least one or two overnight stays a month, adding that many carers do more, fortnightly or weekly.
  • Perth and Kinross Council says arrangements depend on the child and family and can range from care once a month to a week away in the school holidays.
  • Manchester City Council describes its short breaks as flexible: overnight stays or day care at weekends, during the week or in the holidays.
  • East Sussex County Council mentions regular weekends, a week in the school holidays, or an occasional one-off stay.

Can you do it alongside a job?

For many people this is the main attraction. Leicestershire County Council says its short break carers can work full time or part time in other jobs, and East Sussex County Council describes respite as a way of fostering even if you work full time or have other commitments. Northern Ireland's appeal says short breaks can fit around work and family life.

That does not mean there is no commitment. Children who come for short breaks, particularly disabled children, rely on routine and on knowing who will look after them. Services will want to be confident you can keep to the pattern you agree, and some may ask for extra time during school holidays. Before you apply, it helps to be honest with yourself about which weekends and holidays you can reliably keep free. Our guide to fostering and working covers this in more detail for each type of fostering.

Who can become a short break carer

The basic requirements are broadly the same as for any foster carer. You do not need to be a parent, own your home or have fostering experience. Surrey County Council says anyone with a spare room, time and a caring attitude can apply, and that you do not need to be a parent or to have fostered before. Northern Ireland's appeal says applicants can be single, married or living together, with or without children, from any background, and that you do not need to own your home or have particular qualifications. It gives a minimum age of over 21 and no upper age limit, provided you are healthy and have the energy the role needs.

Most services expect you to have a spare bedroom for the child, even though they stay only part of the time. Leicestershire and Northern Ireland both list a spare bedroom as a requirement. Some short break roles for disabled children ask for relevant skills or experience: Leicestershire, for instance, is looking for carers with experience of young people with a range of needs. Others, such as Manchester, say no special qualifications or previous experience are needed because training is provided.

Some services only take short break carers from among their own approved foster carers. Manchester City Council, for example, says you must be an approved Manchester foster carer to give a disabled child a short break. Ask the service how its route works before you apply.

Assessment, training and support

Short break carers are assessed and approved like other foster carers. In England, the Fostering Services (England) Regulations 2011 set out how short breaks for children who are not in the care of a local authority are handled: regulation 42 describes them as a series of short placements with the same foster carer, where no single stay is meant to last more than 17 days, the child goes back to their parent after each stay, and the breaks add up to no more than 75 days in any 12 months. Within those limits some of the usual placement rules are applied in a lighter form.

In practice you can expect the usual checks, references, home visits and a fostering panel. Perth and Kinross Council describes carers being involved in discussions about the children during assessment and after approval by its fostering and permanence panel. Once approved, you should get training and someone to support you. Surrey County Council lists pre-approval training, specialist courses on subjects such as autism and medical needs, e-learning, a dedicated social worker, peer support groups and a 24-hour helpline. Manchester City Council mentions specialist training, help from a social worker, the chance to meet other carers and equipment such as beds, bedding and safety equipment. Perth and Kinross and Leicestershire both say equipment is provided where needed.

How short break carers are paid

Short break carers are paid for the time a child spends with them, usually by the night or by the hour, rather than a weekly amount for a child who lives with you. Rates vary widely between services and many councils do not publish them, so always ask.

Norfolk County Council is one of the few that publishes its figures. Its short breaks page lists £117 per night for overnight care (or £78 per carer when two carers are needed for two-to-one care), £10.20 an hour for daytime-only care up to ten hours a day, and £10.20 an hour for attending child-focused specialist training. Surrey County Council says it pays an age-related allowance for each period of care, from hourly to overnight and multi-day stays. Foster Wales says its part-time carers receive an allowance for each stay to cover things such as food, petrol and days out. Leicestershire describes a remuneration package without giving figures.

Fostering income is normally taxed under qualifying care relief, the special income tax scheme for foster carers, which HMRC's business income manual defines by reference to people approved to provide accommodation and maintenance for a child as foster carers. How it works when you care only for part of the year or part of a week is explained in HMRC's helpsheet HS236 and in our guide to qualifying care relief. If you also claim benefits, short break payments can be treated differently from other income; our guide to fostering and benefits explains where to check. For your own position, speak to HMRC, a tax adviser or a benefits adviser rather than relying on a general guide.

Is it right for you?

Short break fostering suits people who want to make a real difference to children and families but cannot offer a full-time home, or who want to find out what fostering is like before committing to more. Perth and Kinross Council says providing respite can be a good way to start as a carer, and some people go on to foster full time later.

It also asks something particular of you. You will need to welcome a child warmly for short periods, hand them back calmly, and keep the arrangement going over a long time, because the point of a short break is that the child and family can depend on it. If you are drawn to caring for disabled children, look at whether the service offers training in the child's specific needs, such as personal care, communication or medical support, and whether you can offer the kind of home and help the role requires.

A good next step is to read our guides to types of fostering and to fostering disabled children, then ask two or three services in your area how they run short breaks, how often they would need you, and what they pay.

Across the UK

England
Short breaks for children who are not in local authority care are covered by regulation 42 of the Fostering Services (England) Regulations 2011: a series of short stays with the same carer, none intended to last over 17 days and no more than 75 days in total in any 12 months, with the child returning to their parent after each stay. Many councils run short break fostering through their children with disabilities teams, and independent agencies also recruit respite carers.
Scotland
Councils run short breaks foster care themselves and recruit for it directly. East Ayrshire Council describes short break carers looking after children for regular short periods, usually at weekends and in the school holidays, and Perth and Kinross Council approves its short break carers through its fostering and permanence panel.
Wales
Foster Wales, the national network of council fostering services in Wales, prefers the term short breaks to respite and says its part-time carers are fully approved foster carers who get the same local support and an allowance for each stay.
Northern Ireland
Short break foster care is provided through the health and social care trusts and HSC NI Foster Care. The Department of Health's appeal of May 2026 asked for carers aged over 21 who could offer at least one or two overnight stays a month, and said 109 disabled children were waiting to be matched. Enquiries go to HSC NI Foster Care on 0800 0720 137.

Common questions

Is respite fostering the same as short break fostering?

They overlap and many services use the names interchangeably. Respite often means looking after a child from another foster family so their carers can have a break; short breaks often means regular planned stays for disabled children who live with their own families. Foster Wales prefers short breaks for both.

How often would a child stay with me?

It depends on the service and the child. Examples on council pages range from once a month to a week in the school holidays. Leicestershire asks for at least one weekend a month, and Northern Ireland asks for at least one or two overnight stays a month.

Can I do respite fostering if I work full time?

Often yes. Leicestershire County Council says its short break carers can work full or part time, and East Sussex County Council describes respite as a way to foster while working full time. You will need to keep the weekends or holidays you agree free.

Do I need a spare bedroom for short break fostering?

Usually. Leicestershire, Surrey and Northern Ireland all mention a spare room, even though the child stays only part of the time. Ask the service, as expectations can differ.

How much do short break foster carers get paid?

It varies by service and is usually paid per night or per hour. Norfolk County Council publishes £117 a night for overnight care and £10.20 an hour for daytime care. Many services do not publish rates, so ask before you apply.

Can short break fostering lead to full-time fostering?

It can. Perth and Kinross Council says providing respite can be a good way to start as a carer. If you later want to foster full time, talk to your supervising social worker about a change to your approval.
