What is private fostering? The rules on caring for someone else's child

Private fostering is when a child lives with someone who is not a parent or close relative, by private arrangement, for 28 days or more. No fostering service approves the carer and no fostering allowance is paid, but the law still expects the council to be told so that it can check the child is safe and well. Here is what counts, who must tell whom and when, what the council does, and how the rules differ in England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland.

The short answer

Private fostering is an arrangement made between a child's family and another adult, without a council or fostering agency placing the child. In England, the government's guidance describes a privately fostered child as a child under 16 (under 18 if the child is disabled) who is cared for by someone other than a parent or close relative, with the intention that the arrangement should last for 28 days or more. Northern Ireland uses the same age limits, and its official definition refers to more than 28 days.

The person looking after the child is called a private foster carer. They have not been assessed and approved by a fostering service, they do not sit on a fostering panel, and they are not paid a fostering allowance by the council. Money for the child's keep is agreed between the parents and the carer.

What makes private fostering different from simply having a child to stay is the duty to notify. Because nobody has checked the arrangement in advance, the law requires the carer and the parents to tell the local council (in Northern Ireland, the local Health and Social Care Trust). The council then visits, speaks to the child and keeps an eye on how things are going for as long as the arrangement lasts.

What counts as private fostering, and what does not

Three things together make an arrangement private fostering: the child's age, who the carer is, and how long the child is meant to stay. A short holiday with a friend's family is not private fostering. Neither is a child living with a grandparent, because close relatives are left out of the definition.

In England, the guidance names the close relatives who are never private foster carers: a grandparent, brother, sister, uncle or aunt (whether of the full or half blood or by marriage) and a step-parent. That means a cousin, a great-aunt, a family friend, a neighbour, a godparent or the parents of a school friend can all be private foster carers if the other conditions are met. Northern Ireland's list of relatives on nidirect is similar: a parent, grandparent, brother, sister, uncle, aunt, step-parent, or someone with parental responsibility.

The 28 days are about intention as well as fact. In England an arrangement is private fostering if it is meant to last 28 days or more, and the guidance says the period is not broken by an occasional short break. So a child who will live with a friend's family for a school term counts from the day they move in, not from day 28.

  • Usually private fostering: a teenager living with a friend's family for several months while a parent works away or abroad.
  • Usually private fostering: a child living with a family friend or a more distant relative, such as a cousin, after a family breakdown, with no council involvement.
  • Not private fostering: a child living with a grandparent, aunt, uncle, brother, sister or step-parent by family arrangement.
  • Not private fostering: a child placed with you by a council or fostering agency. That is fostering (or kinship fostering if you are family or a friend), with approval, support and an allowance.
  • Not private fostering: a stay that is planned to be shorter than 28 days.

Who has to tell the council, and when

In England the rules are in the Children (Private Arrangements for Fostering) Regulations 2005. Someone who proposes to foster a child privately must tell the council at least six weeks before the arrangement is due to begin, or immediately if it is due to start within six weeks. Anyone involved in making the arrangement, and a parent who knows about it, must also tell the council as soon as possible.

If a child is already living with you privately and nobody has told the council, the regulations say you must do so immediately. Once the arrangement has started, the carer must confirm it within 48 hours. Notices must be in writing and can be sent by post; your council's children's services team can tell you how it prefers to be contacted.

The council will want to know basic details: the child's name, date of birth, religion, cultural and language background, the names and addresses of the carer and the parents, when the arrangement starts and how long it is meant to last. A proposed carer must also say whether they, or anyone in their household, have any convictions or have been disqualified from or prohibited from private fostering.

This matters. The government's guidance for England is plain that failing to notify a private fostering arrangement is an offence for the carer or the parent.

What the council does

The council does not approve or register private foster carers. Instead, the guidance for England says the council has a duty to satisfy itself that the welfare of privately fostered children in its area is being safeguarded and promoted. Overall responsibility for the child stays with the parents.

After it is told about an arrangement, the council must send an officer, usually a social worker, to visit within seven working days. The officer looks at the home, speaks to the carer and everyone in the household, speaks to the parents, and speaks to the child on their own unless that would be inappropriate. The officer writes a report after the visit.

In England the council then visits at intervals of not more than six weeks during the first year, and not more than 12 weeks in each later year. The child, the carer or a parent can also ask for an extra visit. At each visit the officer checks how the child is getting on, including whether the money agreed for the child's care is working.

If the council has concerns before or during the arrangement, it can set requirements, and the guidance explains that one aim of checking early is so that a council can use its powers to prohibit an arrangement, or to impose requirements, before it begins. If a child is at risk, the council can act to protect them in the same way as for any other child.

Money, benefits and support

Private foster carers are not paid a fostering allowance. In England the regulations expect the parents and the carer to have agreed financial arrangements for the child's care and keep, and the council checks at its visits that those arrangements are working. Scotland's official guidance for carers on mygov.scot says it directly: you will not get financial help from your local council.

You may be able to claim Child Benefit for a child you care for privately, but only one person can claim it for each child. The mygov.scot page notes this for Scotland; the same benefit is run by HMRC across the UK. Whether you can claim, and how it affects the parent's claim or any other benefits, depends on your circumstances, so check with HMRC or a benefits adviser rather than relying on this guide.

This is one of the biggest differences from approved fostering. A foster carer approved by a council or an independent fostering agency receives a weekly allowance for the child's costs (often with a fee on top), training, a supervising social worker and out-of-hours support. Our guide to foster carer pay and our council allowance pages set out what approved carers are paid.

When the arrangement ends

Private fostering can end whenever the parents decide. Scotland's guidance for carers says the child's parents can end the arrangement at any time, and the same principle applies elsewhere because the parents keep parental responsibility.

In England and Wales, a private foster carer must tell the council within 48 hours when the child stops living with them, giving the name and address of the person the child has gone to and their relationship to the child. If the carer plans to have the child back within 27 days, no notice is needed, but if that plan changes or the 27 days pass, notice is due within 48 hours. A parent who gave notice of the arrangement must also tell the council when it ends.

Private fostering and becoming a foster carer

People often come across private fostering because they are already caring for a friend's or relative's child and want to know where they stand, or because they would like to help children and are wondering whether private fostering is a way in. It is not a route to becoming an approved foster carer. Councils and fostering agencies do not place children in private fostering arrangements; nidirect makes the same point for Northern Ireland's Trusts.

If you would like to look after children who cannot live at home, the route is to apply to a council fostering service or an independent fostering agency, go through the assessment, and be approved. You then receive an allowance, training and support. Our guides on how to become a foster carer and on how long it takes walk through the steps.

If the child you are caring for is a relative or friend and the council becomes involved because the child cannot safely live at home, the arrangement may change from private fostering to kinship (family and friends) fostering, with you assessed and approved as the child's foster carer. Our guide to kinship fostering and special guardianship explains the options. The council's children's services team can tell you which applies in your case.

Across the UK

England
The Children (Private Arrangements for Fostering) Regulations 2005 apply: a child under 16 (under 18 if disabled), cared for by someone other than a parent or close relative, for 28 days or more. The carer tells the council at least six weeks ahead (or immediately if sooner), the council visits within seven working days, then at least every six weeks in the first year and every 12 weeks after that. Failing to notify is an offence.
Scotland
The law is the Foster Children (Scotland) Act 1984 and the Foster Children (Private Fostering) (Scotland) Regulations 1985. Private fostering means more than 28 days with someone who is not a close relative (grandparent, brother, sister, uncle or aunt, by blood or marriage). Tell the council at least 14 days before the child moves in, or in an emergency within one week. The council assesses within two weeks of being told, visits within one week of the child moving in, then at least every three months in the first year and every six months after that. There is no financial help from the council.
Wales
The Children (Private Arrangements for Fostering) (Wales) Regulations 2006 follow the same pattern as England: notify the council at least six weeks before (or immediately), a visit within seven working days, visits at intervals of not more than six weeks in the first year and 12 weeks after, and notice within 48 hours when the arrangement ends.
Northern Ireland
Private fostering is covered by the Children (Northern Ireland) Order 1995 and the Children (Private Arrangements for Fostering) Regulations (Northern Ireland) 1996. nidirect defines it as an adult who is not a relative caring for a child under 16 (under 18 if disabled) for more than 28 days. The carer or the parents must tell the local Health and Social Care Trust, which does not itself place children in private fostering.

Common questions

Is a child living with their grandparent privately fostered?

No. Grandparents, brothers, sisters, uncles, aunts and step-parents are close relatives and are left out of the definition, so a child living with them by family arrangement is not privately fostered. A cousin, great-aunt or family friend can be a private foster carer.

Do private foster carers get paid by the council?

No. The parents and the carer agree the money for the child's keep between them, and the council checks it is working. You may be able to claim Child Benefit for the child; HMRC can confirm whether you can.

How long does a child have to stay for it to count?

In England it counts when the arrangement is meant to last 28 days or more, and it counts from the start if that is the plan, not only once 28 days have passed. The official pages for Scotland and Northern Ireland refer to more than 28 days.

What happens if nobody told the council?

Tell the council's children's services now. In England the regulations say a carer who has not notified must do so immediately, and failing to notify is an offence. The council's aim is to check the child is safe and well, and it will explain what happens next.

Is private fostering the same as being a foster carer?

No. A private foster carer is not assessed or approved by a fostering service and gets no fostering allowance, training or supervising social worker. To become an approved foster carer you apply to a council fostering service or an independent fostering agency.
