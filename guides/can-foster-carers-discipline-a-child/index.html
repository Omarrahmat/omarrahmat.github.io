Can foster carers discipline a child? Behaviour, boundaries and the rules

Yes, foster carers set boundaries and use fair, proportionate consequences, just as any good parent does. What they may never do is use physical punishment of any kind: every foster carer in the UK signs an agreement that rules it out, whatever the general law says about parents. Each fostering service also has a written behaviour policy, and you are trained in it before and after approval.

The short answer

Foster carers are expected to guide a child's behaviour, set clear limits and follow through when those limits are broken. Fostering is not a hands-off role, and children in care need the same structure, routine and expectations as any other child, sometimes more. What changes is how you go about it.

Two things are fixed everywhere in the UK. First, a foster carer may never use corporal punishment, which means any physical punishment such as smacking, slapping or shaking, on a child placed with them. This is written into the foster care agreement that every approved carer signs, in England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland. Second, every fostering service must have a written policy on behaviour, and you are expected to follow it. That policy sets out the kinds of consequences that are acceptable, the ones that are not, and what to do in the rare situations where a child has to be physically stopped from hurting someone.

Within those rules there is a lot of room for ordinary family parenting: praise, routines, time-limited loss of privileges, earlier bedtimes, extra chores and so on. The rest of this guide explains where the rules come from, what is usually allowed and not allowed, and how you are supported.

Why physical punishment is never allowed for foster carers

In England, the Fostering Services (England) Regulations 2011 require every fostering service to prepare and follow a written policy on acceptable measures of control, restraint and discipline of children placed with its foster carers. Regulation 13 goes further: the service must take all reasonable steps to make sure that no form of corporal punishment is used on any child in foster care, that no child is subject to control, restraint or discipline that is excessive or unreasonable, and that restraint is used only where it is necessary to prevent injury to the child or other people, or serious damage to property.

The same regulations set out what goes into the foster care agreement, the document you sign when you are approved. Schedule 5 lists the carer's obligations, and one of them is simply not to administer corporal punishment to any child placed with you. Another is to comply with the fostering service's policies, including its behaviour policy. Scotland and Northern Ireland have the same obligation in their own regulations, and Wales has an equivalent rule for fostering services (see the nation-by-nation section below).

This rule applies whatever the general law says about parents. In England, for example, the law still gives parents a limited defence of reasonable punishment: section 58 of the Children Act 2004 rules it out for more serious charges, such as causing actual bodily harm or child cruelty, but does not abolish it. That makes no difference to a foster carer. Your agreement with your fostering service forbids physical punishment outright, and breaking it would be taken very seriously.

There is a good reason for the rule beyond the law. Many children come into care after experiences of harm. Physical punishment can frighten them, repeat what they have been through and damage the trust a placement depends on. Fostering services train carers in approaches that work better for children who have had a difficult start.

What the standards expect: a positive approach

In England, the Fostering Services National Minimum Standards, published by the Department for Education in 2011, include a standard on promoting positive behaviour and relationships (Standard 3). Its aim is that children have sound relationships with their foster family, get on with other people and behave appropriately. It sets out what a good fostering household looks like:

  • High expectations of every foster child in the household.
  • A home culture that promotes, models and supports positive behaviour, rather than one that only reacts to bad behaviour.
  • Helping children learn to build relationships, stand up for themselves and resolve conflicts in a positive way.
  • Encouraging children to take responsibility for their own behaviour, in a way that fits their age and ability.
  • Support for carers to manage their own responses and feelings, especially when a child's behaviour is very challenging.
  • Training for every carer in positive care and control, including how to calm a situation down (de-escalation).
  • Making sure carers have the information they need about a child's history and recent events, so they can understand and anticipate the child's needs and behaviour.
  • Handling behaviour in a way that keeps police involvement to a minimum and avoids criminalising children unnecessarily.

Consequences that are usually allowed, and ones that are not

The exact list depends on your fostering service's own behaviour policy, so always read yours and ask your supervising social worker if anything is unclear. As an example of what a policy looks like, Camden Council's fostering behaviour management policy (version 3, October 2020) says any sanction must be time-limited and proportionate, and gives examples of acceptable and unacceptable ones. In summary, the kinds of consequences it allows are:

  • Losing a privilege or a planned treat for a set time.
  • Reasonable extra household chores.
  • Grounding, time out or an earlier bedtime.
  • Taking away an item that could cause harm, or a personal possession for a time (but not a comforter).
  • Using pocket money only to pay towards damage the child has caused.

Sanctions a fostering service will not allow

The same policy lists sanctions that carers must never use. These are typical of fostering services across the UK, because they come from the same principle: discipline must never harm, frighten or humiliate a child, or cut them off from the people and things they need. The policy rules out:

  • Any physical punishment or rough handling, including hitting, slapping or pinning a child against a wall.
  • Humiliating punishments.
  • Refusing meals or drinks.
  • Using or withholding medication, or medical or dental treatment, as a punishment.
  • Stopping or reducing agreed contact with the child's family, or with an independent visitor.
  • Depriving a child of sleep.
  • Isolating a child or locking them in a room, except where that is needed to prevent serious harm.
  • Intimate searches, fines (other than towards damage), and involving other children in a sanction.
  • Threatening to do any of the above.

Physical intervention and restraint

Holding or restraining a child is not a form of discipline. In England, regulation 13 allows restraint only where it is necessary to prevent injury to the child or other people, or serious damage to property, and never in a way that is excessive or unreasonable. The Welsh fostering regulations similarly allow restraint only where it is necessary to prevent likely injury.

In practice this covers situations such as stopping a young child running into a road or separating two children who are fighting. Fostering services usually distinguish between everyday guiding touch, such as leading a child by the hand, and more restrictive holds. Camden's policy, for example, allows the most intrusive restraint only as a last resort where serious injury or serious damage is reasonably expected, by carers who have been trained, and it lists methods that must never be used, such as anything that restricts breathing, pinning a child to the floor or wall, or sitting on a child.

If a child in your care is likely to need physical intervention, this should be discussed and agreed when the placement is planned. Afterwards, any incident is recorded in detail as soon as possible and reported to the child's social worker and your supervising social worker. Recording protects the child and it protects you.

How you are prepared and supported

You are not expected to work this out alone. Behaviour is covered during your assessment and pre-approval training, and the National Minimum Standards in England say every carer must be trained in positive care and control, including de-escalation. Your fostering service must explain its written behaviour policy, and the standards say that policy should be made clear to the child's placing authority, the child and the child's parents before a placement starts, or at the time in an emergency.

Your supervising social worker is the first person to talk to if a child's behaviour is worrying you, if you are unsure whether a consequence is allowed, or if you are finding it hard to cope. Your service may also offer further training, such as courses in therapeutic parenting, and the chance to meet other carers. Asking for help is part of the job and is expected, not a sign of failure.

It also helps to know a child's story. The standards say carers should have the information they need about a child's circumstances and recent events to understand and predict their behaviour, and that the service should chase the placing authority if information is missing. If you have not been told something that would help you, ask.

Across the UK

England
The Fostering Services (England) Regulations 2011 require every fostering service to have a written policy on control, restraint and discipline, to take all reasonable steps to make sure no corporal punishment is used and nothing excessive or unreasonable, and to allow restraint only to prevent injury or serious damage to property. Every foster care agreement includes the carer's promise not to use corporal punishment. Standard 3 of the 2011 National Minimum Standards sets out the positive approach expected. For parents generally, a limited defence of reasonable punishment remains in English law under section 58 of the Children Act 2004, but it has no bearing on what a foster carer may do.
Scotland
All physical punishment of children has been against the law in Scotland since 7 November 2020, when the Children (Equal Protection from Assault) (Scotland) Act 2019 came into force and removed the old defence of reasonable chastisement. Separately, the Looked After Children (Scotland) Regulations 2009 make it an obligation in every foster carer agreement not to administer corporal punishment to any child placed with you.
Wales
Since 21 March 2022 physical punishment of children, such as smacking, slapping or shaking, has been illegal in Wales for everyone, including visitors, under the Children (Abolition of Defence of Reasonable Punishment) (Wales) Act 2020. Fostering services in Wales have also long been required to have a written policy on control, restraint and discipline, to take reasonable steps to make sure no corporal punishment is used, to make sure nothing excessive or unreasonable is used, and to allow restraint only where it is necessary to prevent likely injury.
Northern Ireland
The Foster Placement (Children) Regulations (Northern Ireland) 1996 list the obligations a foster carer agrees to, and one is not to administer corporal punishment to any child placed with them. Your Health and Social Care Trust or independent fostering agency will explain its own behaviour policy and the training it offers.

Common questions

Can foster carers smack a child?

No. Every foster carer in the UK agrees, as part of their foster care agreement, not to use corporal punishment on a child placed with them. That covers smacking and any other physical punishment, and it applies in England even though parents there still have a limited legal defence.

Can I ground a foster child or take their phone away?

Usually yes, if it is time-limited, proportionate and allowed by your fostering service's behaviour policy. Some services have specific rules about phones, for example where a phone is how a child keeps in touch with family, so check with your supervising social worker.

Can I stop a foster child seeing their family as a punishment?

No. Contact with family is arranged as part of the child's care plan, and fostering services do not allow it to be stopped or reduced as a sanction. If you are worried about how contact is affecting a child, raise it with the child's social worker.

What if a child becomes violent?

Your priority is keeping everyone safe. Restraint is allowed only to prevent injury or serious damage to property, and only in the ways your service has trained you in. Afterwards, record what happened and tell the child's social worker and your supervising social worker straight away. Your service should also offer you support.

Will I be trained in managing behaviour?

Yes. Behaviour is covered during assessment and pre-approval training, and in England the National Minimum Standards require every carer to be trained in positive care and control, including de-escalation. Ask your service what further training it offers after approval.
