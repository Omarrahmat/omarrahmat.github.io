What is a foster care agreement?

When you are approved as a foster carer, the law requires your fostering service to make a written agreement with you, known as the foster care agreement. It records the terms of your approval and the support you will get, and it sets out promises you make, such as never using physical punishment and keeping information about children confidential. Here is what it contains, how it differs from the agreement made for each child, and how the rules compare across the UK.

The short answer

A foster care agreement is the written agreement between you and the fostering service that approved you. It is not a contract of employment, and it is not about any one child. It is the standing agreement that covers the whole time you are approved: what the service will give you, and what you agree to do whenever a child lives with you.

Every part of the UK requires one. In England the Fostering Services (England) Regulations 2011 say that when a service decides to approve you it must give you written notice of the terms of your approval and enter into a written agreement with you covering the matters listed in Schedule 5 of those regulations. Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own regulations with very similar lists, set out further down this guide.

The contents are not something each service invents. The law sets out a minimum list of what the agreement must cover, so the core of it looks much the same whichever council or independent agency you foster with. Services add their own detail on top, for example their policies, payment arrangements and how to contact the out-of-hours team.

When you are given it

The agreement comes at the end of the approval process. After your assessment, the fostering panel considers your application and makes a recommendation, and then a decision maker at the service decides whether to approve you and on what terms. In England, regulation 27 of the 2011 regulations ties the agreement to that decision: once the service decides to approve you, it must give you the written notice of your terms and make the foster care agreement with you. Wales has the same rule in regulation 8 of its 2018 panel regulations, and in Scotland regulation 24 of the Looked After Children (Scotland) Regulations 2009 says the council must enter into a written agreement with you once it decides to approve you.

Northern Ireland puts it slightly differently. Its 1996 foster placement regulations say that, once you are approved, no child is to be placed with you until you have entered into the written agreement. Either way, the practical effect is the same everywhere: you should have your foster care agreement before your first child arrives.

The agreement is kept on your file. In England, the case record a service holds for each approved carer must include the foster care agreement, alongside the notice of approval and the reports of your reviews. It is worth keeping your own copy somewhere you can find it, because you will be asked to work within it for as long as you foster.

What the service must set out

In England, Schedule 5 lists six things the agreement must record. Wales lists the same six. Scotland and Northern Ireland cover most of the same ground in their own words.

These are the matters a foster care agreement in England must include:

  • The terms of your approval: for example how many children, of what ages, and for what type of fostering you are approved.
  • The support and training you will be given.
  • How your approval will be reviewed.
  • How placements of children are made, and what goes into a child's placement plan.
  • How the service will meet any legal liabilities you have because of a placement.
  • How you can make a complaint or representations.

What you agree to do

The second half of the agreement is a list of obligations on you as the carer. In England there are eight. They are worth reading closely before you are approved, because they shape everyday life as a foster carer.

Under Schedule 5 of the England regulations you agree:

  • To care for any child placed with you as if they were a child of your own family, and to promote their welfare with the plans for the child in mind.
  • To tell the service in writing, without delay and with full details, about an intended change of address, a change in who lives in your household, any other change in your circumstances or any event that affects your ability to care for a child or the suitability of your home, and any request or application to adopt or to register as a childcare provider.
  • Not to use corporal punishment on any child placed with you.
  • To keep confidential any information you are given in confidence about a child, their family or anyone else, and not to pass it on without the service's agreement.
  • To follow the terms of any placement plan.
  • To follow the service's policies and procedures.
  • To co-operate with the inspectorate when reasonably asked, including letting an authorised person interview you and visit your home at any reasonable time.
  • To keep the service informed about the child's progress, and to tell it as soon as reasonably practicable about any significant event affecting the child.

The foster care agreement and the placement agreement are different

People often mix up two documents. The foster care agreement is about you and lasts for the whole of your approval. A separate document is made for each child who comes to live with you, setting out the arrangements for that particular child.

In Scotland and Northern Ireland this second document is called a foster placement agreement, and the regulations list what it must cover. In Northern Ireland, for example, Schedule 3 of the 1996 regulations says the authority must give you a statement of what you need to know to care for the child, including the plan for them, their history, religion, culture, language and background, their health needs and their educational needs. It also covers money for the child's keep, who can consent to medical or dental treatment, when the child may stay away from your home, visits and reviews, and contact with the child's family. Scotland's Schedule 4 is very similar. In both nations the information statement should be given when the agreement is signed or, where that is not practicable, within 14 days.

In England and Wales the child-specific arrangements are set out in the child's placement plan, which the council responsible for the child draws up. Your foster care agreement commits you to follow that plan. Our guide to how foster children are matched with carers explains how placement plans work and the time limits for them.

Reviews and changing your terms

Your foster care agreement tells you how your approval will be reviewed, and the regulations set the minimum. In England, regulation 28 says the first review must take place not more than a year after approval, and after that at intervals of not more than a year. The service must seek your views, and those of any child placed with you and any council that has placed a child with you in the previous year, and write a report saying whether you and your household are still suitable and whether your terms of approval are still right. The first review must go to the fostering panel.

If, after a review, a service in England proposes to end your approval or change its terms, it must tell you in writing with its reasons, and you then have 28 days to send written representations or, in most cases, to ask for an independent review. Our guide to the annual foster carer review covers that process in more detail.

Changes that come from your side matter too. Because you agree to tell the service in writing about changes such as a new partner moving in, a house move or a change in your health or work, a review of your approval can follow. Telling the service early is part of the agreement, and it usually makes any change easier to manage.

If you decide to stop fostering

The agreement does not lock you in. In England, regulation 28 lets you give written notice at any time that you no longer wish to be a foster carer, and your approval ends 28 days after the service receives your notice. Wales has the same 28-day rule in regulation 9 of its 2018 panel regulations.

In Scotland, regulation 25 says that when a carer tells the council they no longer wish to foster, the council must end the approval; the regulation does not set a notice period. In Northern Ireland the authority ends the approval from a date given in a notice to you. In practice, if a child is living with you, the service and the child's council will want to plan a move carefully, so talking to your supervising social worker as early as you can is in the child's interests as well as yours. Our guide to when a placement ends explains what usually happens next.

If you want to move to another fostering service rather than stop, note that in England a service must not approve someone who is still approved by another service. Your old approval has to end before the new one begins, which is one reason the 28-day notice matters.

Questions to ask before you sign

Because the legal minimum is the same for every service, the useful differences are in the detail each service adds. Before your approval, it is reasonable to ask to see a copy of the agreement and the policies it refers to, and to ask questions about anything you do not understand. This guide explains the rules in general and is not legal advice; if you are unsure what something in your own agreement means for you, ask your fostering service, and get independent advice if you need it.

  • What support and training will I get, and how often will my supervising social worker visit?
  • Who do I call out of hours, and what support is there in a crisis?
  • How are legal liabilities covered, and what insurance does the service hold?
  • Which policies am I agreeing to follow, and can I have copies?
  • How do I raise a concern or make a complaint, and who deals with it?
  • How and when will my approval be reviewed?

Across the UK

England
The Fostering Services (England) Regulations 2011 require the service to make a written foster care agreement with you when it decides to approve you (regulation 27). Schedule 5 lists six matters the agreement must record, including your terms of approval, support and training, and the complaints procedure, and eight obligations on you, including not using corporal punishment and keeping information confidential. Approval is reviewed at least once a year (regulation 28), and you can end it with 28 days' written notice.
Scotland
Under the Looked After Children (Scotland) Regulations 2009, the council must enter into a written agreement with you once it decides to approve you (regulation 24), covering the matters in Schedule 6, such as support and training, complaints, financial arrangements and your duties to give written notice of changes, not to use corporal punishment and to keep information confidential. A separate foster placement agreement is made for each child (Schedule 4). Under the current text of regulation 25, the first review is within 12 months of approval and later reviews within three years of the previous one, or sooner if needed for a child's welfare.
Wales
The Fostering Panels (Establishment and Functions) (Wales) Regulations 2018 require the service to enter into a foster care agreement when it approves you (regulation 8), covering the matters in Schedule 3. The Welsh list of carer obligations is longer than England's: as well as not using corporal punishment and keeping information confidential, it includes caring in line with the child's care and support plan, promoting contact with family and friends, promoting the child's health and development, and keeping your home safe and properly maintained. Local authority services must monitor carers' compliance with the agreement. Approval is reviewed at least once a year, and you can end it with 28 days' written notice (regulation 9).
Northern Ireland
Under the Foster Placement (Children) Regulations (Northern Ireland) 1996, which use the term foster parent, no child is placed with you until you have entered into a written agreement covering the matters in Schedule 2 (regulation 3). A separate foster placement agreement for each child covers the matters in Schedule 3, except in an emergency or immediate placement. Approval is reviewed at intervals of not more than a year (regulation 4), and if you no longer wish to foster, the authority ends your approval from a date it gives you in a notice.

Common questions

Is a foster care agreement a contract of employment?

No. It is an agreement the fostering regulations require between you and the service that approved you, setting out the terms of your approval, the support you will get and what you agree to do. If you have a question about your own legal status, ask your fostering service or get independent advice.

When do I get my foster care agreement?

When the fostering service decides to approve you, after the panel has made its recommendation. In England, Wales and Scotland the regulations link the agreement to the approval decision, and in Northern Ireland no child can be placed with you until the agreement is in place.

Is the foster care agreement the same as a placement agreement?

No. The foster care agreement covers your approval as a whole. A separate document is made for each child: in Scotland and Northern Ireland a foster placement agreement, and in England and Wales the arrangements for the child are set out in their placement plan, which your foster care agreement commits you to follow.

Does the agreement mean I can never smack a foster child?

Yes. In every UK nation the foster care agreement includes a promise not to use corporal punishment on any child placed with you. Our guide on whether foster carers can discipline a child explains what positive approaches services expect instead.

Can I leave fostering once I have signed it?

Yes. In England and Wales you can give written notice at any time and your approval ends 28 days after the service receives it. In Scotland the council must end your approval when you tell it you no longer wish to foster, and in Northern Ireland the authority ends it from a date it gives you. If a child is living with you, a move will need to be planned with the child's council.
