How many children can you foster at once?

In every part of the UK the starting point is three. The law calls it the usual fostering limit, and it can be exceeded when the children are brothers and sisters, or, outside Scotland, when the council where you live grants an exemption. Scotland has its own rule with its own exceptions. In practice, how many children you are approved for depends on your home, your experience and what each child needs.

The short answer

Most foster carers in the UK can look after up to three foster children at the same time. In England and Wales this comes from Schedule 7 of the Children Act 1989, which sets what it calls the usual fostering limit: a person may not foster more than three children. Northern Ireland has an almost identical rule in Schedule 5 of the Children (Northern Ireland) Order 1995. Scotland sets its limit differently, through the Looked After Children (Scotland) Regulations 2009 as amended in 2014, but the number is also three.

Three is a ceiling, not a target. Some carers are approved for fewer children, sometimes a single child, because that is what suits their home and the children who need placements. The number on your approval is agreed with your fostering service during your assessment and can be changed later as your circumstances and experience change.

The limit can be exceeded in two main ways. The first is when the children are all brothers and sisters, so that a sibling group can stay together. The second, in England, Wales and Northern Ireland, is an exemption granted by the authority for the area where the carer lives. The rest of this guide explains how each works and what it means if you are thinking about fostering.

What counts towards the limit

The limit is about the children you foster. Schedule 7 says a person fosters a child if they are a local authority foster parent for that child, if a voluntary organisation has placed the child with them, or if they are fostering the child privately. So children placed with you by a council, by an independent fostering agency working on a council's behalf, or under a private fostering arrangement all count.

The limit applies to council foster carers and agency foster carers alike. Being approved by an independent fostering agency rather than a council does not give you a higher limit, and the exemption, where one is needed, still comes from the council for the area where you live.

Your own children are not foster children, so they are not what the limit counts. They still matter a great deal, though. When an authority decides whether to let a carer go over the limit, the law tells it to consider the welfare of any other children living in the home as well as the children being fostered. A fostering service will look at the whole household, including birth children, stepchildren and any adult children still at home, when it decides how many children you can care for safely.

Brothers and sisters: the sibling exception

Splitting up brothers and sisters can add to the loss a child already feels when coming into care, so the law makes room for sibling groups. In England, Wales and Northern Ireland, a carer may go over the usual fostering limit if the children concerned are all siblings of each other. No exemption is needed in that case.

Scotland's rule works in a similar way. The 2014 amendment says a local authority must not place a child with a foster carer if it would mean more than three children being placed with that carer at any one time, but the limit does not apply when a group of more than three siblings is being placed. The Scottish Government's national practice guidance on brothers and sisters, published in 2021, explains that sibling groups were left outside the limit so that they could be kept together, and it describes Scottish policy as being that looked-after children should live with their brothers and sisters where that is appropriate.

The exception covers children who are all siblings to one another. If you already foster one unrelated child and are asked to take a group of three brothers and sisters, you would have four children who are not all siblings, and the usual rules on going over the limit would apply. Our guide to fostering siblings covers what sibling placements are like day to day.

Exemptions in England, Wales and Northern Ireland

Outside Scotland, a carer can also go over the usual fostering limit if the authority for the area where they live grants an exemption. In England and Wales that is the local authority; in Northern Ireland the legislation refers to the authority within whose area the carer lives. CoramBAAF, the adoption and fostering charity, notes that a council in England cannot grant an exemption to a carer who lives outside its area, so if you foster for one council or agency but live in another council's area, it is your home council that decides.

The law sets out what the authority must weigh up. It must consider in particular the number of children the person proposes to foster, the arrangements for their care and accommodation, the intended and likely relationship between the carer and the children, how long the fostering is expected to last, and whether the welfare of the fostered children and of any other children living in the home will be safeguarded and promoted.

An exemption is personal and specific. The authority must tell the carer in writing that they are exempted, name the children they may foster, and set out any conditions. It can vary or cancel the exemption, or add or change conditions, at any time by giving written notice. Each authority must also have a way for people to make representations or complaints about these decisions.

Councils publish their own procedures for this. Sheffield's children's services procedures, for example, say an application is only made with the carers' agreement, that the child's social worker and the carers' supervising social worker put the reasons in writing to a designated manager, that any exemption is tied to named children and given a time limit, and that it is then reported to the fostering panel. Typical reasons it lists include keeping siblings together, a placement elsewhere having ended, or a carer having particular skills a child needs.

  • The number of children you would be caring for
  • Your care and accommodation arrangements
  • Your relationship with the children, now and as intended
  • How long the arrangement is expected to last
  • Whether every child in the home, fostered or not, will be safe and well looked after

What happens if the limit is exceeded without permission

The limit has teeth. In England, if a carer fosters more than three children without being exempt, and the children are not all siblings, the law stops treating them as a foster carer and treats them instead as carrying on a children's home, which has to be registered and regulated quite differently. In Wales, since a 2018 change, the equivalent rule treats the carer as providing a care home service under the Regulation and Inspection of Social Care (Wales) Act 2016. Northern Ireland's Schedule 5 also says the carer is then treated as carrying on a children's home.

The same applies if an exempted carer takes on a child who is not named in the exemption and that takes them over the limit. This is one reason fostering services are careful about the paperwork for every placement, including emergencies. As a prospective carer you do not need to manage this yourself, but it helps to know why your supervising social worker will check your approval and any exemption before a child moves in.

Emergencies and short stays

Emergencies are handled differently in each nation. In Scotland the 2014 regulations say the three-child limit does not apply when an emergency placement, or a short-term placement of less than four weeks, would take a carer over three. That allows a child to be found a bed quickly without breaking the rule, but only for a short time.

England, Wales and Northern Ireland have no equivalent automatic exception in the fostering limit itself, so an emergency placement that would take a carer over three unrelated children still needs an exemption from the home authority. Council procedures describe out-of-hours arrangements for this; Sheffield's, for instance, allow a duty manager to agree such a placement until the next working day, after which the normal process applies. CoramBAAF also points out a separate rule in England: a child can be placed with a carer outside the terms of their approval in an emergency for a maximum of six working days, after which the placement must end unless the approval has been changed. That rule is about terms of approval, not the three-child limit, and an exemption is still needed if more than three unrelated children would be placed.

How your own approval number is set

The legal limit is only part of the picture. When you are approved, your fostering service will usually set terms of approval, which can include how many children you may care for, their ages and the type of fostering. CoramBAAF explains that in England the fostering panel recommends any terms, the service's decision maker takes the decision, and the carer is told in writing.

The number you are approved for will reflect practical things: how many bedrooms you have and whether any children could share, your experience, the needs of the children the service is placing, your support network and the demands of your own family and work. A first-time carer may be approved for fewer children than the legal limit while they find their feet, and that number can be reviewed later.

Terms can be changed after a review of your approval. CoramBAAF says that in England a change usually takes effect 28 days after the decision unless the carer agrees in writing for it to take effect sooner, and that the agreement must be freely given. You should never feel pressured into taking more children than you can manage, and council procedures stress that a placement outside a carer's agreed range needs the carer's agreement.

  • Space at home and the bedroom arrangements your service accepts
  • Your fostering experience and training
  • The needs and ages of the children who need homes
  • The other children and adults already living with you
  • The support you have from family, friends and your fostering service

What this means if you are thinking about fostering

If you would like to care for more than one child, or for a group of brothers and sisters, say so early. Fostering services across the UK look for carers who can keep siblings together, and a home that could take two or three children is valued. Your assessing social worker will talk through what is realistic for your household.

If you are not sure you could manage more than one child, that is fine too. Fostering one child at a time is a real contribution, and a settled home for one child is exactly what that child needs. Your fostering service, whether a council or an independent agency, can explain how it sets approval numbers and how often it reviews them, and you can compare services in your area before you apply.

Across the UK

England
Schedule 7 of the Children Act 1989 sets the usual fostering limit at three children, with exceptions for children who are all siblings and for carers exempted by the local authority where they live. A carer who goes over the limit without being exempt is treated as carrying on a children's home. Separately, a child may be placed outside a carer's terms of approval in an emergency for up to six working days.
Scotland
The Looked After Children (Scotland) Regulations 2009, as amended in 2014, say a local authority must not place a child with a foster carer if more than three children would then be placed with that carer at any one time. The limit does not apply to a group of more than three siblings, or to an emergency placement or a short-term placement of less than four weeks.
Wales
Schedule 7 of the Children Act 1989 also applies in Wales: the limit is three, with the same sibling exception and exemptions granted by the local authority where the carer lives. Since 2018, a carer who goes over the limit without being exempt is treated as providing a care home service under the Regulation and Inspection of Social Care (Wales) Act 2016.
Northern Ireland
Schedule 5 of the Children (Northern Ireland) Order 1995 sets the same usual fostering limit of three children, with the same sibling exception and exemptions granted by the authority for the area where the carer lives. A carer who goes over the limit without being exempt is treated as carrying on a children's home.

Common questions

What is the maximum number of foster children you can have?

The usual limit is three foster children at once across the UK. It can be exceeded for a group of brothers and sisters, and in England, Wales and Northern Ireland by an exemption from the authority where you live. Your own approval may set a lower number.

Do my own children count towards the three-child limit?

The limit counts the children you foster, so your own children are not counted as foster children. Their welfare is still taken into account when your fostering service decides how many children you can be approved for, and when an authority considers an exemption.

Can I foster four brothers and sisters together?

Yes, the law allows it. In England, Wales and Northern Ireland a carer may exceed the usual limit if the children are all siblings of each other, and in Scotland the limit does not apply to a group of more than three siblings. Your service would still need to be satisfied that your home and support can meet all their needs.

Who decides whether I can foster more than three children?

In England, Wales and Northern Ireland it is the authority for the area where you live, even if you foster for an independent agency or another council. It must consider the children's welfare and that of any other children in the home, and confirm any exemption in writing, naming the children.

Will I be approved for three children straight away?

Not necessarily. Your fostering service sets your terms of approval based on your home, experience and circumstances, and a new carer may start with fewer than three. The number can be reviewed and changed later with your agreement.
