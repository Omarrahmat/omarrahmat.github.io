Can you foster if you are in debt or on a low income?

Often, yes. None of the fostering regulations or official guidance we checked sets a minimum income for foster carers, and having debt, being out of work or claiming benefits does not on its own rule you out. What a fostering service looks at is whether your household finances are stable and well managed, so that a child can join a secure home.

The short answer

There is no salary you have to earn to become a foster carer, and none of the regulations or official guidance we checked says that people with debt cannot foster. The Fostering Network, the UK-wide fostering charity, says that not having a steady income will not stop you fostering as long as you can show financial stability. Council fostering teams say much the same: Manchester City Council tells applicants they can foster while unemployed provided it will not cause financial hardship, and Enfield Council says carers need stable circumstances and the ability to manage their money.

So the honest answer is that money is part of the assessment, but it is looked at as a whole rather than as a pass or fail test. A household with a modest income, a clear budget and debts that are being paid down can be a better fit than a higher-earning household whose finances are under strain. The rest of this guide explains what services ask about, why, and what you can do to prepare.

Why fostering services ask about your money

Children in foster care have often lived through a great deal of instability. A fostering service is looking for a home that will stay settled for as long as the child needs it, and money worries are one of the things that can unsettle a household. Guidance widely used by social workers in England when writing up a fostering assessment, published by the adoption and fostering charity CoramBAAF, says this directly: it points to a safeguarding review in which a high level of household debt was identified as a source of stress in the family, and it asks assessors to consider debt fully for that reason.

The same guidance is just as clear that the conversation should be handled with tact and curiosity, and that applicants should be able to talk about their finances without feeling judged. It also notes that wanting to foster only for the money would be a worrying motivation, while recognising that fostering as a career is legitimate where the other reasons are sound. In other words, the aim is to understand your situation, not to exclude anyone who is not well off.

What the assessment looks at

In England, the information a fostering service must gather about every applicant is set out in Schedule 3 to the Fostering Services (England) Regulations 2011. It does not include a finance test as such, but it does include your past and present employment or occupation, your standard of living and leisure activities, and details of your accommodation. Scotland's equivalent, Schedule 3 to the Looked After Children (Scotland) Regulations 2009, also covers employment, standard of living and accommodation.

In practice the social worker who assesses you will go through your household finances with you. The CoramBAAF guidance for the Form F assessment report in England lists household finances among the checks and says the social worker will complete a financial assessment or statement covering income and expenditure, and will discuss any debt, including how it is being managed and reduced. Questions it suggests include:

  • Who in the household organises income and outgoings, and who pays the bills
  • Whether you have shared or separate bank accounts, and who keeps an eye on them
  • Whether you use a spreadsheet, app or other way of budgeting
  • Whether you have any debt, how much in total, and who is responsible for paying it back

If you have debt

Debt is common, and having some is not in itself a reason to be turned down. What matters is whether it is under control. CoramBAAF updated its financial assessment form in November 2022 so that it records the total amount a household owes, not only the monthly repayments, which tells you that assessors will want the full picture. Lewisham Council, for example, says it needs to know about your financial situation and any outstanding debts, and must be satisfied that they are being managed.

Being open is the best approach. If you have a loan, a credit card balance, a debt management plan or another arrangement, say so early and bring the paperwork. A social worker who can see that you know what you owe, have a realistic plan and keep to it has something positive to write about. Debts that come to light later, or arrears that are growing, are harder to explain.

None of the official sources we checked names a credit score threshold, or says that bankruptcy, an individual voluntary arrangement or a debt relief order automatically rules you out. Each service makes its own judgement, so if you are in a formal insolvency arrangement, ask the service you are thinking of applying to how it would look at your circumstances. If your debts feel unmanageable, it is worth getting free, regulated debt advice before you apply: sorting things out first will help your household and your application.

If you are on a low income, out of work or claiming benefits

You can foster without a job. Foster Wales says that not being in work will not stop you becoming a foster carer, nidirect says people in Northern Ireland can apply whether they are employed or out of work, and Enfield Council says unemployed people can still foster. Leicestershire County Council asks about employment in its online eligibility check and says each case is looked at individually.

You can also foster while claiming benefits. The Fostering Network says fostering payments are generally disregarded when working out welfare benefits, and Lewisham Council tells applicants that its fostering allowance and reward will not affect benefits such as Universal Credit or Housing Benefit. The detail depends on the benefit and your circumstances, so check your own position with the benefits office or an independent adviser, and see our guide to fostering and benefits for the main rules.

One point services make consistently is that the fostering allowance is for the child. The Fostering Network and Lincolnshire's fostering service both say the payments should add to your household income rather than be your main source of it. In England the national minimum allowance for 2026-27 ranges from £176 to £267 a week per child outside London and the South East, depending on age, and it is meant to cover the cost of caring for that child. A service will want to see that your household can meet its own costs, including the gaps between placements when no allowance is paid.

Your home and other costs

You do not need to own your home. GOV.UK says that you do not have to be a homeowner to foster in England but that you will usually need a spare bedroom, and The Fostering Network says the same across the UK. If you rent, services will usually want to know your tenancy is secure, and some, such as Lewisham, ask for written permission from your landlord.

It helps to think through the costs that fostering can bring before you apply, such as a bigger food bill, more travel to school and family time, and the time you may need to take off work, especially for younger children. Services pay allowances and, in many cases, a fee on top, and many pay extra for birthdays, holidays and festivals, but the amounts and the way they are paid vary. Our council pay pages and calculator show what different services publish.

How to prepare

A little preparation makes the money part of the assessment easier for you and your assessor.

  • Write down your monthly income and outgoings, including any debts and what you pay towards them each month
  • Gather recent bank statements and statements for any loans, credit cards or repayment plans
  • Be ready to explain any past money problems and what changed
  • Think about how your household would manage if a placement ended and the allowance stopped for a few weeks
  • If you plan to reduce your working hours to foster, work out what that would mean for your budget
  • Ask each service you are considering what it pays, when, and how it supports carers between placements

Who to ask

Every fostering service, whether a council or an independent agency, makes its own decision, and the final recommendation comes from its fostering panel. If you are worried about your finances, the simplest step is to ring a service's recruitment team and describe your situation in general terms before you apply. Many will tell you at that first conversation whether anything is likely to be a problem.

This guide explains how fostering services generally approach money. It is not financial, legal or benefits advice. For advice on debt, use a free, regulated debt advice service, and for benefits, speak to the benefits office or an independent welfare rights adviser.

Across the UK

England
The information a service must collect is set by Schedule 3 to the Fostering Services (England) Regulations 2011, which covers employment, standard of living and accommodation but sets no income level. Most assessments are written on CoramBAAF's Form F, whose guidance asks social workers to look at income, outgoings and the total amount of any debt.
Scotland
Schedule 3 to the Looked After Children (Scotland) Regulations 2009 lists the information gathered about prospective carers, including employment, standard of living and accommodation. It does not set a minimum income, and your council or agency will look at your finances as part of the wider assessment.
Wales
Foster Wales says being out of work will not stop you fostering and that renting is fine if you feel secure in your home. Your local Foster Wales team will discuss your finances during the assessment.
Northern Ireland
nidirect says you can apply to foster whether you are employed or out of work. The fostering service assesses your household, including checks and references, and will talk through your circumstances with you.

Common questions

Is there a minimum income to become a foster carer?

No. None of the fostering regulations or official guidance we checked sets a minimum income. Services look at whether your household finances are stable and well managed, and The Fostering Network says not having a steady income will not stop you if you can show financial stability.

Will a fostering service do a credit check?

The official sources we checked do not say that fostering services run credit checks or use a credit score threshold. Services do look closely at your finances, including any debt, so be ready to show statements and ask the service what checks it carries out.

Can I foster if I am in a debt management plan, IVA or bankruptcy?

There is no published rule that rules you out automatically. The service will want to understand what you owe and how it is being managed. Each service decides for itself, so describe your situation to the service before applying.

Can I foster if I am on Universal Credit?

Yes, you can foster while claiming benefits, and fostering payments are generally disregarded for welfare benefits. How this works for you depends on your circumstances, so check with the benefits office or an independent adviser.

Can I foster if I am unemployed?

Yes. Foster Wales, nidirect and several English councils say being out of work does not stop you fostering, as long as your household is financially stable and the allowance is not your only means of meeting your own costs.
