Can you take a foster child on holiday abroad?

Yes, foster children often go on holiday abroad with their foster families, but it takes planning. Here is who has to agree, how passports and consent letters work, what to sort out about insurance, school and contact, and how the rules differ in England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland.

The short answer

You can take a child you foster on holiday abroad, and many foster families do. Foster Wales puts it simply: yes, you can, but you need permission from the local authority, and sometimes from the child's birth parents, before you book anything.

The reason is that foster carers do not hold parental responsibility for the child. That stays with the child's parents and, depending on the child's legal order, the council (in Northern Ireland, the Health and Social Care Trust). Taking a child out of the country is one of the decisions the law treats as more serious than everyday parenting, so it cannot be decided by the carer alone.

In practice this means three things: get agreement from the child's social worker before you pay for anything, allow plenty of time for a passport if the child does not already have one, and carry a letter of consent from the council when you travel. The rest of this guide explains each step and what to expect.

Who has to agree, and why it depends on the child's legal order

Who can say yes depends on how the child came to be looked after. The Fostering Network advises carers to start by finding out what legal order the child is on and who has parental responsibility, because that decides who has to consent to the trip and who can apply for a passport.

In England and Wales, section 33 of the Children Act 1989 says that while a care order is in force, nobody may remove the child from the United Kingdom without the written consent of every person with parental responsibility or the permission of a court. The same section makes an exception that allows the council looking after the child to arrange for them to be taken abroad for less than one month. That is why, for a child on a full care order, a holiday of a week or two abroad is usually authorised by the council, while a stay of a month or more needs the written agreement of everyone with parental responsibility or a court order.

Durham County Council's published procedure shows how this works in practice: for a holiday of under a month, parents' consent is not legally required but should be sought where practicable and any objections recorded; for a month or more, everyone with parental responsibility must agree in writing, or the council must go to court. Where a child is accommodated with their parents' agreement rather than under a care order (section 20 in England, section 76 in Wales), the council does not hold parental responsibility, so the parents' consent is normally needed.

Whatever the order, the person to talk to is the child's social worker. Councils usually also need a manager to sign off a trip abroad. Coventry City Council, for example, says the child's social worker approves all holidays, and that holidays abroad also need the approval of the social worker's team manager and operational lead before a holiday approval letter is issued.

Step by step: planning a holiday abroad with a foster child

Every council and agency has its own procedure, but the steps below are common to the official guidance we have read. Gov.uk tells foster carers in England that if you do not have authority to take your foster child on holiday you need to speak to their social worker, tell them when you will be going and when you will be back, and get a letter of consent from the social worker for passport control if you are going abroad.

  • Tell your supervising social worker and the child's social worker as soon as you have an idea of a trip, before booking. No official source we read sets a fixed notice period; the Fostering Network says to allow plenty of time, and Coventry says to tell the social worker as soon as you have an idea of a holiday.
  • Give the details the council will ask for. Coventry and Durham both list the dates and length of the trip, the address and type of holiday, insurance, who will be there, the names and addresses of any friends or relatives you will stay with, emergency contacts and any risk assessments.
  • Check whether the child has a valid passport. If not, the council or a parent will need to apply, which can take time (see the next section).
  • Agree sleeping arrangements in writing. The Fostering Network suggests getting written agreement from the social worker, and Coventry says the safer caring plan should be updated for the holiday, for example if you will share a family room or a chalet.
  • Ask whether contact with the child's family will be affected. Durham's procedure suggests extra contact before and after a holiday that interrupts the usual arrangements.
  • Wait for written approval, then book. Haringey and Foster Wales both advise getting confirmation before you pay for anything.
  • Carry the consent letter, the passport and any other papers the council asks for. Coventry tells its carers to take a copy of the care order, the passports and the holiday approval letter. Foster Wales suggests keeping the permission letter in your hand luggage, as it helps at airport security, particularly where the child's surname is different from yours.

Passports: who applies and how long it takes

Foster carers cannot apply for a child's passport on their own authority. HM Passport Office's guidance for its examiners says the Director of Children's Services (or equivalent) must consent to a passport application for a child in care, and that if a foster parent fills in the form, the examiner must also have signed, written consent from the Director before going ahead. The covering letter can be signed by the Director or a representative such as a social worker, and the referee must be a social worker who knows the child and is not the person giving consent.

What else is needed depends on the order. HM Passport Office says that for a full care order in England and Wales the council can apply without the parents' consent, and a member of children's services confirms in writing that the child is in care; it does not need to see the care order itself. For a child accommodated by agreement, a person with parental responsibility normally needs to consent for a child under 16. For an interim care order, the order must be sent with the application. Passports and documents for a child in care are sent to the social services office, not to the foster home.

In practice the child's social worker usually handles the application with you. Durham's procedure says the social worker completes or helps with the form and that approval comes from a designated manager, and it warns that a last-minute trip may not leave enough time to get travel documents. The Fostering Network makes the same point: a first passport can be slow, so start early.

Travel insurance and healthcare abroad

Check that the child is covered by your travel insurance in the same way as anyone else in your household. The Fostering Network suggests confirming this with your insurer before you go, and Coventry strongly advises taking out insurance that covers cancellation, because a child who has experienced trauma may find it hard to cope away from their routine and the trip may not go ahead. Coventry adds that children's services will not usually be able to reimburse you if it does not.

For trips to Europe and some other countries, a UK Global Health Insurance Card (GHIC) gives access to necessary state healthcare on the same basis as a local resident. The NHS says the card is free, lasts for up to five years and should only be applied for through the NHS website, because unofficial sites may charge a fee. It also says a GHIC is not a replacement for travel insurance: it does not cover being flown home, private treatment or mountain rescue. Every family member needs their own card. Foster Wales notes that a foster child may not already have a passport or GHIC, and that these may need to be requested from the birth parents through the social worker or applied for, so ask the social worker how the card is handled for the child you look after.

Money, school dates and building up to a longer trip

Ask your fostering service what it pays towards holidays and get the answer in writing. The Fostering Network says carers should ask whether a holiday allowance is paid, and describes an allowance that covers day trips and activities as good practice. Foster Wales is candid that the support carers receive is unlikely to stretch to an expensive holiday, so you may need to pay part of the extra cost yourself. Some councils, such as Haringey, say they pay an annual holiday allowance for looked-after children. Durham's procedure says any extra costs need a manager's approval before the social worker can agree them. What is paid varies widely, so check with your own service.

Plan trips for the school holidays. Coventry tells its carers that children must not be taken on holiday during term time except in exceptional circumstances, and Durham's procedure notes that the Department for Education does not treat a family holiday as an exceptional circumstance. Foster Wales says most councils expect holidays to be taken outside term and advises checking with the school and the council before booking a cheaper term-time break.

Think about how well you know the child before booking a long trip. Coventry suggests starting with short breaks and building up, and Foster Wales recommends UK trips such as caravan breaks and staycations, especially soon after a child has arrived. Trips in the UK do not need a passport, and gov.uk says that for any holiday you do not have delegated authority for, the social worker should know when you are going and when you will be back.

When the answer is no, or the child cannot go

Sometimes a trip abroad is not possible. A parent may object, the destination may not be safe, or the child's situation may make travel unwise. Durham's procedure says that where parents oppose a trip the social worker consults the team manager and takes legal advice, and that if the trip is approved despite the objection the reasons are recorded and explained to the parent. It also says the social worker should check the Foreign Office travel advice and raise any concern that the child might not return, and that approval is unlikely for a young person seeking asylum if travel could affect their claim.

If you already have a holiday booked and the child cannot come, talk to your supervising social worker early. Foster Wales says the child could stay with approved carers, family or a trusted foster carer while you are away, though it does not encourage long trips without the children and suggests shorter child-free breaks instead. Respite arrangements and what is paid while a child is elsewhere vary between services.

None of this is a reflection on you as a carer. The checks exist because the child's parents and the council remain responsible for the child, and a trip abroad is one of the decisions they are expected to share.

Across the UK

England
Section 33 of the Children Act 1989 means a child on a care order cannot be taken out of the UK without the written consent of everyone with parental responsibility or a court's permission, except that the council looking after the child can arrange trips abroad of less than one month. Gov.uk tells carers to speak to the child's social worker if holidays are not delegated to them, to say when they are going and coming back, and to get a consent letter for passport control. Each council publishes its own holiday procedure.
Scotland
The Scottish Government's Decision Making Guidance for Foster Carers (2015) says holidays should be part of the child's plan. Short breaks in Scotland do not need special permission, and holidays elsewhere in the UK are normally not a problem, provided the social worker has the details and no order condition says otherwise. Holidays outside the UK need the permission of a person with parental responsibilities and rights, and if that is unreasonably withheld a children's hearing or court can make an order allowing the trip. Passport applications can only be signed by a person with parental responsibilities and rights, or by social work where the court has made a permanence order.
Wales
The Children Act 1989 rule on removing a child on a care order from the UK applies in Wales as in England; children accommodated by agreement are covered by section 76 of the Social Services and Well-being (Wales) Act 2014, so parents' consent is normally needed. Foster Wales says carers can take a foster child abroad with the local authority's permission, and sometimes the birth parents', obtained before booking, and suggests carrying the permission letter in hand luggage.
Northern Ireland
The Department of Health's Guidance on Delegated Authority to Foster Carers in Northern Ireland (2010) says holidays of more than five days should be agreed in advance, including discussion and, where appropriate, agreement with the parents, and that trips abroad funded by the Trust or involving hazardous activities must be agreed with social services first. General consent can be given for regular trips to the Republic of Ireland. Passports are a matter for the Health and Social Care Trust or a birth parent, not the foster carer. HM Passport Office treats care orders under the Children (Northern Ireland) Order 1995 in the same way as in England and Wales.

Common questions

Can a foster carer sign a child's passport application?

Not on their own authority. HM Passport Office requires the consent of the Director of Children's Services for a child in care, and if a foster carer completes the form it must also have the Director's signed, written consent. In Scotland the application can only be signed by a person with parental responsibilities and rights, or by social work where there is a permanence order. Your child's social worker will tell you how it is handled.

How much notice do I need to give?

No official guidance we read sets a fixed notice period, but every source says to ask as early as possible and before booking. If the child needs a new passport, allow several weeks at least, as a first passport for a child in care involves extra paperwork.

Do birth parents have to agree to a holiday abroad?

It depends on the child's legal order and the length of the trip. In England and Wales, for a child on a care order, the council can authorise a trip abroad of less than a month, though parents' views are usually sought. A trip of a month or more needs the written consent of everyone with parental responsibility or a court order. For a child accommodated by agreement, the parents' consent is normally needed. In Scotland, a holiday abroad needs the permission of a person with parental responsibilities and rights or an order.

What documents should I take to the airport?

The child's passport and the council's letter of consent at the very least. Gov.uk says you need a consent letter from the child's social worker for passport control, and some councils ask carers to carry a copy of the care order as well. Ask your service exactly what it wants you to carry.

Can I take my foster child on holiday in the UK without asking?

Sometimes, if your placement plan gives you that authority, but you should still tell the child's social worker where you are going and when you will be back. In Northern Ireland, holidays of more than five days should be agreed in advance. If you are not sure what you are allowed to decide, check the placement plan or ask your supervising social worker.

Who pays for the child's share of the holiday?

It varies. Some services pay a holiday allowance and some councils approve extra costs case by case, while Foster Wales notes that carers may have to cover part of a more expensive trip themselves. Ask your fostering service for its policy in writing before you book.
