How are foster children matched with foster carers?

Matching is the decision about which foster home a particular child goes to. The child's council leads it, your fostering service only puts you forward if you can reasonably be expected to meet the child's needs, and you should be given what is known about the child before they arrive. Here is how it works, what is weighed up, what happens in an emergency, and how the rules differ across the UK.

The short answer

When a child needs a foster home, the council responsible for them (in the regulations, the responsible authority) looks at what that child needs and then looks for a carer who can meet those needs. If you are approved by an independent fostering agency, the council asks agencies as well as its own carers, and your agency decides whether to put you forward. Matching is the name for that whole process of fitting a particular child to a particular household.

In England, the national minimum standards for fostering services say a service should only suggest a carer to a council as a potential match if the carer can reasonably be expected to meet the child's assessed needs, and if the effect of the placement on the people already living in the home has been considered. Where there are gaps, the service and the council should make sure the child's placement plan sets out the extra training, resources or support needed to fill them.

Outside an emergency, a child can only be placed with a carer whose terms of approval fit the child's circumstances. Your terms of approval are set when you are approved and usually say how many children you can care for, of what ages and for what type of fostering. So the first filter on any match is simply whether a child falls within what you have been approved for.

What is weighed up in a match

There is no single formula. Ofsted's research report Matching in foster care, published in November 2020, described matching as often a tricky balancing act between what would be ideal for a child and what is possible with the carers available. Managers it surveyed said carers' experience and skills, and their ability to meet the individual child's needs, were the most important considerations. Location was also one of the most important factors, because it affects school, friendships and family time.

The things services commonly look at include:

  • the child's age, needs, health, behaviour and history, and whether they fall within your terms of approval
  • your skills, training and experience, and the support you would need
  • brothers and sisters: the law in England expects siblings in care to live together so far as is reasonably practicable, and keeping in touch with siblings who live apart is an important part of a match
  • where you live, and how far that is from the child's school, family and community
  • your household: your own children, other children already placed with you, other adults and pets
  • bedrooms: the national minimum standards in England say each child over the age of three should have their own bedroom, unless sharing has been agreed and assessed
  • the child's ethnicity, religion, culture and language, and the child's own wishes and feelings

Culture, religion and identity

A child's identity matters in a match, but an exact match is not usually required and often is not possible. Ofsted found little evidence that professionals were looking for exact matches of religion, ethnicity or cultural identity, although it saw good efforts to meet children's needs in these areas. It also found that a shortage of carers from some ethnic, cultural and religious backgrounds was a common problem.

What this means for you as a carer is that you may be matched with a child whose background is different from your own. Your service should then help you understand and support the child's heritage, faith and language, and the plan for the placement should say how that will happen. In Northern Ireland the regulations go further on religion, as the section on the nations below explains.

What you should be told before a child arrives

The 2011 national minimum standards in England are clear that before each child is placed, the foster carer is given all the information the fostering service holds that they need to do their job well. It should be in a clear, comprehensive written form and should include the support that will be available to you. If the council has left gaps in what it has told the service about the child or the child's family, and those gaps could stop you keeping the child, other children in the home and yourself safe, the service is expected to chase them up.

The standards also say that before a placement begins you should have as full a description as possible of the child's health needs, and that you should receive clear information about the allowances and expenses payable before a child is placed.

In practice, information is not always as complete as it should be. Carers told Ofsted that the information they received was too often out of date, incomplete or impersonal, and some found that what they had been told turned out to be wrong. Carers who took a child in an emergency were much more likely to say they had known nothing about the child beforehand. It is reasonable to ask questions when you are told about a child, and to ask your supervising social worker to follow up anything that is missing.

Saying yes, and the placement plan

Being told about a child is the point at which you, your family and your supervising social worker think honestly about whether this match will work. Ofsted found that the possible effect on carers' own children was an important consideration when carers decided whether to look after a child. It also found that many carers believed having a vacancy was the main reason they had been matched, which is a reminder that a good match depends on carers being open about what they can and cannot manage. Ask your service how referrals are offered to you and what happens if you feel a particular match is not right.

In England, the council must prepare a placement plan before the child moves in, setting out how the placement will help meet the child's needs. If that is not reasonably practicable, for example in an emergency, the plan must be prepared within five working days of the placement starting. The plan has to be agreed and signed, and the child's wishes and feelings must have been found out and considered. The national minimum standards expect fostering services to support carers to play an active role in agreeing the plan's contents, and to give the carer a copy as soon as the council provides it.

Emergency placements

Some children need somewhere to stay the same day, often in the evening or at a weekend. In those cases there is far less time to match carefully, and less information is usually available. The rules allow for this, but they limit how long a placement made outside the normal process can last before it is reviewed or brought into line, and those limits differ by nation (see below).

If you are approved for emergency fostering, expect to be asked at short notice, to be told only the basics at first, and for the placement plan and fuller information to follow within days. In England, if a child has to move from a placement in an emergency, the fostering service is expected to tell the council within one working day. Our guide to emergency fostering explains the role in more detail.

Keeping a placement stable

The aim of matching is a placement that lasts as long as the child needs it to. Ofsted noted that the more stable and settled a child's time in foster care has been, the more likely it is that their outcomes will be good, and that unplanned endings create further instability. When matches do not work, it leads to further distress and instability for children.

The national minimum standards say that once a child is placed, they are not removed from a carer who is willing and able to keep caring for them unless that is in the child's best interests, taking the child's current wishes into account, and decided (outside an emergency) through the child's care planning process. Support matters here too: if a match has gaps, the placement plan should say what training or help will be put in place, and you can ask for it to be reviewed if things change.

Questions to ask your fostering service

Every service runs matching a little differently, so it is worth asking before and after you are approved. Useful questions include:

  • How are referrals passed to carers, and how much information will I usually see before I decide?
  • Who will I talk to about whether a match is right for my family, and is there time to discuss it with my own children?
  • How do you handle emergency referrals, and how quickly will I get the placement plan?
  • What support do you put in place where a match is not perfect?
  • How do you take account of the children already living with us?

Across the UK

England
Placements are made under the Care Planning, Placement and Case Review (England) Regulations 2010. Regulation 9 requires a placement plan before the placement, or within five working days of the start if that is not reasonably practicable. The 2011 Fostering Services National Minimum Standards set out how services should match (standard 15), what information carers should have (standards 12 and 15) and the carer's part in the placement plan (standard 31). Outside an emergency, a child is only placed with a carer whose terms of approval fit the placement.
Scotland
Under the Looked After Children (Scotland) Regulations 2009, a council may only place a child with a foster carer if it is satisfied of several things first, including that the placement is in the child's best interests, that the carer is approved and that the terms of approval are consistent with the placement, and that written agreements are in place with the carer, both the general foster carer agreement and one covering the particular child. Where other children in the family are looked after, the council must where practical and appropriate place them with the same carer or nearby. Scottish Government guidance on the regulations says an emergency placement must be reviewed within three working days and can continue for up to 12 weeks from the date it started.
Wales
Under the Care Planning, Placement and Case Review (Wales) Regulations 2015, the council must put a placement plan in place before the placement, or within five working days of its start if that is not reasonably practicable, after finding out and considering the child's views, wishes and feelings. A child may only be placed with an approved carer whose terms of approval are consistent with the placement and who has signed a foster care agreement. In an emergency, a child can be placed with an approved local authority foster carer outside their terms of approval for no longer than six working days, unless the terms are changed to fit.
Northern Ireland
Under the Foster Placement (Children) Regulations (Northern Ireland) 1996, a child is placed only if it is the most suitable placement in all the circumstances, and, except in an emergency or an immediate placement, only once the authority and the carer have signed a written agreement about that child. Where possible the carer should share the child's religious persuasion, or undertake that the child will be brought up in it. In an emergency a child can be placed with any approved foster carer for up to 24 hours, and an immediate placement with a relative or friend who is not yet approved can last up to six weeks.

Common questions

Who decides which foster carer a child goes to?

The council responsible for the child leads the decision. If you are with an independent fostering agency, the agency decides whether to put you forward, and the council then chooses between the carers suggested. Your own view, and your supervising social worker's, feed into whether the match goes ahead.

Can I say no to a child I am matched with?

Matching is meant to be a careful decision that you take part in, and Ofsted found that carers weigh things such as the effect on their own children when deciding whether to look after a child. How referrals are offered, and what happens if you decline, depends on your fostering service, so ask them to explain their approach.

How much will I know about a child before they move in?

In England the standards say you should be given, before the placement, all the information the service holds that you need to care for the child safely, in clear written form. In emergencies far less may be known at first, and Ofsted found information was not always complete or up to date, so ask questions and ask your service to chase gaps.

Will I only be matched with children of my own background?

Not necessarily. A child's ethnicity, religion, culture and language are considered, but Ofsted found exact matches were rarely sought and there are too few carers from some backgrounds. If you care for a child from a different background, your service should support you to help them keep their identity, faith and language.

How long does it take to be matched after approval?

There is no fixed time. It depends on which children need homes in your area, your terms of approval and your location. Your fostering service can tell you what is typical for carers approved like you.

Are brothers and sisters placed together?

The aim is to keep siblings together where it is reasonably practicable. In England the law expects this, and in Scotland the council must place siblings with the same carer or nearby where practical and appropriate. If siblings cannot live together, keeping in touch with each other should be part of the plan.
