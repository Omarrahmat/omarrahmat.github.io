Delegated authority: what can foster carers decide for a child?

Can you sign a school trip form, book a haircut or say yes to a sleepover for a child you foster? Here is what delegated authority means, which decisions are usually yours, which stay with the council and the child's parents, where it is written down, and how the rules differ in England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland.

The short answer

Delegated authority is the name for the everyday decisions a foster carer is allowed to make for the child they look after. The Fostering Network describes it as the process that lets foster carers make common sense, everyday decisions, such as agreeing to a sleepover at a friend's house, signing a consent form for a school trip or arranging a haircut.

Foster carers do not hold parental responsibility for a child in foster care. That stays with the child's parents and, depending on the child's legal status, the council (or, in Northern Ireland, the Health and Social Care Trust). When you make a decision, you are using authority that has been passed to you by those people, and it should be written down so that everyone knows who decides what.

In England, statutory guidance says that whatever the long-term plan for the child, the carer should have delegated authority to take day-to-day parenting decisions. Bigger decisions, such as which school a child goes to or whether they have an operation, usually stay with the council and the parents. Exactly where the line falls depends on the child, their plan and your fostering service's policy, so your supervising social worker is the person to ask when you are unsure.

Why it matters to children and to carers

Children in foster care should be able to live as ordinary a life as possible. If every permission slip or sleepover had to wait for a social worker's signature, a child could miss out on things their friends take for granted, and could feel singled out because of it. Delegated authority exists so that the adult who knows the child best day to day can say yes or no without unnecessary delay.

The Fostering Network's guidance for fostering services says foster carers should be empowered to take all day-to-day decisions about the child in their care unless stated otherwise, and that authority for everyday decisions should be given without unnecessary delays and restrictions. It also says carers should have enough training and support from social workers to make those decisions well.

For carers, clear delegated authority means less uncertainty. You know what you can agree to, what you need to check, and who to call. It also protects you: if a decision is clearly yours and you make it sensibly, you are acting within what has been agreed.

The three kinds of decision

In England, the government's statutory guidance on care planning (Volume 2 of the Children Act 1989 guidance) sorts decisions about a looked-after child into three broad groups. The gov.uk help pages for foster carers use the same three levels, with examples:

  • Day-to-day decisions: routine matters of health and hygiene, education and leisure. Gov.uk gives dental check-ups, haircuts, school trips, parent-teacher meetings and sleepovers as examples. The guidance says all of these should be delegated to the carer, or to the child if they are able to decide for themselves.
  • Longer-term decisions: routine but bigger choices, for example which school the child attends. These are usually made by the council with the parents, with your views taken into account.
  • Significant decisions: major events, for example surgery. Gov.uk says these are made by the local authority and the birth parents.

Where your authority is written down

Every child in foster care has a placement plan, drawn up by the council responsible for the child. Gov.uk tells foster carers that the placement plan should tell you what decisions you can make. In England, the statutory guidance says the plan must make clear who has authority to take decisions in key areas of the child's day-to-day life, including medical or dental treatment, education, leisure and home life, faith and religious observance, and the use of social media, and that the person with authority for a particular decision or consent must be clearly named.

The placement plan should be drawn up before the child is placed. Where that is not possible, for example in an emergency, the regulations allow it to be made within five working days of the start of the placement. If you are fostering more than one child, your authority can be different for each of them, because each child's plan is separate.

If the plan does not say who can make a particular decision, gov.uk's advice is to contact your fostering service. In England each council should also have a published policy on delegating authority, and placement plans should take it into account, so it is worth asking your service for a copy when you are approved.

Everyday examples: what you can usually decide and what you check

Every service writes its own policy, so treat these as typical examples rather than rules. The detail for your child will be in their placement plan.

Things foster carers are commonly given authority for:

  • Sleepovers and visits to friends' homes, using the same common sense you would for your own child.
  • Signing consent forms for school trips and activities that have been risk-assessed in the usual way. England's guidance says these should be delegated to the carer.
  • Haircuts, routine dental and optician check-ups and attending parents' evenings.
  • Joining clubs, sports and after-school activities.
  • Other routine matters of health, hygiene, education and leisure, which England's guidance treats as day-to-day parenting decisions.

Things you will usually need to check first

Some decisions carry more risk or matter more to the child's family, so they are more likely to stay with the council or the parents, or to need agreement in advance:

  • Holidays and trips away. Gov.uk says that if you do not have authority, speak to the child's social worker, and tell them when you leave and when you return. For travel abroad you will need a letter of consent from the social worker to show at passport control.
  • Medical treatment beyond the routine. Gov.uk advises checking the placement plan for what you can agree to on medication, medical examinations, local or general anaesthetic and surgery.
  • Applying for a passport. Northern Ireland's regional guidance says foster carers cannot legally give this consent; ask the child's social worker how it is handled for your child.
  • Faith and religious observance, such as religious ceremonies. England's guidance names this as one of the key areas the placement plan must cover, so check what the plan says before agreeing.
  • Social media and the media. England's guidance lists use of social media as a key area for the placement plan, and Northern Ireland's guidance says a child's first name only should be used in the wider media where that is specified.

The child's views and the parents' views

Delegated authority is not only about adults. England's guidance says any decision about delegation of authority must consider the views of the child, and that councils should, where appropriate, think about asking the child who they would like to make particular decisions. The Fostering Network adds that a child of sufficient age and understanding should be supported to make decisions themselves.

Parents do not lose parental responsibility when their child is looked after. The guidance says the views of parents and others with parental responsibility should be followed unless that would not be consistent with the child's welfare, and where a parent cannot take part in the discussion the council will need to take a view. Agreement from parents should be sought in advance and recorded in the placement plan.

The Fostering Network lists the factors that shape who decides: the young person's age, views, legal status and care plan, the parents' views, and the experience and views of the foster carers.

When it is reviewed, and what to do if you disagree

Delegated authority is not fixed. England's guidance says decisions about delegation should be considered at each review of the child's care plan, and the review checklist asks whether the delegation of authority to make decisions about the child's care is still appropriate. As a child settles, gets older or moves to a long-term plan, more decisions are often passed to the carer.

If you think you should be able to make a decision that is not delegated to you, or the arrangements are causing a child to miss out, raise it with your supervising social worker and the child's social worker, and ask for it to be discussed at the next review. The Fostering Network says foster carers should be invited to formal and informal discussions about the child, other than in some safeguarding situations. The guidance on reviews says every effort should be made to sort out disagreements informally first.

In an emergency, such as an accident, act to keep the child safe and get medical help, then tell the child's social worker and your fostering service as soon as you can. Your fostering service will tell you how to reach someone out of hours.

Across the UK

England
Delegated authority is covered by the statutory guidance Children Act 1989 guidance and regulations Volume 2: care planning, placement and case review (2021 edition, paragraphs 3.192 to 3.223), which absorbed the 2013 Department for Education guidance on delegation of authority. The placement plan required by regulation 9 of the Care Planning, Placement and Case Review (England) Regulations 2010 must say who can decide in key areas of the child's life, and each council should publish its own delegation policy.
Scotland
The Scottish Government's Decision Making Guidance for Foster Carers (published October 2015 as a consultation paper, prepared with the Scottish Child Law Centre) explains that delegated authority is not a term used in Scottish law. In Scotland, people caring for children who are not their own have powers of decision making in their own right, within the framework of the Children (Scotland) Act 1995, the Age of Legal Capacity (Scotland) Act 1991 and orders made by courts and children's hearings. A child under 16 can consent to medical treatment if a doctor judges they understand it.
Wales
The Welsh Government has issued guidance called Delegated Authority to Foster Carers, which sets out the process for listing the everyday decisions foster carers can make for the children in their care. Fostering services in Wales are regulated under the Regulation and Inspection of Social Care (Wales) Act 2016, and each council and agency writes its own delegation policy. Ask your service, or Foster Wales if you are fostering with a council, for the policy that applies to you.
Northern Ireland
The Department of Health, Social Services and Public Safety issued Guidance on Delegated Authority to Foster Carers in Northern Ireland (Circular CCPD 01/10, 2 February 2010) under Article 5(8) of the Children (Northern Ireland) Order 1995. Foster carers exercise authority on behalf of the Health and Social Care Trust, which keeps the responsibility. Delegation is agreed in writing at the looked-after child review; most decisions are delegated once the plan is long-term fostering or after a year in placement, though some, such as school day trips and photographs, can be delegated earlier. Holidays of more than five days must be agreed in advance, and authority goes back to the Trust or parent when a placement ends.

Common questions

Do foster carers have parental responsibility?

No. Foster carers never hold parental responsibility for a child in foster care. It stays with the parents and, depending on the child's legal status, the council or Trust. You make decisions using authority they have delegated to you, which should be set out in the child's placement plan.

Can I sign a school trip consent form?

Usually, yes. In England the statutory guidance says decisions about routinely risk-assessed activities such as school trips should be delegated to the carer, and gov.uk lists school trips as a day-to-day decision. Check the child's placement plan to be sure.

Can I take my foster child on holiday abroad?

Possibly, but not without checking. If holidays are not delegated to you, speak to the child's social worker first and tell them when you leave and return. For travel abroad, gov.uk says you will need a consent letter from the social worker for passport control. Your service will explain its own process.

What if the placement plan does not say who decides?

Contact your fostering service, as gov.uk advises. Your supervising social worker can find out, and can ask for delegated authority to be clarified at the child's next review.

Does delegated authority change over time?

Yes. It should be looked at again at each review of the child's care plan. As a child settles, gets older or moves to a long-term plan, carers are often given more everyday decisions. In Northern Ireland, regional guidance says most decisions are delegated once the plan is long-term fostering or after a year in placement.
