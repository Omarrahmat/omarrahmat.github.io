Can you choose the age of child you foster?

Yes, in most cases. You agree the ages and number of children you will look after with your fostering service during your assessment, and that becomes part of your approval. You can also turn down a child who would not fit your family. But the more open you are, especially to older children and brothers and sisters, the sooner you are likely to be matched. Here is how it works across the UK.

The short answer

You can usually say which ages of children you want to foster, and fostering services take that preference seriously. Barnsley Council answers the question directly: yes, you can choose the age of the child you want to foster, and it adds that it also welcomes carers who are happy to foster any age. Wigan Council says the same, and explains that your social worker will talk through the most suitable age ranges with you. Manchester City Council says it looks for carers for children from birth to 18, but that it considers your preferred age range when matching.

Your preference is not simply a wish you write on a form. It is discussed, tested and agreed during your assessment, and the result is written into your approval as a foster carer. After that, a fostering service should only ask you to care for children who fall within what you have been approved for, unless you agree to a change.

There is a balance to strike, though. The Fostering Network, the UK's largest fostering charity, encourages people to keep an open mind, because finding the right fit between a carer and a child matters more than any single detail. A very narrow age range can mean a longer wait before a child comes to live with you.

How your age range is agreed during the assessment

During your assessment (sometimes called the Form F assessment in England and Wales), your assessing social worker gets to know you, your household and your experience. The Fostering Network says that at this stage you will usually discuss the number and age of children you may wish to be approved to foster. Wigan Council says the right age range depends on your own experience, skills and preferences, and on the ages of any children you already have.

Things a social worker is likely to explore with you include:

  • Your experience with children of different ages, whether as a parent, a relative, or through work or volunteering.
  • The ages of any children living in your home, and how a younger or older foster child would fit alongside them.
  • Your working pattern. Manchester City Council, for example, says that if you want to care for children under five, one carer must foster full-time.
  • Your health and energy, and whether you smoke. Wigan Council says carers who smoke will not be able to foster children under five or children with additional health needs.
  • Your space. The Fostering Network notes that someone without a spare room might still be able to offer short break care or foster a very young child.
  • What you would find rewarding, and what you honestly think you would find hard.

Terms of approval: your agreed age range in writing

Once your assessment is finished, it goes to a fostering panel, which makes a recommendation, and then a decision maker at the fostering service decides whether to approve you. In England, the Fostering Services (England) Regulations 2011 say the panel can recommend the terms on which you are approved, and that the assessment report must include the service's proposals for those terms. When you are approved, the service must give you written notice of any terms of your approval, and must sign a foster care agreement with you.

In practice, the terms of approval usually describe the kind of fostering you will do, the age range of children and how many children you can care for at once. Kirklees Council's fostering procedures, for example, describe the terms as reflecting the nature and number of children a carer is suitable to look after, based on the carer's preferences and assessed skills.

Your fostering service also keeps a register of its foster carers, which records your current terms of approval. This matters because it is what the service checks when it is looking for a carer for a particular child.

Can you change your age range later?

Yes. Your approval is reviewed regularly, and the age range can be widened or narrowed as your circumstances and confidence change. In England, the regulations require a review of every foster carer no more than a year after approval and then at least once a year. The fostering service must seek your views, and the review report must say whether your terms of approval are still appropriate. The first review must go back to the fostering panel.

If the service thinks your terms should change and you do not agree, it must tell you in writing what it proposes and why, and you have 28 days to make written representations or to ask for an independent review. When terms are revised, you are given the new terms in writing with the reasons.

Local procedures fill in the detail. Kirklees Council, for instance, says terms are not normally changed in a carer's first year except in exceptional circumstances, that some changes such as a new age range can be agreed by a fostering manager at the annual review without going to panel, and that a signed agreement is always made with the carer. Many carers start with a narrower range and widen it once they have some experience, so your first choice does not have to be forever.

Can you say no to a child?

Yes. When a fostering service thinks a particular child could live with you, it will usually tell you about them and ask whether you feel you can meet their needs. The Fostering Network says a foster carer has the right to turn down a proposed fostering arrangement if they don't feel it is appropriate for them, and that you will not have to say yes to a child coming to live with you if you feel you cannot meet their needs.

Saying no is part of good matching, not a failure. A placement that starts with doubt is more likely to end early, which is hard on the child. It does help to explain your reasons honestly to your supervising social worker, so the service can understand what would work for you next time. If you find you are turning down most of the children you are offered, that is a good moment to talk about whether your age range or approval still fits.

Which ages most need foster carers?

Children can be fostered from birth right up to their 18th birthday, and services look for carers across the whole age range. But the children waiting for homes are, on average, older than many people expect.

In England, the Department for Education's statistics show that 81,770 children were looked after on 31 March 2025, and 54,820 of them (67%) were living with foster carers. The department describes children in care as mainly from an older age group: 27% were aged 16 or over, and over the last five years the number aged 16 and over rose by 17%, while the number aged one to four fell by 14% and the number under one fell by 8%. The Fostering Network says that around two-fifths of children in care are aged 11 to 15.

The Fostering Network says finding foster carers for teenagers is a key priority for a number of UK fostering services, alongside carers for brothers and sisters who need to live together, children with learning difficulties or disabilities, young parents and their babies, and unaccompanied asylum-seeking children. Wigan Council says it often needs carers for sibling groups of two or three, and that assessments may be fast-tracked for people who want to foster teenagers, sibling groups or children with complex needs.

So if you are open to older children or to brothers and sisters, you are likely to be matched more quickly, and many services offer extra training and support for those placements. Barnsley Council, for example, says it provides training and support for the age group you choose to foster.

Other things you can usually state a preference about

Age is not the only thing you can talk through. The Fostering Network says you may be able to express a preference based on things such as language, religion or disability, and services will ask whether you are interested in a particular type of fostering, such as short breaks, short-term, long-term, emergency or parent and child fostering. The number of children is part of your approval too: Wigan Council says that if you have the time and space, especially bedroom space, you can foster more than one child.

The national and council sources we checked do not set out a rule on choosing a boy or a girl. If that matters to you, perhaps because of the children already in your home or your bedroom arrangements, raise it with the service at your first conversation; they can tell you how they would approach it.

Whatever your preferences, the fostering service is looking for the best match for the child. Foster Wales puts it simply: you should be matched with a child who is right for your personality, skills and family situation, and it tries to keep children in their local area where it can.

Across the UK

England
The Fostering Services (England) Regulations 2011 require the fostering service to give you written notice of any terms of your approval, to review your approval at least once a year, and to ask for your views on whether the terms remain appropriate. Changes you do not agree with must be proposed in writing, with 28 days for you to respond or seek an independent review.
Scotland
Fostering panels make the decision on approval, and mygov.scot says your approval is reviewed at a panel after the first year and then every two years. Each council or agency agrees the ages and types of fostering with you during the assessment, which mygov.scot says can take 6 to 12 months.
Wales
Foster Wales, the national network of the 22 council fostering teams in Wales, says it works to match you with a child who suits your personality, skills and family situation, and tries to keep children local. Ask your local Foster Wales team which ages it most needs carers for.
Northern Ireland
nidirect explains that a social worker assesses you and a fostering panel recommends whether you can become a foster or kinship foster carer. It does not set out rules on age ranges, so the health and social care trust or agency you apply to will agree these with you during your assessment.

Common questions

Can I choose to foster only babies or young children?

You can say so, and many services will consider it, but babies and young children are a smaller share of children in care, so you may wait longer for a match. Some services also have conditions for under-fives, such as one carer being at home full-time or a smoke-free household.

Can I choose to foster teenagers?

Yes, and many services would welcome it. The Fostering Network says finding carers for teenagers is a key priority for a number of UK fostering services, and some councils, such as Wigan, may fast-track assessments for people who want to foster teenagers.

What happens if a child outside my age range needs a home?

Your service should only place children who fit your terms of approval. If it asks you to care for a child outside them, for example to keep brothers and sisters together, your terms would need to be changed with your agreement, and you can say no.

Can I change my mind about the ages I foster?

Yes. Your approval is reviewed regularly (in England at least once a year), and you can ask to widen or narrow your age range at review. Some services avoid changes in your first year unless there is a good reason.

Do I have to accept every child I am offered?

No. The Fostering Network says foster carers have the right to turn down a proposed fostering arrangement if they feel it is not right for them or that they cannot meet the child's needs.
