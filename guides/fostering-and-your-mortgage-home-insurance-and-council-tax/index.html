Fostering and your home: mortgages, home insurance and council tax

Becoming a foster carer touches three household bills most people never think about until they apply: the mortgage, the home insurance and the council tax. Here is what changes, what usually does not, who to tell, and how the rules differ across England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland.

The short answer

For most households, fostering does not mean a new mortgage, a specialist insurance policy or a bigger council tax bill. What it usually means is a few conversations: with your home insurer, possibly with your mortgage lender if you are borrowing, and with your council about whether a foster carer discount or exemption applies where you live.

The Fostering Network, the UK's leading fostering charity, says you do not need specialist home insurance to foster, but most fostering services will expect you to tell your insurer that you are fostering. On mortgages, the charity explains that lenders differ a great deal in how they count fostering income, so the choice of lender can change how much you can borrow. On council tax, there is no national foster carer exemption anywhere in the UK, but a growing number of councils in England choose to give their own foster carers a discount or a full exemption.

This guide is general information, not financial, legal or tax advice. Your insurer, your lender or a regulated mortgage adviser, your council's council tax team and your fostering service can confirm what applies to you.

Home insurance: who to tell and why

The most common question from people starting out is whether fostering will affect their home insurance. The Fostering Network's guidance is that a standard home insurance policy can be enough, but you should let your insurer know that you foster. It notes that telling an insurer may make no difference to the policy or the premium, and suggests talking to insurers about your fostering role so that you get the cover you need.

The charity also points out a few areas where standard policies can fall short for foster families, and suggests asking about them before you buy or renew. Some insurers may treat a home where fostering takes place as business premises. A foster child's own belongings may need to be treated as contents of the home. Damage caused deliberately, or theft by someone living in the home, may be excluded or may need a crime reference number before a claim is paid. Some insurers ask to be told when children arrive or leave, or about children with a history of causing damage. A standard policy may also not cover visitors who come because of your fostering role, such as social workers.

Specialist policies for foster families exist and are promoted by The Fostering Network to its members. Whether you need one depends on your existing cover, the children you are approved to care for and what your fostering service will reimburse. If you keep a standard policy, The Fostering Network suggests asking your fostering service how it would reimburse you for losses your insurer will not cover.

  • Tell your insurer that you foster, in writing, and keep a copy of their reply.
  • Ask whether there is a limit on the number of foster children in the home, and whether you must report each new placement.
  • Check how accidental and malicious damage, and theft by a member of the household, are treated.
  • Ask your fostering service what it covers, and how, for damage or loss caused by a child in placement.
  • Check that visitors who come because of fostering are covered under your liability section.

Mortgages: does fostering affect your current mortgage?

If you already own your home with a mortgage, fostering does not normally mean changing it. People starting the assessment are often worried that their lender must approve it first; the guidance we checked does not say that, but mortgage terms vary, so if you are unsure, read your mortgage offer or ask your lender. This is particularly worth doing if you plan to make changes to the home, such as converting a room, as those may need the lender's agreement in their own right.

Where fostering makes a real difference is when you apply for a new mortgage or remortgage, because the lender then has to decide how to treat the money you receive from fostering.

Getting a mortgage on fostering income

The Fostering Network's mortgage guidance explains that lenders take very different views of fostering income. Some will calculate how much you can borrow using all of your fostering income. Many high street lenders instead use your net profit from fostering, which is what is left after the foster care allowances are taken off. The charity notes that this gives a much lower income figure, and so a lower borrowing limit, for the same household.

Lenders also want to see a track record. The charity says most high street lenders expect at least six consecutive months of fostering income, while some lenders outside the high street may accept three months, often at a higher interest rate. It says the evidence lenders ask for is likely to be remittance statements from your fostering service, and that the best approach is to ask each lender what paperwork it needs. It also mentions a deposit of at least five per cent for a home to live in, and at least 25 per cent for a buy-to-let.

Because the gap between lenders can be large, people relying on fostering income often use a mortgage broker who knows which lenders count it in full. If you are thinking of moving home in order to foster, or soon after you start, it is worth getting this advice before you commit, and talking to your fostering service about timing: a move during assessment or during a placement affects the child and the service as well as you.

  • Ask lenders whether they use your full fostering income or your net profit after allowances.
  • Keep every remittance statement and your annual statement of payments from your fostering service.
  • Expect to need around six months of fostering income for most high street lenders.

Council tax: the basics that already help foster families

Council tax is charged on the home, and the bill depends partly on how many adults live there. In England, GOV.UK explains that you get 25 per cent off your bill if you live on your own or if everyone else in your home is disregarded, and that people under 18 are among those who are disregarded. That means a single foster carer living with foster children under 18 can still claim the single person discount, because the children are not counted. You have to apply for it; it is not automatic.

Things change when a young person in your home turns 18. They are then counted unless another disregard applies, for example being 18 or 19 and in full-time education. Wales and Scotland also exempt or disregard care leavers in some circumstances: the Welsh Government lists care leavers aged 18 to under 25 among those who can be exempt or disregarded, and mygov.scot lists a care leaver under 26 among the adults who are not counted. If a young person stays with you after 18, check the rules with your council so that any discount continues correctly.

Fostering itself does not count as the full-time carer disregard in the national rules. Both GOV.UK and the Welsh Government describe the carer disregard as applying to someone who cares for a person who is not their partner, spouse or child under 18, and neither page names foster carers. Any council tax help aimed specifically at foster carers therefore comes from individual councils, not from the national scheme.

Council tax discounts and exemptions for foster carers

Councils in England have the power to give their own discounts. The Fostering Network's council tax exemption briefing, published in July 2026 as part of its cost of fostering campaign, says that only a quarter of local authorities in England offer some form of council tax exemption for foster carers, and 16 per cent offer a full exemption. The briefing records that the government has said individual councils can provide discounts, but that it will not create a national disregard for foster carers. The charity is campaigning for all foster carers in England to be exempt.

Where a scheme exists, it is usually tied to fostering for that council. Some examples from councils' own pages show how the rules vary. Camden gives a council tax exemption to foster carers living in Camden and fostering for Camden, starting seven days after fostering begins, with no application needed. Liverpool gives a 100 per cent reduction to registered foster carers from its in-house pool who are actively fostering or available to foster, and continues it while a young person is staying put until they turn 21. Southwark's page says carers registered with Southwark can get 100 per cent relief, renewed each year with a letter from its fostering service. Islington's page says approved foster carers living in Islington do not have to pay council tax there.

These schemes change, and some council pages are several years old, so always check your own council's current page or ask its council tax team. If you are choosing between fostering for your council and fostering with an independent agency, ask both what support comes with the role, as a council tax exemption offered only to a council's own carers can be worth a significant sum each year.

  • Search your council's website for "council tax foster carers" to find any local scheme.
  • Check whether it covers only the council's own carers, and whether you must live in the area.
  • Note when it starts (for example, after a child has been placed) and whether you must reapply each year.
  • Claim the single person discount separately if you are the only adult counted in your home.

Other housing costs and help to know about

If your household is on a low income, help with council tax is assessed separately from any fostering discount. mygov.scot, for example, describes a Council Tax Reduction scheme of up to 100 per cent that takes into account whether there are children living in the household. In England and Wales, councils run their own council tax reduction schemes. How fostering payments are treated for these schemes and for benefits is a specialist question: our guide to fostering and benefits explains the basics, and your council or an advice service such as Citizens Advice can look at your own circumstances.

If you rent rather than own, the questions are different again: landlord permission, room sizes and housing benefit rules. Our guide on fostering if you rent covers those. Whatever your housing, the practical rule is the same: tell the people who need to know that you foster, keep the paperwork your fostering service sends you, and ask before you assume.

Across the UK

England
There is no national foster carer exemption. Councils can offer their own discount or exemption, and The Fostering Network's July 2026 briefing says a quarter of councils in England offer some form of exemption and 16 per cent a full one, usually for the council's own carers. The standard single person discount is 25 per cent, and children under 18 are not counted.
Scotland
Council tax in Scotland has its own rules, set out on mygov.scot. A single adult discount applies if you are counted as the only adult in the home, and a care leaver under 26 is among those not counted. mygov.scot says nothing specific about foster or kinship carers, so ask your council whether it offers any local help.
Wales
The Welsh Government's guidance lists care leavers aged 18 to under 25 as exempt or disregarded, and a home occupied only by people aged 17 or under is exempt. It does not name foster carers in the national rules, so any foster carer discount would be a local decision: check with your council.
Northern Ireland
Northern Ireland has domestic rates rather than council tax. Rates are a property tax based on the capital value of the home and are collected by Land & Property Services. The nidirect guide to rates lists help such as the Rate Rebate Scheme, Lone Pensioner Allowance and Disabled Person's Allowance but nothing specific to foster carers, so ask Land & Property Services or your fostering service about your own position.

Common questions

Do I need special home insurance to become a foster carer?

Not necessarily. The Fostering Network says you do not need specialist home insurance to foster, but most fostering services expect you to tell your insurer that you foster. Check how your policy treats damage, theft and visitors, and ask your fostering service what it would reimburse.

Can I get a mortgage if my income comes from fostering?

Yes, but lenders differ. According to The Fostering Network, some lenders count all of your fostering income, while many high street lenders count only your profit after allowances, which gives a lower figure. Most expect around six months of fostering income. A mortgage broker who knows fostering income can help you find the right lender.

Do foster carers have to pay council tax?

Usually yes: there is no national exemption anywhere in the UK. But some councils, mainly in England, give their own foster carers a discount or a full exemption. Check your council's website or ask its council tax team.

Are foster children counted for council tax?

Children under 18 are not counted in England, so a single carer with foster children can still get the 25 per cent single person discount. When a young person turns 18 they may be counted unless a disregard or care leaver rule applies, so tell your council.

Do I need to tell my mortgage lender that I foster?

The guidance we checked does not say fostering requires your lender's consent, but mortgage terms vary. If you are unsure, read your mortgage offer or ask your lender, and always ask before making changes to the property.
