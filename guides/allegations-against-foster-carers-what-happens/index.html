What happens if an allegation is made against a foster carer?

Allegations are one of the hardest parts of fostering to think about. This guide explains, step by step, how an allegation is handled, what support you can get, the possible outcomes, and how the process differs across England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland.

The short answer

If someone says that a foster carer, or anyone else living in the fostering household, has harmed a child, may have harmed a child or has behaved in a way that suggests they are not safe to care for children, the concern has to be looked into under local child protection procedures. That is the law in every part of the UK, and it applies whoever raises the concern and whatever the carer's record.

In England the government's guidance for foster carers says that when an allegation is made against you or someone in your home, the local authority must investigate it, support you through it, keep you updated on how it is going and help to resolve any disagreements. It also says plainly that the local authority may move your foster child or ask the person the allegation is about to leave the home, and that it will look at the safety of any other children living there.

An investigation is not a finding of guilt. The Fostering Network stresses that the process does not decide innocence or guilt, and that even a substantiated outcome does not automatically mean someone can no longer foster. Many carers who have been through an allegation go on fostering. What helps most is understanding the process before it ever happens, keeping good records, and knowing where to get independent support.

Allegation, concern or complaint: why the word matters

Fostering services sort the worries they receive into different kinds, and each is handled differently. The Fostering Network describes a complaint as an expression of dissatisfaction, for example about how something was handled. A complaint goes through the service's complaints procedure and is not usually treated as an allegation unless it involves physical, emotional or sexual harm.

A concern, often called a standards of care or quality of care concern, is where the care given seems to fall below what is expected of an approved foster carer but does not reach the threshold for a safeguarding investigation. Suffolk's guidance for its carers explains that these are usually looked into by the fostering service itself through fact-finding, and can lead to a review of the carer's approval.

An allegation is the most serious category. The Fostering Network's definition covers three situations: behaviour that has harmed, or may have harmed, a child; a possible criminal offence against or involving a child; and behaviour that suggests the person may not be suitable to work with children. The exact legal definition differs between England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland, so your own fostering service's allegations procedure is the document to check.

Step by step: how an allegation is investigated

The Fostering Network sets out the usual stages, while noting that the order can change depending on how urgent the risk is, what information comes in and whether it arrives out of hours. In England and Wales a concern about a foster carer is passed to the local authority's safeguarding lead, usually called the Local Authority Designated Officer (LADO) or Designated Officer for Safeguarding. That person decides whether the formal safeguarding process should start.

The fostering service has a legal duty to report the concern; the duty to investigate sits with children's social care, under section 47 of the Children Act 1989 in England and Wales, the Children (Scotland) Act 1995 in Scotland, and Article 66 of the Children (Northern Ireland) Order 1995 in Northern Ireland. Where a crime may have been committed, the police lead their own enquiries alongside.

Foster carers are normally told on the same day that an allegation has been made, usually by the registered manager or their supervising social worker, but they may not be given details that could affect the investigation. Carers do not attend the strategy meetings themselves; their account is gathered separately, often in writing and at an interview. Suffolk, for example, asks carers to return a written account within five days of being told what the allegation is about.

  • A strategy discussion between senior staff from children's social care, the fostering service and the police, to decide whether a full strategy meeting is needed and whether anything must happen at once to keep the child safe.
  • One or more strategy meetings, usually chaired by the LADO, which plan who will make enquiries, what they will look at and roughly how long it should take, and whether a criminal investigation is needed.
  • The enquiries themselves: interviews with the child, the carer, household members and others, and a review of records such as daily logs.
  • An outcome meeting once enquiries are finished, where the outcome is agreed and then confirmed to the carer in writing as soon as possible.
  • A review of the carer's approval by the fostering service, which may go to the fostering panel.

Will the children be moved?

This is the question most carers ask first, and the honest answer is that it depends on the risk. Suffolk's guidance says most allegations do not require the child to be moved, and that a child is moved only if they are at immediate risk. Hertfordshire's leaflet says foster children should not automatically be removed. At the same time, FosterTalk's guide for carers, shared on Wandsworth Council's fostering site, warns that where an allegation is serious, foster children in the home are likely to be moved while it is investigated, and The Fostering Network says the likelihood of children being moved as a result of allegations is rising.

Sometimes the alternative to moving the child is for the person the allegation is about to leave the home for a while, so that the placement can continue. The government's England guidance mentions this option, and Suffolk's guidance says a carer or household member may choose to do this.

Your own children are also considered. FosterTalk's guide says the local authority must think about the welfare of any birth, adopted or other children living with you; in very serious cases normal child protection procedures apply to them too, and independent legal advice is strongly recommended. While an investigation is open, fostering services generally do not place new children with you; Suffolk puts any vacancies on hold, and Hertfordshire puts carers on hold if a child has had to move for safeguarding reasons.

Money, support and your rights during an investigation

Allowances and fees during an investigation are set by each fostering service's own policy, not by one national rule, so ask about them at the start. The local examples show how much they vary. Suffolk pays carers in full for 14 days if a child is moved as a direct result of an allegation, and longer by agreement if the child is expected to return. Hertfordshire says that fees can be paid for up to sixteen weeks where a carer is on hold. FosterTalk's guide advises carers to ask about payments early and to get benefits advice if their income is affected. In Scotland, the government's guidance says carers should be told what will happen to allowances and fees if children are moved or the carer is suspended.

Independent support matters because your supervising social worker works for the service that is part of the investigation. Suffolk's guidance points to National Minimum Standard 22.12 in England, which gives carers and their household a right to support that is independent of the fostering service, covering information and advice, emotional support (including at meetings and panels) and mediation. The Fostering Network runs an independent Advocacy and Mediation Service and says your fostering service should fund it; if the service has no arrangement in place, the charity asks it to buy the support for you.

Membership organisations also help. The Fostering Network's members can use a legal helpline that is open 24 hours a day, every day of the year, and its Fosterline service gives free, confidential advice to any foster carer, although it cannot act as your advocate or come to meetings. FosterTalk offers advocacy and legal expenses cover for its members. If the police want to interview you under caution, FosterTalk strongly advises asking for a legal representative.

You should also be kept informed. In England the government's guidance says the local authority must update you on progress, and The Fostering Network says the outcome must be given to you in writing.

How long it takes, and the possible outcomes

There is no fixed timescale. The Fostering Network says there are no prescribed timescales and that carers should expect things to move much more slowly than they would like. FosterTalk's guide says an investigation can take days, several weeks or many months, especially if the police are involved. Suffolk notes that the LADO can formally escalate delays.

At the end, the outcome is recorded using one of a set of terms. Councils use slightly different lists, but most are drawn from these:

  • Substantiated: there is enough evidence to show the allegation is true.
  • Unsubstantiated: there is not enough evidence to prove or disprove it. This does not imply guilt or innocence.
  • Unfounded: there is no evidence or proper basis for it, or evidence shows it did not happen. It may mean the person misread what happened.
  • False: there is enough evidence to disprove it.
  • Malicious: there is clear evidence of a deliberate intention to deceive. The Fostering Network's new Allegations Toolkit recommends dropping this term, following NSPCC guidance, so some services no longer use it.

After the outcome: records, reviews and panel

Fostering services must keep a record of every allegation, how it was investigated and the outcome. After an unfounded outcome, The Fostering Network says the service should give the carer a chance to talk through the impact on the household and agree any extra support. After a substantiated or unsubstantiated outcome, the service reviews whether the carer is still suitable to foster, which may go to the fostering panel, with the final decision made by the agency decision maker after consulting the carer.

The possible decisions are to continue as before, to continue with changes such as different terms of approval or more training, or to end approval. Hertfordshire's leaflet says the panel must be told of every allegation whatever the outcome, and that carers get copies of the reports beforehand and can respond. Suffolk gives timescales for its own panels: a substantiated outcome within 28 working days, an unsubstantiated one within three months, and an unfounded, false or malicious one noted at the next annual review.

In England and Wales, if the service proposes to end or change your approval, you can make representations to the service or apply to the Independent Review Mechanism, and FosterTalk's guide notes that you have 28 calendar days to do so. Where an allegation is substantiated, a referral to the barring service may be made. Resigning during an investigation does not stop it: Hertfordshire and the Scottish guidance both say a conclusion is still reached and recorded.

What you can do to protect yourself and the children

No carer can rule out ever facing an allegation; children in care sometimes say things for reasons that have nothing to do with their carers, and real concerns must always be taken seriously. What every guide we read agrees on is that good habits make an allegation easier to deal with fairly, and help keep everyone safe.

  • Keep detailed daily records and share them with your supervising social worker, including anything unusual, injuries, marks or bruises and how they happened.
  • Agree a written safer caring plan for your household and update it for each child, covering things like bedrooms, bathrooms, personal care and who may be alone with whom.
  • Take the allegations and safer caring training your service offers, and refresh it.
  • Report serious incidents straight away rather than waiting for your next supervision.
  • Make sure every adult in the household knows the procedure and who to contact.
  • Before a child arrives, ask whether they have made allegations before, so the plan can take it into account.
  • Find out now whether your service funds independent support, and consider joining a membership body that includes legal advice.

Across the UK

England
Allegations are reported to the Local Authority Designated Officer (LADO) and investigated by children's social care under section 47 of the Children Act 1989, with the police where a crime may have been committed. The Fostering Network lists the Fostering Services (England) Regulations 2011, National Minimum Standard 22 (handling allegations and suspicions of harm) and Working Together to Safeguard Children as the main rules. Carers have a right to independent support, and the government's guidance says the local authority must investigate, support you, update you and help resolve disagreements.
Scotland
The Scottish Government's guidance, Managing allegations against foster carers and approved kinship carers (2013), applies alongside national child protection guidance and the Looked After Children (Scotland) Regulations 2009. It says responses should be proportionate to risk, carers should be treated fairly and told of progress, those involved should usually be told the result within three working days, and a panel considering ending or changing approval should meet within six weeks of the referral. The Care Inspectorate is notified at key points. Investigation records may show on later PVG checks.
Wales
Allegations are handled under the Wales Safeguarding Procedures, which have a section on allegations against practitioners and people in positions of trust, paid or unpaid. Care Inspectorate Wales announced revisions to that section in July 2026 to make the handling of allegations safer, fairer and more consistent. The Fostering Network also points to the Local Authority Fostering Services (Wales) Regulations 2018, the Regulated Fostering Services (Wales) Regulations 2019 and the Social Services and Well-being (Wales) Act 2014. The investigation duty sits with children's social care under section 47 of the Children Act 1989.
Northern Ireland
Allegations are investigated by the Health and Social Care Trust under Article 66 of the Children (Northern Ireland) Order 1995, with the police where needed. The Fostering Network notes that Northern Ireland currently has no fostering services standards of the kind England has, and it has called for a framework that includes guidance on allegations. Ask your Trust or agency for its written allegations procedure and for details of independent support before you need it.

Common questions

Will I be told what the allegation is?

You will normally be told on the same day that an allegation has been made, usually by your supervising social worker or the registered manager. You may not be given every detail at first if that could affect the investigation, but you will be asked for your account and the outcome must be confirmed to you in writing.

Will I still be paid during an investigation?

It depends on your fostering service's own policy, so ask at the start. Some councils keep paying for a set period if a child is moved; for example Suffolk pays in full for 14 days, and Hertfordshire can pay fees for up to sixteen weeks where a carer is on hold. If your income changes, get independent benefits advice.

Can I get independent support?

Yes. In England carers have a right to support that is independent of their fostering service. The Fostering Network's Advocacy and Mediation Service, usually funded by your service, offers advice, emotional support and mediation; Fosterline gives free confidential advice to any carer; and membership bodies such as The Fostering Network and FosterTalk include legal helplines for members. For advice about your own situation, speak to a solicitor.

Does an unsubstantiated outcome mean people think I did it?

No. Unsubstantiated means there was not enough evidence either way, and it carries no implication of guilt or innocence. Your service will still review your approval, which is a chance to agree any support or changes, and many carers continue to foster afterwards.

Can I resign to stop the investigation?

You can stop fostering at any time, but resigning does not end the investigation. Both Hertfordshire's guidance and the Scottish Government's guidance say a conclusion should still be reached and recorded.

Will an allegation stop me fostering for good?

Not necessarily. The Fostering Network says even a substantiated outcome does not automatically mean a carer can no longer foster. The fostering panel and agency decision maker look at what happened and decide whether to continue your approval, change it or end it, and you can respond to the reports first.
