What happens when a foster placement ends?

Every fostering arrangement ends at some point. Here is what usually happens when a child moves on, planned or unplanned, what support carers can expect, and what changes at 18 in each UK nation.

The short answer

A foster placement ends when the child moves on: home to their family, to long-term foster carers or adopters, to another type of care that suits their needs better, or into adult life. Most endings are planned, with time to prepare the child and say goodbye properly. Some are not, and a few have to happen quickly to keep a child safe.

Whatever the reason, you are not on your own. Your fostering service plans the move with the child's social worker, your supervising social worker supports you and your family through it, and afterwards there is time to reflect on what went well before you are matched with another child. For young people who are 18, each UK nation has a way for them to stay on with their foster family into early adulthood.

This guide is general information. How endings are handled, and what is paid after a child leaves, is set by each fostering service, so your service can confirm what applies to you.

Why foster placements end

The Fostering Network, the UK's leading fostering charity, lists the usual reasons a child leaves a foster home. Many of them are good news for the child, and some are exactly what the fostering arrangement was set up to achieve.

  • Going home to their birth family, often gradually and with the social worker keeping a close eye on how things go.
  • Moving to long-term foster carers or to adopters, after the child's plan has been agreed.
  • Joining brothers or sisters, or moving to carers or a setting better able to meet a particular need.
  • Moving into residential care where that is what the child needs.
  • Leaving care as a young adult, whether to live independently or to stay on with you under the arrangements for over-18s described below.
  • An unplanned ending, sometimes called a disruption or breakdown, when the arrangement cannot carry on as intended.

Planned endings: how a move is prepared

A planned ending is part of the child's care plan and is worked out with the child's social worker, your supervising social worker and, where appropriate, the people the child is moving to. Local authorities usually need time to plan introductions, so moves to adopters or long-term carers tend to happen in stages: meetings, visits and short stays before the child moves for good.

Your part is mostly practical and emotional. The Fostering Network suggests helping the child understand what is happening and when, sorting out their belongings, photos and keepsakes together, and making sure any savings put aside for them go with them. Many carers make a memory book of photos, letters and favourite moments, and life story work, usually led by the social worker with carers helping, gives the child a clear account of their time with you.

Argyll and Bute Council's guidance for its carers, for example, describes a gradual introduction for children moving to permanent carers or adopters and notes that the day of the move itself goes best when it is calm and not left until late in the day. Children going home may be unsettled for a while, and behaviour can slip back as they adjust, which is normal and something your social workers will expect.

When a young person is preparing to leave care, carers play a large part in helping them learn the everyday skills they will need, from cooking and budgeting to dealing with appointments. GOV.UK explains that young people leaving care in England have a personal adviser from 16 or 17 up to age 25 and extra support at 16, 18 and 21.

Unplanned endings and disruption meetings

Sometimes a fostering arrangement ends sooner than planned. The Fostering Network notes that social workers may first try to add support to avoid a move, and that some arrangements have to end straight away for safeguarding reasons. Unplanned endings are hard for everyone, and they do not mean a carer has failed: they are a known part of fostering, and services have processes to learn from them.

If you decide you can no longer care for a particular child, GOV.UK says carers in England must give 28 days' notice to their social worker. That notice period gives the service time to find the right next home and to plan the move rather than rush it.

When a long-term or permanent arrangement ends early, there is often a meeting afterwards to understand what happened. Suffolk County Council's guidance is a typical example: where a placement matched for permanence ends without a notice period or clear plan for the move, a disruption meeting should usually be held within six weeks. The supervising social worker arranges it, the foster carers and the child's social worker attend, and the child's views are sought. The meeting produces key learning points and actions, an account for the child of why the move happened, and a summary that goes to the fostering panel.

Argyll and Bute's guidance describes a similar process in Scotland: a disruption meeting, a review of the child's plan that former carers may be asked to join, and a review of the carers' own fostering that looks at the effect on them and their family and makes recommendations about their future fostering. The details vary by service, so ask yours how it handles unplanned endings before you need to know.

Keeping in touch after a child leaves

Many carers worry that a move means losing contact for good. The Fostering Network's position is that children should be helped and supported to keep relationships with their former foster families unless there is a good reason not to, and it runs a campaign, Keep Connected, on exactly this.

In practice, whether and how you stay in touch is decided as part of the child's plan, with their wishes, safety and the views of their new family or carers taken into account. It might mean letters or cards, a phone call, a visit, or simply passing on news through the social worker. Talk to the child's social worker early about what would help the child, and be honest with the child about what to expect.

How you might feel, and the support available

Saying goodbye to a child you have cared for can bring a real sense of loss, even when the move is the right one. The Fostering Network says a mix of sadness and happiness is normal, and encourages carers to take time to reflect on the ending, with their supervising social worker and with friends, family and other foster carers. Your own children may need support too, especially after a long arrangement.

In England, GOV.UK sets out the support fostering services provide: a supervising social worker, an out-of-hours advice service, support groups, training and a yearly development plan, and the chance of a break from fostering. Fosterline, a free advice line for foster carers in England, is open Monday to Friday, 9am to 5pm. The Fostering Network also offers training on managing children moving on, and advice lines for legal questions and stress counselling.

Your approval as a foster carer is reviewed at least once a year in England, and an unplanned ending may be looked at as part of that review or in a separate one. Many carers find a short gap between children helps them and their family recover before the next match. If you want a pause, say so: your service would rather you came back rested.

  • Ask your service before you start what support it gives after a child moves on.
  • Ask what happens to your fostering payments after a child leaves: some payment schemes include retainer payments in set circumstances, and the rules differ from service to service.
  • Keep your own notes of what worked well; they are useful at your annual review and for the next child.

When a young person turns 18

At 18 a young person is no longer in foster care in law, but that does not have to mean leaving home. Each UK nation has a scheme that lets a young person stay on with their foster family into adulthood if everyone agrees. In England it is called Staying Put, in Wales When I am Ready, in Scotland Continuing Care, and in Northern Ireland Going the Extra Mile (GEM). The details, including how long it lasts and what the carer is paid, differ by nation and by service.

The money is an area to check carefully. The Fostering Network reports that there is no minimum allowance for these arrangements, that many services pay carers less than they received for fostering and expect the young person to make up some of the gap with housing benefit, and that some carers have to be re-approved as foster carers when the arrangement ends. Ask your service what its own policy pays and whether your approval continues while a young person stays on.

Across the UK

England
Staying Put, which began in 2014, lets a young person stay with their former foster carers up to age 21. Guidance from the Department for Education, HMRC and the Department for Work and Pensions sets the framework each local authority follows in writing its own Staying Put policy. Carers must give 28 days' notice if they no longer wish to care for a child, and approval is reviewed at least once a year.
Scotland
Continuing Care, introduced in April 2015 under the Children and Young People (Scotland) Act 2014, lets a young person who stops being looked after on or after their 16th birthday stay in their foster, kinship or residential home with the same support until they turn 21. The Fostering Network notes it means staying with the same carer, not moving to another. Aftercare support is then available up to 26.
Wales
When I am Ready, which began in 2016, lets a young person stay with their former foster carers after 18, up to age 21. The Welsh Government publishes a good practice guide for the professionals who arrange it, and each council has its own policy.
Northern Ireland
Going the Extra Mile (GEM), run through the Health and Social Care trusts' fostering service, lets foster carers continue to care for young people aged 18 to 21 whom they have fostered, supporting them into education, training or work. The Fostering Network notes that the scheme has been placed in law but those provisions have not yet commenced.

Common questions

How much notice do I have to give to end a placement?

In England, GOV.UK says a foster carer must give 28 days' notice to their social worker if they no longer wish to care for a particular child. Services elsewhere in the UK set their own expectations in the foster carer agreement, so check yours. The notice period gives time to plan the child's move properly.

Will I still be paid after a child leaves?

It depends on your fostering service. Some payment schemes, such as West Sussex County Council's, include retainer payments in set circumstances, but not every scheme does. Your service's payment policy, or your supervising social worker, can tell you what applies to you.

Can I stay in touch with a child after they move?

Often, yes. The Fostering Network says children should be supported to keep relationships with former foster families unless there is a good reason not to. What contact happens is agreed as part of the child's plan, so raise it with the child's social worker before the move.

What is a disruption meeting?

A meeting held after a long-term or permanent foster placement ends earlier than planned, to understand what happened and learn from it. Foster carers and the social workers usually attend, the child's views are sought, and a summary normally goes to the fostering panel. It is not a disciplinary meeting.

Can a young person stay with me after they turn 18?

Usually, if everyone agrees and it is in their interests. England has Staying Put and Wales has When I am Ready, both up to 21; Scotland has Continuing Care up to 21; Northern Ireland has the GEM scheme for 18 to 21 year olds. Payments and your status as a carer during the arrangement depend on your service's policy.
