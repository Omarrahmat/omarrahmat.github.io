Can you work and foster? Fostering alongside a job

Yes, many foster carers also have a job. Whether it works for you depends on the type of fostering, the age of the child, your hours and how flexible your employer is. Here is what services expect, which kinds of fostering fit around work, and what to ask before you apply.

The short answer

You do not have to give up work to foster. GOV.UK says you may be able to work and foster in England, and that whether you can depends on the child's circumstances and on the fostering service you apply to. The Scottish Government's mygov.scot says you can foster whether or not you work full time, although some councils only allow certain types of fostering if you do. Foster Wales says that nearly 40 per cent of foster carers combine fostering with other work.

Councils that recruit carers say the same. Liverpool City Council's fostering service answers the question with a plain yes. Foster Wales Monmouthshire says there are no strict rules on working and fostering beyond putting the child's best interests first. Trafford Council says paid work can be possible depending on your fostering role, your support network and how flexible your employer is.

So the useful question is not whether working is allowed, but which kind of fostering would fit around your job, and what has to bend when a child needs you. Services judge this case by case, and they will want an honest picture of your hours, your commute and who could step in if you were held up.

Why fostering needs time in the working day

Fostering is more than caring for a child in the evenings and at weekends. GOV.UK lists attending meetings, going to training, keeping records and writing reports among a foster carer's responsibilities. Many of these happen in office hours, and some cannot be moved to suit you.

Councils describe the same pressure points. Foster Wales Monmouthshire lists regular meetings with your social worker, medical appointments, school meetings and training, and says you need flexibility when a child is unwell or during school holidays. Liverpool mentions collecting a child from school every day and taking children to contact with their family. Trafford asks carers to talk to their supervising social worker about how their hours might affect their availability for meetings, school runs and emergencies.

Action for Children, a national charity that runs a fostering service, points out that the early weeks of a placement take a lot of time, because you are building a relationship with a child who has just arrived. It says more time may free up once the child has settled, and that any change to your working pattern should be agreed with your social worker.

  • Meetings with your supervising social worker and the child's social worker
  • Reviews, school meetings and health appointments
  • Family time (contact) arrangements, which may involve driving the child
  • Training, especially in your first year
  • School drop-off and pick-up, sick days and school holidays
  • Short-notice calls from school or from the service

Which types of fostering fit around a job

The type of fostering you choose makes the biggest difference. Short breaks, sometimes called respite, are the easiest to fit around full-time work, because the child stays with you for set periods rather than living with you all the time. Liverpool gives examples such as one weekend a month or two weekends a month, and describes respite and short stays as suiting carers who have commitments elsewhere. Foster Wales Monmouthshire says short-break fostering or emergency weekend care lets carers keep a full-time job.

Day-to-day fostering, where a child lives with you for months or years, is more demanding of your time. Liverpool says carers who work full time are more suited to looking after a school-age child or young person, and that babies and pre-school children need you to be present more. Action for Children makes a similar point: a primary school child needs collecting from school and someone at home afterwards, while a secondary pupil who travels to school on their own frees up more of the day, although you still need to be reachable if the school calls and at home when they return.

Some types of fostering usually need a carer who is available full time. Action for Children notes that in some parts of the UK the main carer is required to be available full time, and mygov.scot says some Scottish councils restrict which types of fostering a carer who works full time can do. Parent and child placements, specialist or therapeutic fostering and care for very young children are commonly in this group, but the rules belong to each service, so ask rather than assume.

  • Usually easiest alongside full-time work: short breaks or respite, some weekend emergency care
  • Often possible with flexible or part-time work: school-age children and teenagers in short or long-term fostering
  • Often needs a carer at home: babies and pre-school children, parent and child, some specialist placements

Couples, single carers and who is the main carer

Where two adults foster together, a common pattern is for one to work full or part time while the other is the main carer. Action for Children describes this arrangement, and services will usually ask which of you will be available for the school run, appointments and emergencies.

Single carers can work too. Action for Children says single carers may take outside work to supplement their fostering income, provided it does not harm the child's welfare. Because there is no second adult to cover the day, a strong network of family and friends matters even more. Liverpool describes a good support network as valuable for carers who work. Our guide to fostering as a single person covers this in more detail.

Whatever your household, the fostering assessment will look at your routine. Being clear about your hours, how much flexibility you really have, and who could collect a child if you were delayed will help your assessor recommend the right kind of fostering for you.

Working from home, part time and self-employed

Flexible work tends to fit fostering best. Liverpool says some of its carers work full time because their job is flexible or home-based. Foster Wales Monmouthshire mentions home workers, freelancers, contractors and people who run their own business and can set their own hours, and says others work part time with enough flexibility to take almost any kind of placement.

Action for Children says working from home can be possible with flexibility, but you still need to be able to go to meetings and training and be there when the child comes home from school. A child at home is not a background task: if your job needs long, uninterrupted calls, think about how a sick day or a school closure would work.

Being self-employed alongside fostering raises its own questions about tax, National Insurance and record-keeping. These depend on your circumstances, and we do not give individual tax advice. HMRC, your fostering service or an accountant can explain how the rules apply to you, and our guide to foster carer pay and allowances explains how fostering payments are made up.

Time off work: what employers can offer

There is no general legal right to time off work for fostering. GOV.UK says your employer may let you take time off when a child first arrives or for training, but that you do not have a statutory right to it. The one exception GOV.UK gives is fostering for adoption, where the carer is entitled to adoption pay and leave from when the child comes to live with them.

Many employers go further voluntarily through The Fostering Network's Fostering Friendly scheme. Brighton & Hove City Council describes it as a scheme where employers adopt a fostering friendly HR policy offering flexible working and paid time off for foster carers to train and settle a child, at no direct cost to the employer. It reports that 40 per cent of people who combine fostering with work say a supportive employer is vital. Foster Wales says The Fostering Network's template policy offers five days of additional paid leave to foster carers and to people applying to foster, and lists dozens of member employers in Wales, from councils to national banks and retailers.

Large private employers have joined too. In 2023 The Fostering Network reported that the John Lewis Partnership had become the largest Fostering Friendly employer, giving foster carers an additional week of paid leave, and said the scheme then had more than 100 member organisations. Councils also sign up for their own staff: Brighton & Hove offers paid leave and flexible working to employees who foster, and Trafford offers flexible working and time off for training or settling a child.

Before you apply, check your employer's HR policies or ask HR directly whether they have a fostering policy or would consider joining the scheme. Exact terms vary from employer to employer.

Childcare and money

If you foster a young child and work, you may be able to get funded childcare in England. Redcar and Cleveland Council explains that working families can receive government-funded childcare for foster children aged nine months to four years, where the child is eligible and the local authority agrees. To qualify, each foster parent must work in paid employment outside fostering and earn at least the equivalent of 16 hours a week at the national living or minimum wage, and fostering itself does not count towards that work requirement. People earning over £100,000 do not qualify. Ask your fostering service and your local Families Information Service how the scheme works where you live.

Fostering allowances are paid to cover the cost of looking after a child, and many services also pay a fee for the carer's time and skills. nidirect, for example, says the allowance covers a child's costs and that some carers also receive a fee. Monmouthshire notes that a salary outside fostering means you are not relying on fostering as your only income, which can help during gaps between placements, when no allowance is usually paid.

How earnings from work and from fostering interact with tax and benefits depends on your circumstances. We do not give individual tax or benefits advice: HMRC, your fostering service, or Fosterline (England) can explain how the rules apply to you.

Questions to ask a fostering service

Each council and agency sets its own expectations on work, so it is worth asking the same questions of every service you are considering. The answers will tell you which types of fostering you could be approved for and how much flexibility you would need.

Our directory lets you compare council and independent fostering services in your area, and our guide to choosing between a council and an independent agency explains the differences.

  • Do you approve carers who work full time, and for which types of fostering?
  • Does the main carer need to be at home during the day for the placements you most need?
  • How many meetings and training sessions would I have in a typical month, and are any in the evening or online?
  • What happens if I cannot get to a meeting or school pick-up because of work?
  • Do you offer short-break or respite fostering that could start alongside my job?
  • What support do you give when a child is ill or excluded from school?

Across the UK

England
GOV.UK says you may be able to work and foster, depending on the child and the fostering service, and that you have no statutory right to time off except in fostering for adoption, which brings adoption pay and leave. Funded childcare for working foster carers of eligible children aged nine months to four years is explained by local councils such as Redcar and Cleveland.
Scotland
mygov.scot says you can foster whether or not you work full time, but some councils may only allow certain types of fostering if you do. Ask your local council fostering team or an agency about its own policy.
Wales
Foster Wales says nearly 40 per cent of foster carers combine fostering with other work and promotes fostering friendly employers, with a template policy giving five days of additional paid leave. Local Foster Wales teams, such as Monmouthshire, say there are no strict rules on working beyond the child's best interests.
Northern Ireland
nidirect sets out the fostering allowance rates, training and support for foster carers but does not set rules on working. Ask your Health and Social Care Trust or an independent agency how it approaches carers who work.

Common questions

Can I foster and work full time?

Often, yes, especially for short breaks or respite and for school-age children. Liverpool City Council says full-time workers are more suited to school-age children, while babies and pre-school children need a carer at home more. Some services require a main carer who is available full time for certain placements.

Do I get paid time off work to foster?

There is no statutory right to time off for fostering, according to GOV.UK, except adoption leave and pay in fostering for adoption. Many employers in The Fostering Network's Fostering Friendly scheme give extra paid leave, often five days a year under its template policy.

What kind of fostering suits people who work?

Short breaks or respite, such as one or two weekends a month, are the easiest to fit around a full-time job. Fostering older children who are at school all day can also work if your job is flexible.

Can I work from home and foster?

Yes, if you have enough flexibility. Action for Children says you still need to attend meetings and training and be there when the child gets home from school.

Will working affect my fostering allowance?

Fostering allowances are paid for the child's costs, and services set their own rules on fees. How your earnings and fostering payments interact with tax and benefits depends on your circumstances, so ask HMRC, your fostering service or Fosterline in England.
