Fostering and benefits: Universal Credit, Child Benefit and what changes when you foster

Fostering allowances are generally ignored when means-tested benefits are worked out, but fostering changes other parts of a claim too: work requirements, the bedrooms you are allowed for housing costs, Child Benefit and National Insurance. This guide explains the general rules and who can check your own position.

The short answer

Many people who are thinking about fostering already receive a benefit, or expect to claim one, and worry that fostering will cost them that support. In most cases the general rules work in a foster carer's favour. Guidance from The Fostering Network, the charity Turn2us and Law Centre NI all say that the allowance a fostering service pays to cover a child's care is ignored (in the jargon, disregarded) when means-tested benefits such as Universal Credit are worked out. Fostering is not treated as paid work for these benefits, and foster carers are not treated as self-employed for them, even though they are treated as self-employed for income tax.

Fostering does change other parts of a claim. It can reduce what the Department for Work and Pensions (DWP) expects you to do to look for work, it can allow you an extra bedroom when your housing costs are worked out, and it means you generally cannot claim Child Benefit or the Universal Credit child element for the child you foster, because the fostering allowance is meant to cover the child's costs instead.

This guide explains those general rules from the sources linked at the bottom. It is not advice about your own claim. Benefit rules are detailed, they change, and the outcome depends on your household, what your fostering service pays and how it describes those payments. Before you make a decision, ask your fostering service for a written breakdown of what it pays, and check your position with an independent welfare rights adviser, Citizens Advice, or the benefits advice many fostering services offer their carers.

How fostering payments are treated for Universal Credit and other means-tested benefits

Turn2us, in its guide for foster carers reviewed in September 2026, says that all of the money you get as a fostering allowance should be ignored as income for Universal Credit. The Fostering Network's guide to benefits goes further and says fostering income is disregarded for all means-tested benefits, naming Housing Benefit and Pension Credit as well as Universal Credit. Law Centre NI's adviser guide says the same for Northern Ireland: payments for a child's accommodation and maintenance are disregarded.

There is one important caveat. The Fostering Network notes that some payments connected with fostering may be treated as ordinary earnings rather than fostering income. Its example is being paid to deliver training to other foster carers. Fostering services also describe their payments in different ways, often splitting them into an allowance for the child and a fee or reward element for the carer. If your service pays anything beyond the basic fostering payments, or you are unsure which category a payment falls into, get specialist advice before you assume it will be ignored.

The Fostering Network also recommends that you tell the DWP about your fostering income on your claim, with a note that it should be fully disregarded, rather than leaving it off. That way the payments going into your bank account are not mistaken for undeclared wages later. Keep the payment statements your fostering service sends you; they are the evidence of what each payment was for.

  • Fostering allowances: generally ignored for Universal Credit and other means-tested benefits (Turn2us, The Fostering Network, Law Centre NI).
  • Payments for other work, such as training other carers: may count as earnings (The Fostering Network).
  • Tell the DWP about fostering payments on your claim and keep your payment statements.
  • Income tax is separate: see our guide to qualifying care relief for how fostering income is taxed.

Looking for work while you foster: Universal Credit work requirements

When you claim Universal Credit, the DWP places you in a group that sets what you must do to look for or prepare for work. Fostering is one of the circumstances that can reduce those requirements, because looking after a fostered child is recognised as a caring role.

Turn2us sets the general rules out by the age of the child you foster. If the child is under one, you should have no work-related requirements at all. If the child is aged between one and 16, you should only have to attend work-focused interviews, which are meetings with a work coach to talk about future plans rather than a requirement to search for jobs. If the young person is over 16, you may still be limited to work-focused interviews where they have care needs and your work coach agrees. The Fostering Network describes the same pattern and notes that single carers of a child aged 16 or over are usually expected to look for, or be available for, work.

Couples are treated differently. The Fostering Network explains that a couple who foster together nominate one of them as the lead carer. The reduced requirements apply to the lead carer; the other partner generally has the usual requirements for their circumstances.

Gaps between placements are common, especially in short-term, emergency and respite fostering. Turn2us says that if you have had a foster child in the last eight weeks and expect to have another placement, you should only have to attend work-focused interviews during the gap. The Fostering Network describes this as an unbroken period of eight weeks without a child placed before the full requirements apply, as long as you intend to continue fostering. Your work coach decides how this applies to you, so tell them as soon as a placement starts or ends.

  • Child under one: no work-related requirements (Turn2us).
  • Child aged one to 16: work-focused interviews only (Turn2us; The Fostering Network gives the band as one to 15 for single carers, so check the current rule with your work coach).
  • Young person over 16 with care needs: possibly work-focused interviews only, if your work coach agrees.
  • Between placements: work-focused interviews only for up to eight weeks if you expect another placement.
  • Couples: one lead carer gets the reduced requirements.

Housing costs and the extra bedroom

If you rent and get help with your rent through the housing element of Universal Credit or through Housing Benefit, the amount you get depends partly on how many bedrooms your household is allowed. Without a special rule, a bedroom kept for a foster child could count as spare and reduce your help with rent. This is the rule often called the bedroom tax in social housing, and the bedroom limit in the private rented sector.

Approved foster carers are allowed one extra bedroom. Turn2us says this applies even when no foster child is living with you, and that the extra allowance is one bedroom however many children you foster. Its Housing Benefit guide says the same applies between placements, and that in the private rented sector the overall rate is capped at the four-bedroom level. The Fostering Network gives the example of a couple fostering two children, who are allowed a two-bedroom home: one room for themselves and one extra for fostering. Any further rooms used by foster children count as spare.

There is a time limit between placements. Law Centre NI's guide says the extra bedroom continues for Universal Credit as long as it has not been more than 12 months since the last placement ended or since you were approved, and gives 52 weeks for Housing Benefit. If you go a long time without a placement, check where you stand. If your help with rent still falls short, The Fostering Network notes you can apply to your council for a Discretionary Housing Payment, a short-term top-up the council decides case by case.

If you are thinking of fostering and you rent, our guide to fostering if you rent covers landlord permission and the spare room question from the fostering service's side.

Child Benefit and the Universal Credit child element

This is the rule that surprises people most. HMRC's Child Benefit Technical Manual says a foster carer who receives payments from a local authority for a child cannot get Child Benefit for that child, and that this applies straight away, without the eight-week run-on that applies in some other situations. The manual applies the same rule to placements made by authorities in Northern Ireland and lists the fostering regulations for each part of the UK. The Fostering Network's guide puts it simply: foster carers are not entitled to Child Benefit for fostered children. Citizens Advice gives the same rule from the other side: a foster parent can only claim Child Benefit if the council is not paying towards the child's accommodation or maintenance.

Universal Credit follows the same logic. Turn2us says you cannot get any extra Universal Credit for the child you are fostering, and The Fostering Network says the child element is for your own dependent children, not fostered children. Law Centre NI notes an exception where a carer holds a residence order for the child, but that is no longer an ordinary fostering arrangement.

None of this affects Child Benefit or the child element for your own children, who remain part of your claim in the usual way. If you foster a child with a disability who receives a disability benefit and you care for them for at least 35 hours a week, The Fostering Network says you may be able to claim Carer's Allowance (Carer Support Payment in Scotland) or the Universal Credit carer element, and that fostering income is disregarded for Carer's Allowance. Law Centre NI says the same for Northern Ireland.

  • Child Benefit for a fostered child: not payable where the council or trust pays fostering allowances (HMRC).
  • Universal Credit child element for a fostered child: not payable (Turn2us, The Fostering Network).
  • Your own children: no change.
  • Disabled foster child: Carer's Allowance or the carer element may be possible (The Fostering Network, Law Centre NI).

National Insurance credits and your State Pension

Because most foster carers pay little or no tax on fostering income, they often pay no National Insurance on it either, which can leave gaps in their National Insurance record. Gaps matter because your record decides how much State Pension you get.

GOV.UK's page on National Insurance credits says that if you are a foster carer, or a kinship carer in Scotland, you can apply for Class 3 credits. For foster carers these are not listed as automatic: you apply using the form for parents and carers (CF411A) linked from that page. Your fostering service can confirm the dates you were approved. Parents getting Child Benefit for their own child under 12 receive credits automatically, and carers getting Carer's Allowance receive Class 1 credits automatically.

If you are already working and paying National Insurance, or getting credits another way, you may not need them. Check your National Insurance record on GOV.UK before you apply, and ask HMRC if you are unsure.

Pension age, the benefit cap and other benefits

Some foster carers are of pension age. The Fostering Network includes Pension Credit among the means-tested benefits where fostering income is disregarded, so receiving fostering allowances should not on its own reduce it.

The Fostering Network says foster carers are generally not affected by the benefit cap, the limit on the total amount of benefit a working-age household can receive in England, Scotland and Wales. It also says that fostering does not prevent a new claim for Employment and Support Allowance, and Law Centre NI notes that a foster carer receiving maintenance payments is not treated as being in full-time work for income-based Jobseeker's Allowance or Employment and Support Allowance.

Council Tax Reduction (Council Tax Support) is run by each council with its own scheme. The Fostering Network lists it among the means-tested benefits, but the details vary by area, so ask your council how it treats fostering payments. For childcare, The Fostering Network says carers in England who are in paid work outside fostering may be able to use the 30 hours of funded childcare for a foster child aged nine months to four, arranged through the child's social worker and local authority.

What to do before and after you start fostering

The most common problems come from changes not being reported, or from payments being misunderstood. A few practical steps avoid most of them.

  • Ask any fostering service you are considering for a written breakdown of its payments: which part is the allowance for the child, which is a fee, and whether there are other payments such as for training or mileage.
  • Ask whether the service offers benefits advice to its carers. Many do.
  • Before approval, get an independent benefits check from Citizens Advice, a welfare rights service or a charity such as Turn2us, using your real figures.
  • Tell the DWP (and your council, if you get Housing Benefit or Council Tax Reduction) when you are approved and every time a placement starts or ends.
  • Report fostering payments on your claim with a note that they should be disregarded, and keep every payment statement.
  • Apply for National Insurance credits for each year you foster if you need them.
  • Keep a simple record of placement dates; it helps with work requirements, the extra bedroom rule, National Insurance credits and your tax return.

Across the UK

England
Universal Credit, Housing Benefit and Child Benefit follow the GB rules described above. The Fostering Network says foster carers in England in paid work outside fostering may be able to use 30 hours of funded childcare for a foster child aged nine months to four, arranged through the local authority. Council Tax Reduction schemes are set by each council.
Scotland
Universal Credit and Child Benefit are UK-wide, but some benefits are devolved. The Fostering Network notes that Child Disability Payment and Adult Disability Payment replace DLA and PIP, Carer's Allowance is replaced by Carer Support Payment, and foster carers are not entitled to Scottish Child Payment for fostered children. GOV.UK says kinship carers in Scotland can also apply for National Insurance credits.
Wales
Universal Credit, Housing Benefit, Child Benefit and National Insurance credits follow the GB rules described above. Ask your council how its Council Tax Reduction scheme treats fostering payments.
Northern Ireland
Law Centre NI's adviser guide says fostering maintenance payments are disregarded, the extra bedroom continues for 12 months after a placement for Universal Credit (52 weeks for Housing Benefit), and foster carers generally cannot claim the child element for a fostered child. HMRC's Child Benefit rule also applies to placements by Northern Ireland authorities.

Common questions

Will fostering stop my Universal Credit?

Not by itself. Turn2us and The Fostering Network say fostering allowances are ignored as income for Universal Credit, so they should not reduce your award. Other payments, or changes to your household, can affect it, so get an independent benefits check with your own figures and report the change to the DWP.

Can I claim Child Benefit for a foster child?

Generally no. HMRC's Child Benefit manual says a foster carer receiving payments from a local authority for a child cannot get Child Benefit for that child. The same principle applies to the Universal Credit child element. Your own children are not affected.

Will I have to look for work if I foster and claim Universal Credit?

It depends on the age of the child and your household. Turn2us says there are no work-related requirements with a foster child under one, and work-focused interviews only with a child aged one to 16 and for up to eight weeks between placements. In a couple, only the nominated lead carer gets the reduced requirements. Your work coach decides.

Will I lose help with my rent for the foster child's bedroom?

Approved foster carers are allowed one extra bedroom for Universal Credit housing costs and Housing Benefit, including between placements. Law Centre NI's guide puts the limit at 12 months after a placement ends for Universal Credit. The extra allowance is one bedroom however many children you foster.

Does fostering count towards my State Pension?

Fostering does not build National Insurance automatically if you pay none on it, but GOV.UK says foster carers can apply for Class 3 National Insurance credits, which count towards the State Pension. You apply with the CF411A form for parents and carers.

Who can check my own benefits position?

Citizens Advice, a local welfare rights service, Turn2us, or the benefits advice many fostering services offer their carers. Ask before you are approved, with a written breakdown of what the fostering service pays.
