Can you foster if you smoke or vape?

Yes, in many cases, but smoking narrows the children you can care for. Most fostering services will not place children under five, disabled children or children with breathing or heart conditions with a smoking household, and many treat vaping the same way. Here is what the guidance says, how services apply it and what changes if you have recently quit.

The short answer

Smoking does not automatically stop you becoming a foster carer. No UK nation has a law that bars smokers from fostering, and plenty of fostering services approve carers who smoke. What smoking does is limit which children a service will place with you, and it will be discussed openly during your assessment.

The national position is clear about the direction of travel. A joint briefing from Action on Smoking and Health (ASH), ASH Wales and The Fostering Network, published in June 2022, says that adults in a caring role should not smoke, and that fostering services should move towards placing children only in smokefree homes, even where the carers themselves smoke outside. It allows for exceptions where an existing relationship outweighs the risk, such as a grandparent or family friend caring for a child they already know, as long as there is a plan to stop.

In practice, the biggest single rule you will meet is about young children. Councils across the UK say they will not place a child under five with a foster carer who smokes, and many apply the same rule to disabled children and to children with asthma, heart disease or glue ear. Some go further: Gateshead Council and Perth and Kinross Council ask for non-smokers for children up to the age of 11.

This guide explains where those rules come from, how vaping is treated, what counts as having quit, and the smoke-free car law that applies to every carer. Each fostering service writes its own smoking policy, so the service you apply to is the one that can tell you exactly how it would apply to your household.

Where the smoking rules come from

The fostering regulations in England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland require a medical report and a full assessment of every applicant, but they do not list smoking as a reason to refuse anyone. The rules you will meet come from national good-practice guidance, which each council and independent agency turns into its own written policy.

The most widely used guidance comes from CoramBAAF, the national membership body for adoption and fostering. The 2022 ASH briefing quotes its recommendations: children under five should not be placed with carers who smoke, and children with a disability that means they often cannot play outside or move away from smoking adults, children with breathing problems, and children with heart disease or glue ear should not be placed with smoking families.

Because each service writes its own policy, the details vary. One council may set its limit at age five, another at eleven. One may count vaping as smoking, another may not mention it. Reading the smoking policy of the services you are interested in, or asking for it at your first conversation, saves surprises later.

Which children you may not be able to foster

These are the groups that councils most often say they will not place with a carer who smokes, or who lives with someone who smokes. The rule usually covers everyone in the household, not just the main carer, so a partner or adult son or daughter who smokes counts too.

  • Children under five. Stockport Council, for example, says it cannot place a child under five with a foster carer who smokes, and Bolton and Wigan councils say the same.
  • Disabled children, particularly those who cannot easily play outside or move away from smoke. Coventry City Council and Torfaen County Borough Council both list them.
  • Children with asthma or other breathing problems, heart disease or glue ear. Coventry, Torfaen and Bolton all name these conditions.
  • Children up to 11 in some areas. Gateshead Council requires non-smokers for children up to 11 and for parent and child placements, and Perth and Kinross Council requires applicants for children up to and including 11 to be non-smokers and non-vapers.
  • Parent and child placements in some areas, as Gateshead's policy shows, because a baby is part of the placement.

What you can still do if you smoke

Many services welcome smokers to foster older children and teenagers, provided the home stays smokefree. Coventry City Council, for example, says a smoking applicant's assessment looks at the changes they will make, and its panel considers this, while Perth and Kinross Council sets out conditions for carers of young people aged 12 and over who smoke or vape.

Expect clear conditions. Coventry City Council's policy asks carers who smoke to do so only outside, and to keep the rooms children play, eat and sleep in free of smoke. Perth and Kinross Council asks those carers to smoke or vape outside the house and, if possible, out of sight of the young people. Gateshead Council says it still expects the home to be smoke free when you care for older children. Visitors are usually asked to smoke outside too.

Your assessing social worker will talk to you about your smoking, what changes you would make and how you would keep children away from smoke, and this goes in the report the fostering panel reads. Coventry's policy says this is checked again at supervision visits and at the annual review, so it is a continuing commitment rather than a one-off promise.

Some policies, such as Torfaen's and Perth and Kinross's, also expect carers to encourage a young person who smokes to stop, and the ASH briefing recommends that carers never provide cigarettes or e-cigarettes to anyone under 18.

How vaping and e-cigarettes are treated

This is where policies differ most. The 2022 ASH briefing describes e-cigarettes as significantly less harmful than smoked tobacco and recommends that fostering policies recognise this and support adults who vape to stay off cigarettes. It also says carers who vape should avoid doing it in front of children, and that non-smokers and children should be strongly discouraged from vaping.

Many councils, however, simply apply their smoking rules to vaping. Torfaen County Borough Council's policy says everything that applies to smoking also applies to e-cigarettes and vaping. Wigan Council says its under-five restriction includes e-cigarettes. Coventry City Council applies its smoking policy to e-cigarette use while accepting they can help people quit. Perth and Kinross Council treats smokers and vapers in the same way throughout.

So if you vape, do not assume it puts you in the non-smoker category. Ask the service directly how it treats vaping, whether it matters if you vaped to give up cigarettes, and whether the rules on young children apply to you.

If you have given up: the 12-month rule

Most services want to see that a former smoker has stayed stopped before placing the youngest or most vulnerable children. The usual period is 12 months. The ASH briefing recommends at least 12 months without smoking before someone is treated as a non-smoker, because the risk of starting again is highest in the first year, and it quotes CoramBAAF's guidance that carers who have stopped should not foster children in high-risk groups until they have given up successfully for at least a year.

Councils follow this closely. Stockport Council says former smokers must be free from smoking for 12 months before fostering children under five. Coventry City Council asks for 12 months before a household member counts as a non-smoker, Bolton Council for at least a year, and Torfaen and Perth and Kinross councils for at least 12 months. A few older policies use shorter periods: Hertfordshire County Council's 2013 policy, for example, set six months for under-fives.

If you are thinking about fostering and are trying to stop, it is worth starting the conversation now. The assessment itself usually takes several months, and a service can plan around the date you quit. Your GP or your local NHS stop smoking service can help you stop.

Smoking in the car is against the law with children on board

Whatever a service's own policy says, the law on cars applies to everyone. Since 1 October 2015 it has been illegal in England and Wales to smoke in a private vehicle that has someone under 18 in it, and it is also an offence for the driver not to stop someone smoking. The rule applies to any vehicle with a roof, even with the windows open, and the fine for each offence is £50, according to the government announcement at the time. Similar laws came into force in Scotland in 2016 and in Northern Ireland in 2022, according to the ASH briefing.

These laws cover tobacco, not e-cigarettes. Fostering services usually go further: Hertfordshire's policy says carers must not smoke in a car used to carry children, and Torfaen expects carers' homes and cars to be smoke and vape free. The ASH briefing recommends a smokefree home and car, including caravans and holiday homes.

What to do next

Be open about smoking or vaping from your first enquiry. It is a routine question, it will come up in your medical and your assessment, and services would far rather plan around it than discover it later. Being honest also helps you find the service whose policy fits your household.

  • Ask each service for its smoking and vaping policy before you apply, and check the age limits and the smoke-free period it uses.
  • Think about which children you hope to foster. If you would like to care for babies or young children, a household that smokes will usually need to stop for a year first.
  • If you smoke and want to foster older children or teenagers, ask what the service expects at home, in the garden and in the car.
  • If you are quitting, note your quit date and tell the service; it may affect when they can place younger children with you.
  • Your GP, your local NHS stop smoking service or the fostering service can talk through your own situation; this guide is general information, not medical advice.

Across the UK

England
No law bars smokers from fostering. Councils and agencies set their own smoking policies, usually based on CoramBAAF guidance: no smokers for under-fives, disabled children or children with breathing or heart conditions, and 12 months stopped to count as a non-smoker. Smoking in a car carrying anyone under 18 has been illegal since 1 October 2015.
Scotland
Policies are set locally. Perth and Kinross Council, for example, requires non-smokers and non-vapers for children up to and including 11, and 12 months stopped before former smokers foster high-risk groups. The ASH briefing records that smoking in cars carrying under-18s became illegal in Scotland in 2016.
Wales
Smoking in cars carrying under-18s has been illegal since 1 October 2015. Local policies apply: Torfaen County Borough Council will not place under-fives, disabled children or children with asthma, heart disease or glue ear with a smoking household, applies the same rules to vaping, and points carers to the Help Me Quit service.
Northern Ireland
As elsewhere, no regulation names smoking as a bar; the health and social care trust or agency sets its own policy. The ASH briefing records that smoking in private vehicles carrying under-18s became illegal in Northern Ireland in 2022.

Common questions

Can I foster if I smoke?

Often yes, but usually not children under five, disabled children or children with asthma, heart disease or glue ear. Many services place older children and teenagers with carers who smoke, as long as the home stays smokefree and nobody smokes near the child. Each service sets its own policy.

Does vaping count as smoking for fostering?

It depends on the service. National guidance from ASH and The Fostering Network sees vaping as much less harmful than smoking, but many councils, including Torfaen, Wigan and Perth and Kinross, apply the same rules to vaping as to smoking. Ask the service you are considering.

How long do I need to have stopped smoking to foster a baby or young child?

Usually 12 months. Most councils, following CoramBAAF guidance, ask former smokers to have stopped for at least a year before they place children under five or other high-risk groups. A few older policies use a shorter period.

What if my partner or another adult at home smokes?

The rules normally apply to the whole household. If anyone who lives with you smokes, the service will usually treat it as a smoking household when deciding which children it can place with you.

Can I smoke in the car when a foster child is with me?

No. Smoking in an enclosed private vehicle with anyone under 18 inside is illegal in every UK nation, and in England and Wales the driver can also be fined for not stopping someone smoking. Fostering services usually also expect your car to be smokefree.

Will a fostering service help me stop smoking?

Many will point you to support. Coventry gives carers information about where to get help to cut down or stop, Torfaen points to Help Me Quit in Wales, and Perth and Kinross encourages carers to seek advice from their GP. Your local NHS stop smoking service can also help.
