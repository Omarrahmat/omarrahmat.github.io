Can I foster if I rent? Landlords, spare rooms and tenancies explained

Renting does not stop you fostering. What matters is a settled home, a bedroom a child can call their own and, in most cases, your landlord's written agreement. Here is how it works with private landlords, councils and housing associations across the UK.

The short answer

Yes, you can foster if you rent. Official guidance in all four UK nations is clear that home ownership is not a requirement. GOV.UK says you do not need to own your home to foster in England; mygov.scot says you can foster in Scotland whether you rent or own; Foster Wales says renting is not a barrier; and nidirect says people in Northern Ireland can apply whether they own or rent.

What fostering services look at instead is whether your home is stable, safe and has room for a child. For a tenant that usually comes down to three practical questions: is your tenancy secure enough that a child placed with you will not have to move unexpectedly, will your landlord agree to you fostering, and do you have a spare bedroom. The rest of this guide takes each in turn, then covers insurance, benefits and moving home while fostering.

Every fostering service, council or independent agency, sets its own detailed policies on top of the national rules, so treat what follows as a guide to the questions to ask rather than the final word for your situation.

Why services care about stability rather than ownership

Many children in foster care have already moved more than once. A foster home that is itself at risk of ending suddenly would add to that, which is why services ask about your housing in detail during the assessment. Leicestershire County Council's guidance for renters, for example, says applicants should ideally be settled where they are, not planning frequent moves, with no rent arrears and no risk of eviction. Lewisham Council asks that applicants are settled in their home and have lived in or near the borough for more than a year.

None of this means you need a long fixed-term tenancy. A rolling tenancy with a landlord you have been with for several years can be just as reassuring to an assessor as a mortgage. Be ready to talk about how long you have lived there, how your tenancy works and whether you expect to stay.

In England, the rules for private renting changed on 1 May 2026 under the Renters' Rights Act. The government's guidance for private tenants says section 21 'no fault' evictions have been abolished, fixed-term assured tenancies have ended and existing ones have become rolling periodic tenancies, so a landlord now needs a valid legal ground, such as selling the property or rent arrears, to seek possession. That gives many private tenants more security than before, which is helpful to mention in an assessment. These changes do not apply to council or housing association tenants, and the rules are different in Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland.

Getting your landlord's permission

Most fostering services will ask for your landlord's written agreement before or during the assessment. Lewisham Council says it needs written permission from your landlord; Leicestershire says renting is not a barrier but you need your landlord's consent; Argyll and Bute Council says a rented home is fine with the landlord's agreement; and Action for Children's information pack for Scotland says you must get a private landlord's permission and tell them about the home safety checks that are part of the process.

It is worth raising this early, before you have spent months on an assessment. Read your tenancy agreement for any clauses about who may live in the property, the number of occupants or using the home for business, then write to your landlord or letting agent explaining what fostering involves. Many landlords simply have not been asked before and are reassured by a few plain facts.

Points that often help a landlord say yes:

  • Fostering is not a business tenancy or a lodger arrangement: a fostered child lives with you as part of your household.
  • You will have a supervising social worker and the child will have their own social worker, and placements are carefully matched and risk-assessed before a child arrives (points Leicestershire's guidance makes to landlords).
  • Your home will have a health and safety check before approval, and services such as Hertfordshire's repeat these checks each year.
  • You remain responsible for any damage, exactly as with any other tenancy.
  • Your fostering service can usually provide a short information sheet for landlords, and some, such as Leicestershire, publish one.

Council and housing association tenants

If you rent from your council or a housing association, the same principle applies: you can foster, and you should tell your landlord and check whether its tenancy conditions require its agreement. Argyll and Bute Council lists housing association homes alongside owned and privately rented homes as suitable for fostering.

Social tenants sometimes have an extra route to a bigger home. The government's statutory guidance to councils in England on allocating social housing says councils can give priority on welfare grounds to people who need to move to a larger home to foster or adopt a looked-after child, including those still being assessed. It suggests councils work with children's services and could set aside a quota of homes each year for people that children's services recommend. Because the guidance leaves the detail to each council, schemes vary widely: some councils have a dedicated policy, others none. Ask both your housing department and the council's fostering team what applies where you live.

The spare bedroom

For most types of fostering you will need a spare bedroom, and this is usually a bigger question for renters than the tenancy itself. GOV.UK says you will usually need one in England, mygov.scot says the same for Scotland while noting some councils make exceptions depending on the child's age, and Foster Wales says you need a spare room where a child can feel settled. Argyll and Bute asks for a spare bedroom for each child you foster.

In England the Fostering Services National Minimum Standards (standard 10.6) say each child over three should have their own bedroom. Sharing is possible only where each child's responsible authority agrees and each child has their own space in the room, after the service has considered the risk of bullying, any history of abuse and the children's own wishes. Worcestershire's bedroom-sharing policy, for instance, allows babies up to two to sleep in the carers' room. Lewisham notes that a spare room matters most if you want to foster children over five.

If you do not have a spare room yet, you can still talk to a service. Some types of care, such as short breaks for a family you already know, may be more flexible, and an initial conversation costs nothing. But be realistic: without a suitable room, most services will not start a full assessment.

Insurance and safety checks

Fostering services check that your home is safe for a child before you are approved and then regularly afterwards. Expect questions about smoke alarms, gas and electrical safety, how medicines and cleaning products are stored, windows, stairs and outdoor space. In a rented home some fixes may need your landlord's agreement or action, so if the check turns up anything that needs changing, ask your landlord early and keep copies of any safety certificates they give you.

You will also need to tell your insurers. Leicestershire County Council asks approved foster carers to inform their home, buildings, contents and car insurers and to get written confirmation that fostering is covered. As a tenant you will usually only hold contents cover, as the building is your landlord's to insure, which is another reason to keep your landlord informed. Leicestershire's guidance says premiums do not commonly rise, but check with your own insurer.

Housing costs, benefits and the extra bedroom

If you get help with your rent through Universal Credit or Housing Benefit, fostering may change how many bedrooms you are treated as needing. Under the Universal Credit rules (Schedule 4 to the Universal Credit Regulations 2013, as amended in 2013), a foster parent can be allowed one additional bedroom, and that can continue between placements as long as no more than 12 months have passed since the last placement ended. Shelter's guidance describes the same 12-month rule. Wandsworth Council's Housing Benefit guidance says registered foster carers can have an extra bedroom while a child is placed or for up to 52 weeks since the last placement.

These rules are detailed and depend on your circumstances, including whether you are already approved, how many people live with you and which benefit you claim. This guide cannot tell you how your own claim will be affected: speak to your local council's benefits team, Shelter, Citizens Advice or the benefits adviser your fostering service can put you in touch with before you rely on any figure. Action for Children's Scotland pack also advises carers on housing benefit to check with their local authority.

Moving home while fostering

Renters move more often than owners, and that is not a bar to fostering. What matters is planning. Tell your supervising social worker as early as possible if you are thinking about moving or if your landlord says they want the property back. The service will need to check your new home before a child moves in, and it may want to time the move around the children in your care.

When you look for a new rented home, be open with prospective landlords that you foster. In England, the government's guidance on the Renters' Rights Act says it is now unlawful for landlords to refuse to let to people because they have children or receive benefits. Even so, a short letter from your fostering service confirming that you are an approved foster carer can make the conversation easier.

Across the UK

England
GOV.UK: you do not need to own your home but usually need a spare bedroom. National minimum standard 10.6 expects each fostered child over three to have their own room unless sharing is formally agreed. From 1 May 2026 private tenancies are rolling periodic tenancies and section 21 'no fault' evictions have ended. Councils can give housing priority to people who need a larger home to foster.
Scotland
mygov.scot: you can foster whether you rent or own, and you need a spare bedroom, though some councils make exceptions depending on the child's age. Action for Children's Scotland pack says a private landlord's permission is needed and that home safety checks are part of the assessment.
Wales
Foster Wales: whether you rent or have a mortgage, what matters is feeling secure where you live; you need a spare room, but not a large home. Contact your local Foster Wales team about your landlord's agreement.
Northern Ireland
nidirect: you can apply whether you own or rent your home. Contact your Health and Social Care Trust, HSC NI Adoption and Foster Care or an independent agency to ask about your tenancy and room.

Common questions

Do I have to own my home to foster?

No. Official guidance in England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland all says you can foster in a rented home. Services look for a stable, safe home with space for a child.

Do I need my landlord's permission to foster?

Usually yes. Most fostering services ask for your landlord's written agreement, and your tenancy agreement may require it. Raise it early, before the assessment is far along.

Can I foster in a council or housing association home?

Yes. Tell your landlord and check your tenancy conditions. In England, councils can give housing priority to people who need a larger home to foster; ask your council what its allocation scheme says.

What if my landlord says no?

Ask what their concern is and share your fostering service's information for landlords; many objections are about insurance or damage and can be answered. If the answer is still no, talk to the service about your options, which may include waiting until you move.

Will fostering affect my Housing Benefit or Universal Credit?

It can. The rules allow foster carers an extra bedroom in some circumstances, including for a period between placements, but the detail depends on your claim. Check with your council's benefits team, Citizens Advice or Shelter rather than relying on a general guide.
