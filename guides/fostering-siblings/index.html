Fostering siblings: can you keep brothers and sisters together?

Brothers and sisters who come into care are often split up because there are not enough foster homes with room for them all. Here is what fostering a sibling group involves, what the law says in England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland, the space and support you would need, and how to tell if it could be right for you.

The short answer

Yes. Many fostering services are actively looking for carers who can take two, three or more brothers and sisters together. You go through the same assessment as any other foster carer; the difference is that your approval will say how many children you can care for at once, and you need the space, time and energy to meet several children's needs in the same household.

Across the UK, the law and official guidance start from the position that brothers and sisters in care should live together where that is right for them. In practice, services say the main reason siblings are separated is not that it is better for the children, but that there are too few carers with enough room. Hertfordshire County Council, for example, says on its fostering pages that more than half of the 1,000 children in its care are siblings, and that 56% of its sibling groups are split up because it does not have enough foster carers who can, or wish to, look after siblings.

This guide explains why keeping siblings together matters, what each nation's rules say, the practical questions you will be asked about bedrooms and money, and what to ask a fostering service before you apply.

Why keeping brothers and sisters together matters

When children are removed from home, their brothers and sisters are often the one familiar thing left. Hertfordshire's sibling campaign makes the point simply: siblings kept together can support and comfort each other at a time of uncertainty. Foster Wales Wrexham says its experience is that children kept with their siblings, especially where there is a strong emotional bond, are far more likely to settle and start making positive progress, and that a brother or sister can be a reassuring presence in a strange new home.

Foster Wales Cardiff says separated siblings can feel isolated, and that keeping them together, where it is safe, tends to bring better mental health outcomes and fewer moves between homes. Northern Ireland's regional Adoption and Foster Care service (part of Health and Social Care) says every effort should be made to maintain sibling relationships, and points to research linking placement together with higher rates of children returning to their birth families or moving to adoption or guardianship.

The Scottish Government's national practice guidance, Staying together and connected, goes further. It describes relationships between brothers and sisters as lifelong, and says siblings should be supported to keep those relationships even when they cannot live together.

  • Comfort and continuity: a brother or sister shares the child's history, memories and family identity.
  • Stability: services report that children placed with siblings often settle better and move less.
  • Lifelong relationships: brothers and sisters are usually the longest relationships a person has, long after care ends.

Why siblings are sometimes placed apart

Keeping siblings together is the starting point, not an absolute rule. The child's welfare comes first, and sometimes the professionals involved decide that children are better cared for separately, at least for a time. The reasons councils give on their own pages include a family group that is very large, a big age gap, very different needs, or a relationship between the children that is unsafe or harmful for one of them.

The reason that should not decide it, but often does, is a shortage of homes. Foster Wales Wrexham lists a shortage of carers with enough space as a barrier, and Buckinghamshire Council's fostering blog quotes one of its short-term carers saying siblings are sometimes split up simply because there are not the carers to look after them. Northern Ireland's Adoption and Foster Care service notes that sibling groups can range from two to nine children, and that services usually try to keep two or three together at once, because few carers have the space, time and ability to take a whole large group.

When siblings do live apart, the plan should still keep them connected: placements near each other, regular time together, and contact that continues as they grow up. As a foster carer you may be asked to help with this even if you are caring for only one child from a family, for example by taking a child to see a brother or sister who lives with another carer.

What the law says

Each UK nation sets its own rules for children in care. In England, section 22C of the Children Act 1989 says that, when deciding on the most appropriate placement for a looked after child, the council must, so far as is reasonably practicable in all the circumstances, make sure that if the child has a sibling it is also looking after, the placement enables them to live together. The same wording appears in section 81 of the Social Services and Well-being (Wales) Act 2014 for Wales. legislation.gov.uk notes that changes made by a 2025 Welsh Act to that section are not yet shown in the text, so check the current position with Foster Wales if it matters to you.

In Scotland, a specific duty to place siblings together was added to the Looked After Children (Scotland) Regulations 2009 from 26 July 2021 (regulation 5A). Where appropriate, the council must place brothers and sisters with the same kinship carer, foster carer or residential home, or, if it is better for the child's welfare, in homes near each other. The child's welfare is the paramount concern. Scotland's guidance also takes a wide view of who counts as a sibling: not only children who share a parent, but children who have lived together and have a sibling-like relationship, which can include a foster carer's own children.

In Northern Ireland, the regional Adoption and Foster Care service says every effort should be made to maintain sibling relationships, whether children live together or apart.

None of this is something you as a carer have to enforce. It shapes the decisions the child's social worker and the council make, and it is the reason services keep asking for carers who can take sibling groups.

Space and bedrooms

Space is usually the deciding practical question. Most services expect each foster child to have their own bedroom, but many allow brothers and sisters to share where it suits them. Hertfordshire County Council, for example, says sibling carers need a spare room for each sibling, while some same-sex siblings can share a room. Whether sharing is allowed depends on the children's ages, needs and history, and the decision is made child by child; a room that works for two young brothers might not work for two teenagers, or for children who have been harmed.

During your assessment the social worker will look at your home and agree how many children you can be approved for, and what ages. If you have three bedrooms free, you might be approved for up to three children; if you have one large room, you might be approved for two young siblings who can share. Our guide on whether you need a spare bedroom to foster explains the general rules in more detail.

Think beyond bedrooms too. A sibling group needs room at the dinner table, seats in the car, space for school bags and coats, and enough of you to go round at bath and bedtime.

Money and support for sibling carers

Fostering allowances are generally paid for each child you look after, so a carer looking after three brothers and sisters normally receives an allowance for each of them. Hertfordshire, for instance, describes paying carers a weekly fee for their skills plus a weekly allowance for each child. How fees work for sibling groups varies by service: some pay a skills fee per child, some per household, and some add a payment for taking a larger group. Lancashire County Council, for example, offers a one-off incentive when a sibling group of three or more children are matched and placed at the same time.

Training and support matter as much as money. Hertfordshire and Foster Wales Wrexham both say they give specialised training to carers who look after sibling groups, alongside the usual support of a supervising social worker, out-of-hours advice and other carers to talk to.

Because every council and agency sets its own pay and support, ask each one you speak to the questions below before you apply. Use our foster carer pay calculator to see the kind of allowance councils publish for each age group, and remember that a figure for one child does not simply multiply for a group if the service pays fees per household.

  • Is the allowance paid for each child, and is the fee paid per child or per household?
  • Is there any extra payment or equipment grant for taking a sibling group (beds, a bigger car seat set-up, a larger car)?
  • What training do you give carers of brothers and sisters?
  • What respite or short-break support is available if I am caring for several children?
  • How many sibling groups are waiting for a home with you right now, and what ages are they?

What fostering siblings is like day to day

Carers who look after brothers and sisters often describe it as one of the most rewarding kinds of fostering, and also one of the busiest. Buckinghamshire's foster carer Judith, writing on the council's fostering blog, says every set of brothers and sisters is individual, and that the hardest part is working out what each child is struggling with and meeting each child's needs rather than treating them as a unit.

Siblings who have lived through neglect or chaos together may have taken on roles that do not fit their age. An older child may have been parenting younger ones and find it hard to let an adult take over; younger children may compete for attention or copy each other's behaviour. Part of the carer's job is to let each child be a child again, while keeping the bond between them safe.

Practically, expect several school or nursery runs, more appointments, more contact arrangements with birth family, and more meetings. Many carers of sibling groups find it works best when one carer is at home full time, or when two carers can share the load; services will talk to you about what is realistic for your household.

Could fostering a sibling group be right for you?

Fostering siblings tends to suit people who have room in their home and in their week, who are comfortable with a busy household, and who can give each child individual attention in the middle of family life. Many carers of sibling groups have raised their own children, worked with children, or fostered single children first. Being new to fostering does not in itself rule you out: ask each service how it decides.

You can also say during your assessment that you would like to be considered for brothers and sisters, even if you start with one child. Approval can be reviewed as you gain experience, and your circumstances change.

If you are interested, contact a few fostering services in your area, say early that you would like to keep brothers and sisters together, and ask how many sibling groups they are looking for homes for. The answers will tell you a lot about the need where you live.

Across the UK

England
Section 22C of the Children Act 1989 requires councils, so far as is reasonably practicable, to place a looked after child with any sibling they are also looking after. Each council and independent agency sets its own approach to bedroom sharing and its own fees for sibling groups.
Scotland
Since 26 July 2021, regulation 5A of the Looked After Children (Scotland) Regulations 2009 requires councils, where appropriate, to place brothers and sisters with the same carer or in homes near each other. The Scottish Government's Staying together and connected guidance defines siblings broadly, including children who have lived together with a sibling-like bond.
Wales
Section 81 of the Social Services and Well-being (Wales) Act 2014 contains a duty, so far as is reasonably practicable, to place a looked after child with any sibling the council is also looking after; a 2025 Welsh Act makes changes to that section, so check the current position. Foster Wales teams, such as Cardiff and Wrexham, are recruiting carers specifically for brothers and sisters.
Northern Ireland
The regional Adoption and Foster Care service (Health and Social Care) says every effort should be made to keep sibling relationships, noting that sibling groups can range from two to nine children and that services usually try to keep two or three together at once.

Common questions

Can siblings share a bedroom in foster care?

Often, yes, but it depends on the children. Many services allow brothers and sisters to share where their ages, needs and history make it suitable, and some say same-sex siblings can share. The decision is made for each group by the children's social workers and your fostering service.

Do you get paid more for fostering siblings?

Allowances are usually paid for each child, so a carer looking after three siblings normally receives three allowances. Fees and any extra payments for sibling groups vary: some councils, such as Lancashire, offer a one-off incentive for taking a group of three or more. Ask each service how it pays for sibling groups.

How many siblings can one foster carer look after?

Your approval sets the number, based on your home, your experience and your circumstances. Northern Ireland's Adoption and Foster Care service says sibling groups can range from two to nine children, and services usually aim to keep two or three together at once.

Is it a legal requirement to keep siblings together in care?

In England and Wales the law requires councils to place siblings together so far as is reasonably practicable, and in Scotland where appropriate, always with the child's welfare first. It is a strong starting point, not an absolute rule: sometimes children are placed apart because it is better for them, and then their relationship should be kept up through contact.

Can I foster siblings as a first-time foster carer?

It depends on the service. Your assessment looks at your space, time, experience and support network, and some services prefer you to start with one or two children. Tell the service early that you are interested in brothers and sisters so they can assess you for it.
