Can you foster with a health condition?

Usually, yes. No health condition, physical or mental, automatically rules you out of fostering. Every applicant has a medical, and the fostering service looks at whether you can safely care for a child, now and over the years ahead. Here is how the medical works and what services take into account in each UK nation.

The short answer

Having a health condition, a disability or a history of mental ill health does not, on its own, stop you becoming a foster carer. None of the fostering regulations in England, Scotland, Wales or Northern Ireland lists a medical condition that bars you. What the law does require is that the fostering service collects information about your health, backed by a medical report, as part of deciding whether you are suitable.

Council fostering teams say this plainly. Bromley Council tells enquirers that a health condition or disability will not stop you becoming one of its foster carers, provided you have the fitness and stamina to look after a child or young person. Cornwall Council says many disabled people have fostered. Southampton City Council says a past mental illness does not usually prevent someone becoming a foster carer, although you need to be in a stable place with your mental health.

So the real question is not whether you have a condition, but how it affects your day-to-day life, how well it is managed, and what it would mean for a child living with you. This guide explains how the medical works, what fostering services weigh up, how mental health is approached, and where the rules sit in each nation. It is general information, not medical or legal advice: your GP and the fostering service you apply to are the people who can talk through your own circumstances.

What the law says about your health

In England, regulation 26 of the Fostering Services (England) Regulations 2011 sets out how a fostering service assesses a prospective foster parent, and Schedule 3 lists the information it must gather. Paragraph 2 of that schedule asks for details of the applicant's health, supported by a medical report, alongside personality, relationships and other details.

The other nations use very similar wording. In Scotland, Schedule 3 of the Looked After Children (Scotland) Regulations 2009 asks for the prospective foster carer's health, supported by a medical report. In Wales, Schedule 1 of the Fostering Panels (Establishment and Functions) (Wales) Regulations 2018 asks for details of health supported by a medical report. In Northern Ireland, Schedule 1 of the 1996 fostering regulations (SR 1996 No. 467) lists the applicant's age and health, supported by a medical report.

Two things stand out. First, the law asks for information; it does not set a pass mark or list conditions that disqualify you. Second, the health requirement in these schedules applies to the person applying to foster. Other adults in the household are named so that the service has particulars about them, and they go through criminal record checks, but the regulations do not ask for a medical report on each of them in the same way.

How the foster carer medical works

The medical is one of the standard checks in the assessment, along with criminal record checks, references and the home study by a social worker. Bolton Council explains that each applicant has a medical assessment completed by their GP, and that the council's medical adviser then decides whether any health factors would prevent fostering. In Northern Ireland, nidirect says that after training and your application you will be asked to have a medical with your GP.

Services commonly use a standard adult health report form, which your GP fills in from your records and an examination; the Fostering Network has recommended that this form be reviewed and that the approach to medicals be standardised nationally. The Fostering Network, the national fostering charity, explains that the assessment should look at you as a whole person, including any specific illness, disability, past or current treatment and prescribed medication. The medical information is then read by the fostering service's medical adviser, a doctor who advises the service and its fostering panel.

The Fostering Network also notes that the medical is one part of a wider suitability assessment, and that what matters most is whether a carer can safely care for children and meet their needs. Medicals are not a one-off. Once you are approved, your fostering service decides how often you need an updated medical; the Fostering Network gives every three years as an example of a local policy, rather than a national rule.

Practice varies between services on timing and on whether there is a fee from your GP, and the Fostering Network has highlighted cost and delay as known problems with fostering medicals. Ask the service you apply to how it handles this before you book anything.

  • Your GP completes the medical, usually on a standard form, from your records and an examination.
  • The service's medical adviser reads it and advises on any health issues.
  • Your assessing social worker talks with you about how your health fits with fostering.
  • The fostering panel sees the information when it makes its recommendation.
  • Medicals are updated after approval at intervals the service sets.

What fostering services take into account

Services are not looking for perfect health. They are looking at whether you can meet the needs of the children you would care for, safely and over time. Southampton City Council says that any health conditions and medication are taken into account in the medical process, and that carers need to be active and emotionally strong enough to cope with the demands of caring for a child.

In practice, the conversation with your social worker usually covers questions like these. How does the condition affect your energy, mobility and daily routine? Is it stable and well managed, and do you have a plan for flare-ups or hospital stays? Would a child need to step into a caring role for you, which services want to avoid? Who would look after a child at short notice if you were unwell? Does the condition point towards some types of fostering, such as short breaks or school-age children, rather than others?

The answers can shape the kind of fostering you are approved for rather than whether you foster at all. Someone who cannot lift a toddler may be a very good match for teenagers. Someone with a fluctuating condition may suit planned short breaks better than emergency placements in the middle of the night. Bromley Council, for example, says it works with applicants to find which type of fostering suits their needs.

Fostering with a disability

Disabled people foster successfully across the UK, and many services actively welcome applications. Southampton City Council says it welcomes applications from disabled people and considers each one individually, exploring the person's conditions and any limitations. Cornwall Council says the social worker will discuss the impact, if any, on your lifestyle and on a child. Bolton Council says the GP is asked to confirm that the applicant is physically able to look after children and is not putting their own health at risk.

Foster Wales Swansea has published the story of a couple who foster there: one partner is deaf and asked for lip-reading adjustments during the assessment, and the other was diagnosed with multiple sclerosis partway through the process. The team asked the questions it needed to about his health, and the couple carried on with their application. Their advice to others is to be honest and to ask for what you need.

If you have a disability, it is reasonable to ask the service at the first contact what adjustments it can make to training, home visits and paperwork, and how it has supported disabled carers before.

Mental health and fostering

Mental health is assessed in the same way as physical health: as part of the medical and the wider assessment, not as a box that rules you in or out. Southampton City Council says a past mental illness does not usually prevent someone from fostering, but that you need to be in a very stable place with your mental health, and that you should think about how fostering might affect it.

That second point matters. Many children in foster care have experienced trauma, loss or neglect, and caring for them can be emotionally demanding. Your social worker is likely to talk with you about what has helped you in the past, what your warning signs are, who supports you, and how you look after yourself under pressure. Experience of coming through difficult times can be a strength: Bromley Council, for example, says that past difficulties in an applicant's own life can be a real positive.

Being open is far better than playing things down. Your GP records will show past treatment and medication, and the assessment is built on honesty. A service is much more likely to be reassured by someone who understands their own mental health and has a clear support plan than by someone who leaves things out.

Smoking, vaping and other lifestyle factors

Smoking is the one health-related area where many services set firm rules, mainly to protect young children and children with breathing or heart conditions. Bolton Council does not place children under five, or children with respiratory, heart or other medical conditions, with a carer who smokes, and asks applicants to have been smoke-free for at least a year before it will consider them for under-fives. Cornwall Council asks for at least six months as a non-smoker, including e-cigarettes, to foster children under five. Southampton City Council will not place under-fives where anyone in the household smokes.

Foster Wales notes that councils have different approaches to smoking, that being honest is the most important thing, and that help is available to quit. These rules are set by each service, so check the policy of the service you are talking to. Our separate guide on smoking and vaping goes into this in more detail.

How to prepare and what to say

You do not need to have every answer before you enquire. It does help to think through a few things and to raise your health early, so that nobody is surprised later in the assessment.

  • Mention any significant health condition, disability or mental health history in your first conversation with the service.
  • Ask how the service handles the medical: who arranges it, roughly when it happens in the process, and whether there is any GP fee.
  • Think about your support network: who could help with school runs or care if you were unwell or in hospital.
  • Be ready to talk about how your condition is managed, including medication and any specialists you see.
  • Ask whether your health might point towards particular types of fostering, and keep an open mind about them.
  • If you are told your health is a concern, ask what exactly the concern is and whether more information from your specialist would help.

Across the UK

England
The Fostering Services (England) Regulations 2011, regulation 26 and Schedule 3, require the fostering service to obtain details of the applicant's health supported by a medical report. No health condition is listed as a bar. The medical is usually done by the applicant's GP and reviewed by the service's medical adviser.
Scotland
The Looked After Children (Scotland) Regulations 2009, Schedule 3, require the prospective foster carer's health, supported by a medical report, as part of the assessment. As elsewhere, no condition is named as disqualifying; the fostering service and its medical adviser weigh health as part of overall suitability.
Wales
The Fostering Panels (Establishment and Functions) (Wales) Regulations 2018, Schedule 1, list details of health supported by a medical report among the information gathered about prospective foster parents. Foster Wales says there is no upper age limit and that councils take different approaches to smoking.
Northern Ireland
The 1996 Northern Ireland fostering regulations (SR 1996 No. 467), Schedule 1, list the applicant's age and health supported by a medical report. nidirect says applicants have a medical with their GP after training and the application, alongside an Access NI check, a social services check and references, before a social worker's assessment goes to the fostering panel.

Common questions

Is there a health condition that automatically stops you fostering?

No. None of the fostering regulations in England, Scotland, Wales or Northern Ireland lists a health condition that disqualifies you. Each requires information about your health backed by a medical report, and the fostering service decides whether you can safely meet a child's needs.

Who does the foster carer medical?

Usually your own GP, often using a standard adult health form, from your records and an examination. The fostering service's medical adviser then reads the report and advises the service and its fostering panel on any health issues.

Can I foster if I have had depression or anxiety?

Often, yes. Southampton City Council, for example, says a past mental illness does not usually prevent someone from fostering, provided their mental health is stable. Expect to talk openly about what has helped you, your support network and how you manage stress.

Can I foster if I am disabled?

Yes, many disabled people foster. Services consider each application individually and look at how a disability affects day-to-day care of a child. Ask the service what adjustments it can make during training and the assessment.

Do I have to have a new medical once I am approved?

Yes, from time to time. Your fostering service decides how often. The Fostering Network gives every three years as an example of a local policy; there is no single national interval.

Can my health limit the type of fostering I do?

It can. A condition that affects lifting or night-time care might make teenagers or planned short breaks a better fit than babies or emergency placements. This is discussed with your social worker and reflected in your approval.
