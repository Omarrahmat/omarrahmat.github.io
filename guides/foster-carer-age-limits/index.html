Is there an age limit for foster carers? Minimum and maximum ages explained

You must be at least 18 to foster anywhere in the UK, most services ask for 21, and there is no upper age limit. Here is how age, health and experience are weighed, and what differs between England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland.

The short answer

Age is one of the first things people worry about when they start thinking about fostering, and it is usually less of an obstacle than they expect. Across the UK the legal starting point is 18, most fostering services ask for applicants to be at least 21, and there is no upper age limit anywhere in the UK. Official guidance in all four nations says so in one form or another.

GOV.UK's page on becoming a foster parent in England says you must be at least 18, while noting that most fostering services will want you to be 21 or over. The Fostering Network, the UK's leading fostering charity, says the same, adds that there is no legal lower age limit as such, and states that legally there is no upper age limit to foster. mygov.scot, the Scottish Government's information site, says you need to be 18 or over, that some agencies will not let you foster until 21, and that there is no upper age limit. Foster Wales says there is no upper age limit, and nidirect, the Northern Ireland government site, says there is no maximum age for becoming a foster or kinship foster carer.

So for most people the honest answer is: if you are 21 or over, your age on its own will not stop you. What fostering services look at instead is your health, your energy, your experience and whether your circumstances suit the children who need homes. The rest of this guide explains where the minimum ages come from, how services think about older applicants, and what to ask before you apply.

Minimum age: 18 in law, 21 in practice

The figure of 18 is the floor. Nobody under 18 can be approved as a foster carer, and the checks every applicant goes through assume an adult: in England, the Fostering Services (England) Regulations 2011 require the fostering service to gather an applicant's date of birth and an enhanced criminal record certificate for the applicant and every adult in the household aged 18 or over.

Above that floor, each fostering service sets its own minimum, and 21 is by far the most common. Many council fostering services state 21 plainly on their websites: Leicestershire County Council, Norfolk County Council and Wigan Council in England, and Argyll and Bute Council in Scotland, all say applicants need to be over 21. Some go higher. The London Borough of Camden, for example, says you must be over 25 to foster for it.

A few services are more flexible at the younger end. Lincolnshire County Council says it prefers carers to be at least 21 but will consider younger applicants who can show enough life experience. The Fostering Network makes a similar point: because there is no legal lower age limit beyond 18, some services will consider people in their late teens or early twenties, while others will not.

Why do services prefer 21 or older? They rarely set out a single reason, but the assessment itself explains a lot. Fostering services need to understand your relationships, your experience of caring for or working with children, your stability at home and how you have handled difficult times. Those things are easier to show with a few more years behind you. If you are under 21 and keen, it is worth asking services directly what they would expect from you, and in the meantime building experience with children and young people through work, volunteering or family.

  • 18: the minimum age anywhere in the UK.
  • 21: the minimum most councils and agencies set for themselves.
  • 25: the minimum at least one London borough sets.
  • Under 21: considered by some services where the applicant shows enough life experience, so ask before ruling yourself out.

No upper age limit: what that really means

There is no legal maximum age for fostering in England, Scotland, Wales or Northern Ireland, and most fostering services do not set one either. Leicestershire, Norfolk, Lincolnshire and Wigan councils all say there is no upper age limit to fostering. Foster Wales says people foster well into their 70s, and The Fostering Network says many excellent foster carers are in their 60s or 70s.

The national figures back this up. Ofsted's main findings on fostering in England for the year to 31 March 2025 report 56,345 approved mainstream foster carers. The largest group of them is in their 50s, at 37 per cent, and around one third are aged 60 or over. Among carers approved for the first time that year, the largest group was in their 40s. In other words, fostering in England is largely done by people in mid-life and beyond, and starting in your 40s or 50s is entirely normal.

No upper age limit does not mean age is never discussed. Services are thinking about the children they place and how long a placement might last. A service is likely to talk with an applicant in their late 60s about the kind of fostering that would suit them, whether that is short-term, short breaks or caring for older children, and about what would happen if their health changed. That is a conversation about matching, not a bar. Wigan Council puts the test simply: there is no upper age limit as long as you feel healthy enough to look after a child.

Health, fitness and the medical

Because age is not the test, health is where much of the conversation happens instead. Every applicant, whatever their age, has a medical as part of the assessment. In England the 2011 regulations require information about the applicant's health supported by a medical report. GOV.UK says you will need a medical statement, usually from your GP. nidirect says applicants in Northern Ireland will be asked for a medical with their GP, and mygov.scot says the Scottish assessment looks at your medical history.

A health condition is not an automatic barrier. The Fostering Network says nothing in the fostering regulations or standards makes a particular illness an automatic bar. Leicestershire County Council says health issues do not mean you cannot foster but it will need to understand your health, and Norfolk County Council looks at significant recent health issues case by case. What services want to know is whether you can meet a child's needs safely, now and for the length of time you would be caring for them.

Fostering can be physically demanding. Leicestershire notes that the children it looks after are often very active, and day-to-day care involves school runs, appointments, contact visits, broken nights with younger children and keeping up with teenagers. If you are older, think honestly about your energy and stamina, and about the age group you would be best placed to care for. Many older carers find that caring for older children, offering short breaks, or fostering on a short-term basis fits them well.

  • Expect a GP medical whatever your age.
  • Be open about any condition: services judge each case on how it affects caring for a child.
  • Think about stamina for the age group you want to foster, and ask which types of fostering suit you.

Retired, near retirement or still working

Retirement is often a good time to start fostering. Retired applicants usually have time, flexibility and a lifetime of experience, which are exactly what many children need. Nothing in the official guidance treats being retired as a problem, and councils that state no upper age limit welcome older applicants.

Money is worth thinking through before you apply. Foster carers receive an allowance to cover the cost of caring for a child, and some services pay a fee on top, but this is not a salary and it varies by service and by the child's needs. If you are drawing a pension or claiming benefits, the way fostering payments interact with them depends on your circumstances. Our guides to foster carer pay, qualifying care relief and fostering and benefits explain the general rules; for your own position, speak to HMRC, the Department for Work and Pensions or an independent adviser such as Citizens Advice.

If you have not yet reached State Pension age, there is a further point to know. HMRC's National Insurance manual explains that approved foster carers can claim National Insurance credits for the weeks they foster, which count towards their State Pension record. A claim has to be made, and the time limits matter, so check the details with HMRC before relying on them. We do not give individual tax or pension advice, and HMRC or the Future Pension Centre can confirm what applies to you.

Age and the children you can foster

Some rules about age are about the child rather than the carer. A few of these come up often in assessments and are worth knowing at the outset.

Smoking and young children is the most common. Norfolk County Council says children under five should not be placed in a household where anyone smokes, Argyll and Bute Council says the same, and Lincolnshire County Council says carers who smoke or vape can only foster children aged five and over. Bedroom rules can also depend on age: Lincolnshire says you will need a spare room to foster a child over three, and Camden says applicants without a spare room can only be approved for babies and toddlers up to two. mygov.scot notes that some Scottish councils may make bedroom exceptions depending on the child's age.

You will also be asked what age range you feel able to care for. GOV.UK says you can tell the service your preferences, including the child's age, when matching is discussed. Your own age often shapes this choice: younger carers sometimes have more energy for babies and toddlers, while many older carers bring patience and experience that suits teenagers. Services will talk this through with you during the assessment, and your approval will usually set out the age range you are approved for.

What to do next

If you are worried that you are too young or too old, the best step is to ask rather than rule yourself out. Every service sets its own minimum age and some are more flexible than their website suggests, while others are stricter. A short enquiry call or email will tell you where you stand.

When you contact a fostering service, it helps to have a few questions ready. Fostering services expect these questions and should answer them plainly.

  • What is your minimum age, and do you consider younger applicants with relevant experience?
  • Do you have an upper age limit, or any age-related policy on the types of fostering you offer?
  • What does the medical involve, and how do you approach existing health conditions?
  • Which types of fostering, such as short breaks, short-term or caring for teenagers, are most needed locally?
  • What support do you give older carers or carers with health needs, including respite?

Across the UK

England
GOV.UK says applicants must be at least 18 and that most fostering services require 21. There is no upper age limit in law. The Fostering Services (England) Regulations 2011 require the service to record your date of birth and your health, supported by a medical report. Individual councils and agencies set their own minimum, most commonly 21; Camden asks for 25.
Scotland
mygov.scot says you need to be 18 or over, that some agencies will not accept applicants until 21, and that there is no upper age limit. Scotland's national foster care campaign also says applicants must be over 18 with no upper limit. Some councils, such as Argyll and Bute, set 21 as their minimum.
Wales
Foster Wales, the national network of council fostering services, says there is no upper age limit and that people foster well into their 70s. It does not publish a single minimum age, so check with your local Foster Wales team; the UK-wide floor of 18 applies.
Northern Ireland
nidirect says there is no maximum age limit for becoming a foster or kinship foster carer, and that applicants will have a GP medical, an Access NI check and a social services check before an assessment goes to a fostering panel. Ask your Health and Social Care Trust about its minimum age.

Common questions

What is the minimum age to foster in the UK?

You must be at least 18 everywhere in the UK, but most fostering services ask for applicants to be at least 21, and a few set a higher minimum such as 25. Some services will consider younger applicants with relevant life experience, so ask the service you are interested in.

Is there an upper age limit for fostering?

No. There is no legal maximum age in England, Scotland, Wales or Northern Ireland, and most services do not set one. Ofsted reports that around one third of approved mainstream foster carers in England are aged 60 or over.

Can I foster if I am retired?

Yes. Being retired is not a barrier, and many people start fostering after they stop work. Think about your health and energy, and check how fostering payments fit with your pension or any benefits with HMRC, DWP or an independent adviser.

Will I need a medical if I am older?

Every applicant has a medical, usually through their GP, whatever their age. A health condition is not an automatic bar; the service considers how it affects your ability to care for a child.

Can I foster at 18 or 19?

It is legally possible, but most services require 21. A few, such as Lincolnshire County Council, say they will consider younger applicants who show enough life experience. Building experience with children in the meantime will help your application.
