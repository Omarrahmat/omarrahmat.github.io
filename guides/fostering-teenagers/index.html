Fostering teenagers: what it is really like and who can do it

Most children in care in England are aged 10 or over, and teenagers are the group services most often struggle to place. Here is what fostering a teenager involves, the skills that matter, the support on offer, and what happens when they turn 18 in each UK nation.

The short answer

Yes, you can choose to foster teenagers, and councils and charities say they need more people who will. Older children make up most of the children in care in England, and Medway Council says myths about adolescent fostering stop some young people getting the care they need. That is why several councils now publish pages aimed specifically at people who could foster 11 to 17 year olds.

You do not need to have raised a teenager yourself. What services look for is steadiness: people who can stay calm, keep listening, hold sensible boundaries and keep showing up when a young person tests them. You get training before and after approval, a supervising social worker, and an allowance for the young person's costs, plus a fee with many services.

This guide is general information. Payments, training and the support offered for teenagers are set by each fostering service, so the service you are thinking of joining can confirm what applies to you.

Why teenagers need foster carers

The Department for Education's statistics for England, published on 19 November 2026, counted 82,910 children looked after on 31 March 2026. Of those, 64% were aged 10 or older: 31,110 were aged 10 to 15 and 22,140 were 16 or over. Two thirds of children in care (66%) were living with foster carers. In other words, if you foster in England, the young people most likely to need a home are older children and teenagers.

Local figures tell the same story. Leicestershire County Council says that more than 62% of the young people in its care in July 2026 were preteens and teenagers, and Bristol City Council says half of the young people who come into its care are over 11. Central Bedfordshire Council describes a significant number of teenagers who need foster carers, and Leicestershire runs a page aimed only at people who could foster preteens and teenagers.

Some teenagers come into care for the first time in their teens. Others have been in care for years and need a new home because an earlier arrangement ended. Some arrive in the UK alone: the same statistics counted 6,000 unaccompanied asylum-seeking children in care in England in March 2026, and 91% of them were 16 or older. Barnardo's points out that for some young people, a foster home may be the first positive experience of family life they have had.

What teenagers in care may be dealing with

A teenager coming into foster care is going through the usual pressures of adolescence, such as exams, friendships, identity and growing independence, while also living away from their family. Many have experienced neglect or harm for longer than younger children have, simply because they were at home longer before help arrived. Councils describe young people who feel insecure or misunderstood, who may have missed out on ordinary childhood experiences, and who are aware of the unfair assumptions people make about teenagers in care.

That can show up as pushing boundaries, mistrust of adults, difficulty at school, low self-esteem or worry about their family. Some young people need help from other services, for example mental health support through CAMHS, and your supervising social worker and the young person's social worker will help arrange it. None of this means a placement will be chaotic. Many teenagers settle well and simply want a stable, ordinary home where adults are reliable.

The qualities and skills that matter

No fostering service expects perfection, and the qualities councils ask for are mostly about temperament rather than qualifications. Pulling together what Central Bedfordshire, Medway, Lancashire, Leicestershire and Barnardo's say, the people who do well with teenagers tend to share a few traits.

  • Patience and staying power: being the adult who is still there after a hard week, and not taking difficult behaviour personally.
  • Reliability and honesty: doing what you said you would, and being straight with the young person, including when the answer is no.
  • A non-judgemental, open approach: listening first, being curious rather than interrogating, and accepting that the young person has a history and a family you do not replace.
  • Clear, fair boundaries: a few rules that matter, applied consistently, while picking your battles rather than criticising everything.
  • Willingness to let them grow up: giving more freedom as trust builds, rather than keeping a teenager on a tight leash.
  • A sense of humour and the energy to keep going, which every council campaign mentions in one form or another.

What fostering a teenager looks like day to day

Much of the work is ordinary family life with extra intention behind it. Councils describe carers helping with school, college or university applications, encouraging hobbies, helping with a first part-time job, and making space for friends to come round for dinner. Family meals, a predictable routine and someone who notices how the day went do a great deal of the work.

Preparing for adulthood is a large part of the role. Lancashire and Central Bedfordshire both list practical skills such as cooking, managing money and building confidence. Carers in Action for Children's published account helped a young person budget with pocket money, look for work and re-engage with school. You will also help the young person keep in touch with their family where that is part of their plan, and you will work with their social worker, school and any other professionals involved.

Expect some testing early on. Medway Council's advice is to choose your battles, avoid constant nit-picking, show interest without firing questions, share something about your own day first, and model the behaviour you hope to see. Ask what your service's training covers for this age group, such as online safety and managing risk, and use your supervising social worker to talk things through.

Training, support and payments

Every approved foster carer gets training and a supervising social worker, and Leicestershire, like other services, points carers of teenagers to further training to build their knowledge. Lancashire County Council describes extensive training and a package of support for carers of older children, and Central Bedfordshire describes regular supervision plus a separate intensive support scheme for young people with more complex needs.

On money, you receive an allowance to cover the young person's day-to-day costs. Many services also pay a fee for your time and skills, and some pay more for specialist or intensive schemes. Allowances are often banded by age, so check what your service pays for the age group you want to foster. None of the council pages we read gave a single extra figure for teenagers, which is why it is worth asking each service directly. Our guide to foster carer pay and our council allowance pages show what services publish.

Ask any service you are considering how it supports carers of teenagers in practice: out-of-hours help, respite or short breaks, support groups with other carers of teenagers, access to therapeutic advice, and how quickly it can link a young person to mental health or education support.

When the young person turns 18

Turning 18 does not have to mean leaving. In England, Wales and Scotland a young person can stay on with their former foster carers up to age 21, and Northern Ireland has its own scheme, as set out below. The Fostering Network notes that young people outside care leave home at 24 on average, so these arrangements give care leavers something closer to what other young adults have.

The arrangement changes once the young person is an adult. They are no longer a fostered child, the paperwork and payments are different, and the young person may contribute to costs, for example through housing support or wages. The Fostering Network's State of the Nations research in 2024 found that three quarters of foster carers were worse off financially when they offered a post-18 arrangement, so ask your service early what it pays and how it works locally. Benefits and tax can change too; your service, HMRC or an independent adviser can confirm what applies to you.

Is fostering teenagers right for you?

Fostering teenagers can suit people whose own children are older or grown up, people who work with young people, and anyone who enjoys conversation, debate and seeing someone take real steps towards adult life. It may suit you less if you want to shape a child's routine from the start, or if your household could not cope with some testing behaviour while trust is built.

If you are unsure, consider respite or short-break fostering for teenagers first, which lets you look after young people for weekends or short periods while their main carers have a break. Talk to more than one fostering service, ask to speak to carers who foster teenagers, and ask how matching works so you are only asked to care for young people whose needs fit your household. Most services will be honest about the challenges, and that honesty is a good sign.

Across the UK

England
Staying Put has been a legal duty since 2014. Guidance from the Department for Education, HMRC and the Department for Work and Pensions sets out how a care leaver can remain with their former foster carers after 18, up to age 21, and each local authority publishes its own Staying Put policy with the payments it makes.
Scotland
Continuing Care, under Part 11 of the Children and Young People (Scotland) Act 2014, lets a young person who leaves care after their 16th birthday stay in their foster, kinship or residential home with the same level of support up to their 21st birthday. The Scottish Government's guidance was updated in July 2024. It means staying with the same carer, not moving to a new one.
Wales
When I am Ready, in place since 2016, lets a young person stay with their former foster carers after 18, up to age 21. The Welsh Government publishes a good practice guide for the professionals who arrange it, and each council has its own policy.
Northern Ireland
Going the Extra Mile (GEM), launched in 2010, supports young people to stay with their former foster carers after 18. The Fostering Network says it can continue to 25 for young people in education, employment or training, and that the law placing it on a statutory footing has not yet commenced.

Common questions

Do I need experience of bringing up teenagers to foster one?

No. Barnardo's and several councils say experience of parenting teenagers is not required. Services look for patience, reliability and a willingness to learn, and you will be trained before and after approval.

Can I choose to foster only teenagers?

Usually, yes. During your assessment you agree with your fostering service the age range and type of fostering you are approved for. Many services are actively looking for carers who will take young people aged 11 and over.

Is fostering teenagers paid more?

It depends on the service. Allowances are often set by age band, and some services pay a higher fee for specialist or intensive schemes for young people with complex needs. Ask each service for its published rates for the age group you want to foster.

What happens when a teenager I foster turns 18?

They can stay with you after 18 if you both want that: up to 21 under Staying Put in England, When I am Ready in Wales and Continuing Care in Scotland, and through the GEM scheme in Northern Ireland. The arrangement and payments change, so ask your service how it works locally.

Can I foster a teenager if I have younger children at home?

Often, yes, but your service will think carefully about matching so that the placement is safe and works for everyone in the household. Talk through your family's situation at the start of your assessment.
