Kinship fostering and special guardianship: caring for a child you already know

If a grandchild, niece, nephew or a family friend's child cannot live with their parents, you may be asked to step in. Here is how kinship fostering works, how it differs from special guardianship and informal family arrangements, what support you can expect, and how the rules change across England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland.

What kinship care means

Kinship care is when a child who cannot live with their parents is raised by someone they already know. The Department for Education's statutory guidance for councils in England, updated in October 2026, describes it as a child living full-time or most of the time with a relative, friend or connected person who provides most or all of their care. It can be formal or informal, and short or long term. You will also hear it called family and friends care or connected persons care, the terms Torfaen Council in Wales uses alongside kinship care.

You do not have to be a blood relative. Citizens Advice Scotland says you must have a pre-existing relationship with the child rather than a family tie, and the Department of Health in Northern Ireland gives examples of people with a prior connection, such as a childminder, teacher or youth worker. Grandparents, aunts, uncles, older brothers and sisters, step-parents and close family friends are the most common kinship carers.

Leeds City Council's guide to kinship care says children usually come to live with relatives because their parents cannot provide the care and support they need, and lists common reasons such as drug or alcohol misuse, neglect, domestic abuse or bereavement. Many arrangements are made within the family without social workers being involved at all. Others begin when children's services are already working with the family and need somewhere safe for the child to go, often at very short notice.

The different kinship arrangements

The word kinship covers several quite different legal set-ups. Which one you are in decides who holds parental responsibility, whether a social worker stays involved, and what financial help you can get. The England guidance lists six; the same broad shapes exist elsewhere in the UK under different names.

  • Informal family arrangement: a close relative looks after the child by agreement with the parents, with no court order and no council role. The parents keep parental responsibility.
  • Private fostering: someone who is not a close relative cares for a child under 16 for 28 days or more by private arrangement. In the law on private fostering, a close relative means a grandparent, brother, sister, uncle, aunt or step-parent.
  • Kinship foster care: the child is looked after by the council (under a care order or by agreement with the parents) and placed with you as an approved foster carer. This is the arrangement most people mean by kinship fostering.
  • Child arrangements order: a family court order saying the child is to live with you. It gives you parental responsibility, shared with the parents.
  • Special guardianship order: a family court order that gives you parental responsibility which you can use to the exclusion of the parents in most day-to-day and long-term decisions, usually until the child is 18.
  • Adoption by a relative or friend: ends the birth parents' parental responsibility permanently and makes you the child's legal parent.

How kinship fostering works

When a child is looked after by the council and the social worker thinks living with a relative or friend is the best plan, the council can place the child with you before you have been through the full fostering assessment. In England this is set out in regulation 24 of the Care Planning, Placement and Case Review (England) Regulations 2010. The council must first check that you, your home and every other adult in the household are suitable, and must start the full assessment straight away.

Temporary approval in England lasts up to 16 weeks. If the full assessment is likely to take longer, regulation 25 lets the council extend it once, for up to eight more weeks, after seeking the fostering panel's view and telling the child's independent reviewing officer, and the extension must be signed off by a nominated senior officer. If you have been assessed, not approved, and ask for a review of that decision, temporary approval can continue until the outcome is known. If it runs out without approval, the council has to find the child another home.

The full assessment covers much of the same ground as any foster carer's: your health, your history, your relationships, references, criminal record and social services checks, and your home. Torfaen Council in Wales calls it a connected person's assessment and says it usually takes around 12 to 16 weeks, with home visits, DBS checks and references. Child Law Advice, run by the charity Coram, explains that in England the assessing social worker's report then goes to a fostering panel, which recommends whether you should be approved.

The assessment is not about whether you are a perfect family. It is about whether you can keep this particular child safe and meet their needs, and what help you will need to do so.

Money and support for kinship foster carers

While a child is looked after and placed with you as an approved kinship foster carer, you are a local authority foster carer. The government's kinship care guidance for England says kinship foster carers should be paid on the same basis as other foster carers, and Child Law Advice says the fostering allowance is payable even during temporary approval. In Wales, Cardiff University's guide to children's social care law states that kinship foster carers are paid foster care allowances. Your council's own allowance scale will tell you the weekly amount by the child's age.

You will also have a supervising social worker, the child will have their own social worker, and there will be regular reviews of the child's care plan. Torfaen Council, for example, says its supervising workers visit at least monthly during a kinship carer's first year, and offers support groups and around 15 hours of learning a year. Newport Council in Wales offers a dedicated social worker from its family and friends team, and Leeds runs three kinship care teams that advise carers on benefits, legal matters, contact and education.

Being a kinship foster carer also means living with the rules of foster care: visits, paperwork, training, and decisions shared with the council and often the birth parents. For some families this structure is a help; for others, especially when the arrangement is going to last until the child grows up, it feels intrusive. That is one reason many kinship foster carers later consider special guardianship.

Benefits and tax are affected differently depending on the arrangement. Citizens Advice Scotland notes, for example, that kinship carers of looked-after children in Scotland cannot claim the child element of Universal Credit for that child, while carers of children who are not looked after may be able to. This guide cannot tell you what you are entitled to: a welfare rights adviser, Citizens Advice or your council's kinship team can check your own situation.

Special guardianship explained

A special guardianship order is made by a family court under section 14A of the Children Act 1989, which applies in England and Wales. A special guardian must be 18 or over and cannot be the child's parent. Hampshire County Council describes the order as giving the carer overriding parental responsibility, and says it lasts until the child turns 18 unless the court discharges it.

Under section 14C of the Act, a special guardian can exercise parental responsibility to the exclusion of anyone else who has it, apart from another special guardian. The birth parents still have parental responsibility and remain the child's legal parents, but in practice you make the decisions. A few things still need the written consent of everyone with parental responsibility or the court's permission, including changing the child's surname and taking the child out of the UK for three months or more. Shorter trips abroad, such as a family holiday, are allowed.

Some people can apply without first asking the court's permission. These include a local authority foster carer or a relative with whom the child has lived for at least one year immediately before the application, and anyone named in a child arrangements order as a person the child lives with. Others need the court's leave. Anyone applying must give the council written notice of their intention within the three months before applying, and Hampshire County Council explains that where the council is involved it must carry out an assessment.

Once an order is made, the child stops being looked after by the council (Leeds City Council notes that a child stays looked after unless a child arrangements order or special guardianship order is made). That means no fostering allowance, no supervising social worker and no foster care reviews. Instead, support comes from the council's special guardianship support services: Cardiff University's guide notes that special guardians may be entitled to financial support, and Hampshire, for example, runs a post-order support service for special guardians. What is on offer, and how it is assessed, varies by council, so ask for the support plan in writing before the order is made.

Hampshire County Council sums up the trade-off: special guardianship gives a child a permanent home and the stability that comes with it, and a private family life without ongoing involvement from children's services. It also says plainly that children in kinship care have often been through significant trauma and may not respond in the ways carers are used to, so knowing where to go for help matters.

Kinship fostering or special guardianship: which fits?

There is no right answer for every family, and the decision is usually made with the child's social worker and, in court cases, by a judge. These are the questions families most often weigh up.

  • How long will the child need you? Kinship fostering can be temporary while the family's plans are worked out; special guardianship is meant to last until 18.
  • Who should make decisions? As a foster carer you work within the council's care plan and delegated authority. As a special guardian you make most decisions yourself.
  • How much support do you need? Foster care brings an allowance, a supervising social worker and regular reviews. After special guardianship, support depends on the council's assessment and your support plan.
  • How does the child feel? Many children want to feel they live with family rather than in care; others benefit from the extra professional support while things settle.
  • What about the birth parents? Contact can continue under either arrangement. Special guardians make most decisions, but the parents keep parental responsibility and remain the child's legal parents.

What changed in 2026 in England

The Department for Education updated its kinship care guidance in October 2026 to reflect new duties on councils in the Children's Wellbeing and Schools Act 2026 and new national kinship standards. Every council in England must now consult on and publish a kinship local offer setting out the support available to kinship children and their carers, replacing the old family and friends care policy. If you are thinking about kinship care, your council's kinship local offer is the first document to read.

The guidance also says that from the autumn 2026 term schools can record all children living in kinship care through the school census, and that consultations on fostering standards, training standards for foster carers including kinship carers, and the Adoption and Special Guardianship Support Fund are expected to report later in 2026. The Law Commission is reviewing the legal framework for kinship care and is due to publish recommendations in autumn 2027, so the law described here may change.

First steps if you are asked to care for a child you know

Kinship care often starts with a phone call and very little time to think. These steps help you protect the child and yourself.

  • Ask the social worker which arrangement this is: is the child looked after by the council, and are you being placed with them as a temporarily approved foster carer?
  • If the child is looked after, ask about the fostering allowance from day one, who your supervising social worker will be, and the timetable for your full assessment.
  • If you are asked to take the child informally, ask why the council is not placing the child with you as a foster carer, and what support you would get under each option.
  • Before agreeing to a child arrangements order or special guardianship order, ask for the proposed support plan, including any financial support, in writing.
  • Get independent legal advice before any court hearing; Child Law Advice, run by Coram, offers free guidance on family and friends care.
  • Read your council's kinship local offer (England) or ask your council or Health and Social Care Trust for its kinship care information.

Across the UK

England
Kinship foster carers are approved under the Fostering Services (England) Regulations 2011, with temporary approval for up to 16 weeks (extendable once by up to eight weeks) under the Care Planning, Placement and Case Review (England) Regulations 2010. Special guardianship and child arrangements orders are made under the Children Act 1989. Since the Children's Wellbeing and Schools Act 2026, every council must publish a kinship local offer, and the October 2026 statutory guidance sets national kinship standards for councils.
Scotland
Scotland has its own system. Mygov.scot explains that when a council places a close relative's or friend's child with you, this is a looked-after kinship care arrangement, and the council checks you can care for the child, including a PVG disclosure, medical, references and a home check. A kinship care order gives you shared parental responsibilities and rights and means the child is no longer looked after, with possible council support until 16. The Scottish Government says kinship carers of looked-after children should get an allowance at least equal to the foster care rate in their council area, and not below the Scottish Recommended Allowance introduced in August 2023. Councils must also provide kinship care assistance under the Kinship Care Assistance (Scotland) Order 2016. A permanence order keeps the child looked after, with council support until 18. The Kinship Care Advice Service for Scotland (run with Children First) gives free advice.
Wales
Wales uses the Social Services and Well-being (Wales) Act 2014, which, as Cardiff University's guide explains, gives priority to placing a looked-after child with a relative, friend or other connected person. Under the Care Planning, Placement and Case Review (Wales) Regulations 2015 a child can be placed with a connected person for up to 16 weeks while they are assessed, and kinship foster carers are paid foster care allowances. Special guardianship orders are made under the Children Act 1989, as in England, with financial support governed by a Welsh code of practice. Councils such as Torfaen and Newport run dedicated family and friends teams.
Northern Ireland
In Northern Ireland, kinship care means a looked-after child placed by a Health and Social Care Trust with a relative, friend or other person with a prior connection to the child. The five Trusts are responsible for all looked-after children, and nidirect lists kinship foster carers alongside emergency, short-term, longer-term and short break carers. Assessment follows the same route as other foster carers, including Access NI and social services checks, a medical, references and a fostering panel. The Department of Health publishes minimum kinship care standards for professionals, and the law is the Children (Northern Ireland) Order 1995 and the Foster Placement (Children) Regulations (Northern Ireland) 1996. Orders available to kinship carers differ from Great Britain, so ask the Trust or a solicitor which applies.

Common questions

Do kinship foster carers get paid?

If the child is looked after by the council and placed with you as an approved or temporarily approved foster carer, you should receive a fostering allowance on the same basis as other foster carers in that council. If the child lives with you under an informal arrangement, a child arrangements order or a special guardianship order, any money depends on the council's assessment and local policy. Ask the council or Trust what applies to you.

Can a grandparent foster their grandchild?

Yes. Grandparents are among the most common kinship carers. If the council places the child with you, you can be temporarily approved as a foster carer straight away and then assessed in full. Being related does not mean the checks are skipped: the council still assesses your health, home, household and ability to meet the child's needs.

What is the difference between kinship fostering and special guardianship?

In kinship fostering the child is looked after by the council, which shares decisions and provides a supervising social worker and an allowance. A special guardianship order is a court order that gives you parental responsibility you can exercise over the parents' wishes on most matters, usually until the child is 18, and the child is no longer looked after. Support then comes through a special guardianship support plan.

How long does temporary approval last?

In England and Wales a connected person can be temporarily approved for up to 16 weeks while their full assessment is completed. In England the council can extend this once by up to eight weeks if the assessment is likely to take longer, with a nominated senior officer's approval.

Do I need a solicitor for a special guardianship order?

You do not have to have one, but independent legal advice is strongly recommended before agreeing to any court order, because it affects parental responsibility and the support you will get. This guide is general information, not legal advice.

Can I become a general foster carer after kinship fostering?

Some kinship carers go on to foster other children. Whether your approval covers other children depends on the terms of your approval, so ask your supervising social worker or a fostering service what assessment you would need.
