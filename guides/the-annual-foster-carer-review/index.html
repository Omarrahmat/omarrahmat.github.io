The annual foster carer review: what happens and how to prepare

Once you are approved as a foster carer, your fostering service has to review your approval regularly. Here is what the review is for, who is asked for their views, what gets discussed, when it goes to panel, what happens if there is a disagreement, and how the rules differ in England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland.

The short answer

Approval as a foster carer is not a one-off decision that lasts forever. Every fostering service in the UK, whether it is a council, a health and social care trust or an independent agency, must look again at each carer's approval at set intervals. This is usually called the foster carer review, and in England, Wales and Northern Ireland it has to happen at least once a year, which is why most people know it as the annual review.

The review asks two main questions. Are you and your household still suitable to foster? And are the terms of your approval, such as how many children you can care for and of what ages, still the right ones? Along the way it looks back at the past year: what went well, what was hard, what training you have done, and what support you need next.

Every approved carer has reviews, so being reviewed is a normal part of fostering, not a sign that anything is wrong, and the review is also the usual time to ask for a change to your terms. The exact process, forms and timings differ from one service to another, so your supervising social worker is the person to ask about how yours works.

How often a review must happen

In England, regulation 28 of the Fostering Services (England) Regulations 2011 says the first review must take place no more than a year after you are approved, and after that at intervals of no more than a year. The service can also hold a review at any other time it thinks one is needed.

Wales has an almost identical rule in regulation 9 of the Fostering Panels (Establishment and Functions) (Wales) Regulations 2018: first review within a year of approval, then at least once a year, and whenever the provider thinks it necessary. In Northern Ireland, regulation 4 of the Foster Placement (Children) Regulations (Northern Ireland) 1996 requires approval to be reviewed at intervals of not more than a year.

Scotland is different on paper. Regulation 25 of the Looked After Children (Scotland) Regulations 2009 requires the first review within 12 months of approval, and then later reviews within three years of the previous one, plus an extra review whenever the council considers one necessary or appropriate to safeguard a child placed with you. Every one of those formal reviews goes to the fostering panel. Your service may choose to review more often than the minimum, so ask how often it reviews carers.

The Fostering Network notes that an allegation, a complaint or a big change in your household, such as a serious health problem, a separation or a divorce, can also lead to a review earlier than planned.

Who is asked for their views

The law in each nation sets out a minimum list of people whose views must be sought and taken into account. Services often go further and ask more people, because a review is only as good as the information behind it.

In England and Wales the regulations name you, any child placed with you (taking account of their age and understanding) and, in England, any council that placed a child with you during the past year. In Wales the placing council's views are required where the provider is an independent (regulated) fostering service. In Scotland the regulation names the child, the foster carer and any other council that placed a child with you. In Northern Ireland it names you and any authority that placed a child with you in the previous year.

In practice, Hertfordshire County Council's published procedure gives a typical picture of who is asked:

  • You, and any other carer in your household, usually through a written feedback form completed before the review meeting.
  • Children and young people who have lived with you since the last review.
  • Your own children and other important members of the household.
  • The social worker of each child placed with you during the review period, whose written feedback is attached to the review report.
  • Your supervising social worker, who usually writes the review report itself.

What gets discussed

The review is a chance to take stock of the whole year, not only to tick boxes. Fostering services publish slightly different lists, but the same themes come up again and again in official guidance and council handbooks.

The Fostering Network says a review should reflect on what has gone well, what has been challenging and what support you need, and should consider whether your terms of approval still fit your household and the children you have been caring for. Hertfordshire's procedure adds that the review should look at the training you have completed and what you need next, go through a health and safety checklist, revisit your family's safer caring plan, and record any complaints or allegations during the year.

Things you can expect to talk about include:

  • The children who have lived with you, how placements went and what you learned.
  • Training you have done, and the training and development you would like in the coming year.
  • The support you have had from your service, and anything that would have helped more.
  • Changes in your household: someone moving in or out, a new job, a house move, health changes or a new pet.
  • Your safer caring plan and the safety of your home.
  • Your terms of approval: the number, ages and needs of children you are approved for, and whether you want to change them.
  • Any complaints, concerns or allegations during the year, and how they were resolved.

The review report, the meeting and the decision

Most services hold a review meeting, usually at your home, and write a report. In England, Wales and Northern Ireland the law requires a written report at the end of each review. In England and Wales it must say whether you and your household remain suitable and whether your terms of approval remain appropriate. In Scotland the written record must include the information gathered, what happened at any review meeting and the decisions made.

Hertfordshire's procedure shows how much planning sits behind a review. Feedback starts being collected around eight months after the last review, a draft report is shared with the carers before the meeting, the meeting must take place no more than 12 months after the previous one, and the carers then see the completed report and can add comments before it is signed off. The final decision is made by a senior manager known as the agency decision maker, and the carer receives a letter and, where relevant, an updated foster carer agreement.

The Fostering Network says the written report should be sent to you, and that you can add your own comments to it. If anything in the report seems inaccurate, say so in writing at this stage.

When the review goes to fostering panel

In England and Wales, the report from your first review must go to the fostering panel, the same group that recommended your approval. After that the service may send later reviews to panel but does not have to. Many services do so in particular situations: Hertfordshire, for example, sends a review to panel if one has not gone for three years, after serious concerns or a major household change such as a couple separating, when carers want to widen their approval, and when termination is proposed and the carers disagree.

In Scotland, every formal review under regulation 25 must be referred to the fostering panel for a recommendation, and The Fostering Network describes this as reviews going back to panel at least every three years. Northern Ireland's regulation 4 does not require a panel referral, so ask your trust or agency how it handles reviews.

In England and Wales, going to panel after your first review happens to every carer. Ask your supervising social worker how your service's panel works and whether you will be invited.

If the service proposes to change or end your approval

Occasionally a review ends with the service proposing to change your terms of approval without your agreement, or to end your approval. The rules here are where the nations differ most, and the letter you receive should set out your options and deadlines.

In England, the service must give you written notice of what it proposes, with reasons and a copy of any panel recommendation. This is called a qualifying determination. You then have 28 days to either send written representations to the service, in which case the case goes back to panel, or apply to the Independent Review Mechanism (IRM) for an independent panel to look at it. GOV.UK says the IRM is free, aims to reach its recommendation within four months, and makes a recommendation only: the service still makes the final decision. You cannot use both routes.

In Wales, regulation 9 sets out the same pattern: written notice with reasons, then 28 days to make representations to the provider or apply to the Welsh Ministers for an independent review panel. IRM Cymru, run by Children in Wales, provides that independent review process; it says it is not an appeal and that the agency decides. If your letter and IRM Cymru's guidance give different deadlines, act within the shortest one and ask IRM Cymru to confirm.

In Scotland and Northern Ireland the review regulations themselves do not set out a 28-day representations process. Your council, trust or agency should explain its own procedure in writing. If you are in this position, ask for the reasons in writing, keep copies of everything, and consider contacting a fostering charity's advice service. This guide cannot advise on an individual case.

Ending your own approval

You can also decide to stop fostering at any time. In England and Wales, if you give written notice that you no longer want to foster, your approval ends 28 days after the service receives it. Hertfordshire's handbook adds that once given, this ends approval even if you later change your mind, and you would need a fresh assessment to foster again.

In Scotland, the council must end your approval once you tell it you no longer wish to foster. In Northern Ireland, the authority must end approval from a date it gives in a notice to you. If you are thinking about a break or a change rather than stopping, talk to your supervising social worker before giving notice, so you know what your service can offer.

How to prepare for your review

A little preparation makes the review more useful to you. Your service will tell you its own paperwork, but these steps help almost everywhere.

  • Keep notes during the year: training courses, support groups, achievements, difficulties and how you handled them.
  • Fill in your own feedback form honestly, including what support you need. The review is your chance to ask for it.
  • Talk with your own children and other household members beforehand so they know their views count.
  • Think about your terms of approval. If you want to care for older children, siblings or a different type of fostering, say so.
  • Check practical items your service tracks, such as safety checks and whether household background checks are due. Hertfordshire, for example, renews DBS checks for carers and adults in the household every three years.
  • Read the draft report carefully and add written comments if anything is wrong or missing.

Across the UK

England
Fostering Services (England) Regulations 2011, regulation 28: first review within a year of approval, then at least yearly. The first review must go to fostering panel. A proposal to end or change approval is a qualifying determination, with 28 days to make representations or apply to the Independent Review Mechanism. A carer's own written notice ends approval after 28 days.
Scotland
Looked After Children (Scotland) Regulations 2009, regulation 25: first review within 12 months of approval, then within three years of the previous review, plus extra reviews where needed to safeguard a child. Every formal review is referred to the fostering panel, and the views of the child, the carer and any placing council must be taken into account.
Wales
Fostering Panels (Establishment and Functions) (Wales) Regulations 2018, regulation 9: first review within a year, then at least yearly; first review to panel. A proposal to end or change approval gives 28 days to make representations or seek an independent review panel through the Welsh Ministers; IRM Cymru, run by Children in Wales, provides the independent review process.
Northern Ireland
Foster Placement (Children) Regulations (Northern Ireland) 1996, regulation 4: approval reviewed at intervals of not more than a year, seeking the views of the foster carer and any authority that placed a child in the past year. A report is prepared and the carer is given notice of the decision; the regulation does not require a panel referral.

Common questions

Is the annual foster carer review a test I can fail?

It is not a test. It is a required check that you and your household remain suitable and that your terms of approval still fit. It is also a chance to ask for more training, more support or a change to your terms.

Who attends the annual review meeting?

Usually you, any other carer in the household and your supervising social worker. Some services, such as Hertfordshire, involve an independent reviewing officer in certain circumstances. Views from children placed with you, your own children and the children's social workers are usually gathered in writing beforehand. Your service will tell you who it invites.

Does every annual review go to fostering panel?

In England and Wales only the first review must go to panel; later ones may, often after a major change or concern or when terms are being widened. In Scotland every formal review is referred to panel. Northern Ireland's regulation does not require it.

Can I change my terms of approval at my review?

Yes. The review is the usual time to ask, for example to care for older children, siblings or more than one child. The service will consider whether the change suits your household, and a wider approval may go to panel.

What can I do if I disagree with the outcome?

Add your comments to the report and raise it with your supervising social worker. If the service formally proposes to end or change your approval, in England and Wales you have 28 days to make representations or seek an independent review. Follow the deadlines in your letter, and for help with an individual case contact a fostering charity's advice service.
